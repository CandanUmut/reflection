## Q37 — Can I be "spiritual but not religious"?

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via the site's verified Tanzil data (`src/data/quran.json`) — 103:1–3, 2:186.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 50 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:50 — the hadith of Jibril, Abu Hurayra's version. (Muslim 8, ʿUmar's version, could not be opened in full; see Gaps.)
- Sahih al-Bukhari 46 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:46 — the man from Najd.
- "Murji'ah", *Wikipedia*, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murji%27ah.
- "Faith, Islam, and the Community", ch. 3 of an explanatory edition of *al-Fiqh al-Akbar*, islam.wiki, https://islam.wiki/books/fiqh-al-akbar/3 (no translator or edition named).
- Faraz Rabbani, "How Do I Revive a Heart That Feels Spiritually Numb?", SeekersGuidance (3 August 2026), https://seekersguidance.org/answers/general-counsel/how-do-i-revive-a-heart-that-feels-spiritually-numb/ — paraphrasing al-Qushayri.
- Pew Research Center, "More Americans now say they're spiritual but not religious" (6 September 2017), https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2017/09/06/more-americans-now-say-theyre-spiritual-but-not-religious/.
- Review of Paul Heelas & Linda Woodhead, *The Spiritual Revolution* (2005), *Catholic Books Review* (2006), https://catholicbooksreview.org/2006/heelas.htm.

### The question, at its strongest
"I believe in God. I pray sometimes, in my own words. But the mosque is where I was judged, the rules seem endless, and the people most loud about religion are often not the kindest. Faith is a matter of the heart. Why can't I keep the heart and leave the institution? Isn't sincere belief worth more than going through the motions?"

### Why people ask it
Some were hurt in religious communities — shamed, controlled, or met with hypocrisy. Some find rules rigid and the spirit missing. Some live in a culture where personal spirituality is the norm: in the United States, 27% of adults described themselves as spiritual but not religious in 2017, up 8 points in five years (Pew).

### Short answer
Islam agrees that the heart comes first: faith is, at its root, what the heart affirms. The Quran often pairs faith with deeds, as in: "Indeed, mankind is in loss, Except for those who have believed and done righteous deeds and advised each other to truth and advised each other to patience" (103:2–3). The hadith of Jibril describes religion in three layers — outward practice (*islām*), inward belief (*īmān*) and excellence (*iḥsān*, "To worship Allah as if you see Him") — and calls all three together "their religion". The Sunni schools disagreed on whether deeds are *part* of faith, but none of them held that deeds do not matter (an early group, the Murjiʾa, came closest; see below). The minimum Islam asks is small and clear (Bukhari 46). If a community hurt you, that is a wrong done to you, not a verdict on God; you can rebuild practice slowly, starting small, and choose where and with whom.

### The full answer

#### heart — If religious people are where you were hurt
**What was done to you was wrong, and God is not the one who did it.** The Quran says God is close to the person who calls on Him, without any middleman: "And when My servants ask you, [O Muhammad], concerning Me - indeed I am near. I respond to the invocation of the supplicant when he calls upon Me" (2:186). You can begin there: alone, in your own words.

**You do not have to go back to the same place.** Practice is owed to God, not to a particular mosque or group. Many people rebuild their practice at home first, then find a smaller, kinder community, a teacher they trust, or one friend to pray with. If what happened to you was abuse, talk to someone outside that community; Find A Helpline (findahelpline.com) lists free, confidential support lines in many countries.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Faith and deeds together (103).** "By time, Indeed, mankind is in loss, Except for those who have believed and done righteous deeds and advised each other to truth and advised each other to patience" (103:1–3), a short Meccan sura. It names four things that save from loss: belief, righteous deeds, and two things done *with others* — urging one another to truth and to patience. The sura's picture of salvation is not private.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**The hadith of Jibril.** A stranger came to the Prophet (pbuh) and asked about faith, Islam and excellence. In Abu Hurayra's (ra) report: "Faith is to believe in Allah, His angels, (the) meeting with Him, His Apostles, and to believe in Resurrection"; Islam is "To worship Allah Alone and none else, to offer prayers perfectly", to pay zakat and to fast Ramadan; and *iḥsān* is "To worship Allah as if you see Him", and if you cannot, to know that He sees you. Afterwards the Prophet (pbuh) said: "That was Gabriel who came to teach the people their religion" ({hadith:bukhari:50}). The point for this question: "religion" here is all three layers at once — the outward, the inward, and the spirit that joins them.

**The minimum, from a man who asked for it.** A man from Najd asked the Prophet (pbuh) what Islam required. He was told: "You have to offer prayers perfectly five times in a day and night (24 hours)", "You have to observe fasts during the month of Ramadan", and "You have to pay the Zakat (obligatory charity)". After each duty he asked whether there was more; each time the answer was no, unless he wished to do more voluntarily — for prayer, "No, but if you want to offer the Nawafil prayers (you can)"; for zakat, "No, unless you want to give alms of your own". He vowed to do exactly that, no more and no less, and the Prophet (pbuh) said: "If what he said is true, then he will be successful (i.e. he will be granted Paradise)" ({hadith:bukhari:46}, narrated by Talha ibn ʿUbaydullah (ra)). Islam's required core is small.

#### scholars — Are deeds part of faith? The classical debate
**Why it mattered.** In the first Islamic century, the Kharijites held that a person who commits a grave sin leaves Islam (*Wikipedia*, "Murji'ah"). Others, the Murjiʾa, "prioritized the importance of one's professed faith over the acts, deeds, or rituals they performed", holding that deeds do not affect faith (*Wikipedia*). The later schools took positions between these.

**The Hanafi–Maturidi view.** In the creed attributed to Abu Hanifa (*al-Fiqh al-Akbar*), faith is "affirmation by the heart (tasdiq bil-qalb) and attestation by the tongue (iqrar bil-lisan)"; "actions are not part of the definition of iman, though they are obligatory requirements and indicators of its strength"; and "iman does not increase or decrease" (islam.wiki's explanatory chapter). Abu Hanifa "was often associated with the Murji'ah" (*Wikipedia*), but this view still treats deeds as obligatory.

**The other view.** Other Sunni scholars, especially the hadith scholars, held that faith includes deeds and increases and decreases with them. (No source setting out this position in their own words was read for this run; see Gaps.)

**What both sides agree on.** No Sunni school taught that prayer and fasting are optional for someone who believes. Even the Hanafi side, which keeps deeds out of the definition, holds (in islam.wiki's explanation of *al-Fiqh al-Akbar*, ch. 3) that "actions are not part of the definition of iman, though they are obligatory requirements"; the same chapter gives the other side — Shafiʿi and Hanbali scholars and the hadith scholars — as holding that faith "increases through acts of obedience and decreases through sin". The disagreement is about definitions — whether a believer who neglects deeds is a sinful believer or someone whose faith is damaged — not about whether deeds matter.

**Al-Qushayri on inner and outer religion.** Al-Qushayri, the author of the classic Sufi manual *al-Risāla*, is cited by Rabbani for the distinction between states of the heart: fear and hope concern what is to come, while contraction (*qabḍ*) and expansion (*basṭ*) concern the present moment. Rabbani's summary: these states are "neither achievements nor failures. They are visitations, and they alternate." In this tradition, inner life is lived *through* steady practice, not instead of it: "worship carried out without sweetness is frequently weightier than worship carried by feeling" (Rabbani's words).

#### reasoning — Why practice sustains faith
**1. Faith that is never acted on fades.** Trust between people is kept alive by acts — a call, a visit, a promise kept. The Quran's pairing of "believed and done righteous deeds" (103:3) treats faith the same way: belief is shown and strengthened in what one does. This is an argument from ordinary experience, not a proof.

**2. Practice keeps you honest.** Private spirituality is easily shaped to fit our moods. Fixed practice — prayer at set times, a month of fasting, giving a share of wealth — asks things of us when we do not feel like it. That is where character is formed.

**3. Religion includes other people.** 103:3 ends with "advised each other to truth and advised each other to patience". No one can do that alone. A community, at its best, is where someone notices you are struggling.

*Limit:* the argument shows why practice and community help faith; it does not show that a particular community is healthy. A community can also damage faith. The answer to that is to choose carefully, not to give up practice.

#### science — What research on "spiritual but not religious" shows
**In the United States.** Pew's 2017 survey found 27% of adults spiritual but not religious, 48% both religious and spiritual, 6% religious but not spiritual, and 18% neither. The share calling themselves religious fell 11 points since 2012, to 54%; the share calling themselves spiritual held at 75%. Most of the growth came from people who had previously called themselves both (Pew). [Established, for the US] The sample size was not stated on the page read.

**In Britain.** Paul Heelas and Linda Woodhead's *The Spiritual Revolution* (2005) studied the town of Kendal. They contrasted "life-as" religion (living by others' expectations) with "subjective-life" spirituality (living by one's inner path), and the "congregational domain" with the "holistic milieu". The holistic milieu grew by about "300%" in the 1990s, yet in 2001 the congregational side still had five times as many participants. The authors predicted that "in 40 or so years time the congregational domain and holistic milieu of Britain will have become much the same size"; the reviewer called this "optimistic, at best" (*Catholic Books Review*, 2006). [Moderate; one town] The current page's summary that individualised religion "lacks community" was not found in the review read.

**Wellbeing.** No study comparing the wellbeing of religious and "spiritual but not religious" people was read for this run (see Gaps).

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Sincere belief is worth more than empty ritual."** *Reply:* Islam agrees that ritual without sincerity is hollow — the Prophet (pbuh) said a fast that does not stop false speech is not needed by God ({hadith:bukhari:1903}). But the answer to hollow practice is sincere practice, not no practice. *Limit:* someone may still feel that their sincerity is better kept outside structures; the tradition's reply is that this is a stage, not a home.

**"Religious communities are full of hypocrites."** *Reply:* some are; the Quran itself condemns hypocrisy at length. Other people's failures do not cancel your own relationship with God. *Limit:* if the hurt is deep, the practical way back may take years, and that is acceptable.

**"Isn't this just about control?"** *Reply:* the required core is small (Bukhari 46); much of what feels like control is culture or one group's rules, not the religion's minimum. *Limit:* some rules are clear and binding, and a person who rejects them is disagreeing with Islam, not only with a mosque.

#### views — The range of views
- **Mainstream Sunni teaching** — faith and practice belong together; the required core is the five prayers, Ramadan, zakat (and Hajj for those able).
- **Hanafi–Maturidi creed** — deeds are not part of faith's definition but are obligatory.
- **Hadith scholars and others** — deeds are part of faith.
- **Sufi tradition (e.g. al-Qushayri)** — inner states matter, but are lived through steady practice.
- **"Spiritual but not religious" in the West** — inner spirituality without institutional practice (Pew; Heelas & Woodhead).

### Questions readers ask next
**Why can't faith in the heart be enough?** For the Hanafi–Maturidi school, faith *is* in the heart and on the tongue — but deeds are still obligatory. For others, deeds are part of faith. Either way, the Quran ties salvation to belief *and* deeds (103:3). Practice is how faith is kept alive and tested.

**What is the minimum Islam asks of me, and what does "start small" mean?** The core, as in Bukhari 46: the five daily prayers, fasting Ramadan, zakat (Hajj, for those able, is in other reports not read for this run). "Start small" can mean: one prayer on time each day, then two; learning the meaning of al-Fātiḥa; one voluntary act a week. "the most beloved deed to Allah is the most regular and constant even if it were little" ({hadith:bukhari:6464}, narrated by ʿĀʾisha (ra)).

**What if mosques and religious communities are where I was hurt?** Start privately with God, who says "I am near" (2:186). Find one safe person or small group later. If it was abuse, seek outside help.

**What is the hadith of Jibril, and what does it say about *islām*, *īmān* and *iḥsān*?** A report in which Jibril, in human form, asked the Prophet (pbuh) about religion. *Islām* is outward practice; *īmān* is belief; *iḥsān* is "To worship Allah as if you see Him" ({hadith:bukhari:50}).

**Who was al-Qushayrī, and what did he say about inner and outer religion?** A Sufi scholar, author of *al-Risāla*. Rabbani reports his distinction between fear and hope (about the future) and contraction and expansion (states of the present), which come and go; the response to them is steady practice.

**How do Muslims see Sufism?** As the tradition of inner purification within Islam, not outside it; al-Qushayri's *Risāla* is a classic of that tradition. Its relation to the law was debated by some, but its main authors insisted on practice. (No survey of Muslim views of Sufism was read for this run.)

### Honest limits
The position that deeds are part of faith was not read in its holders' own words. Muslim 8, the KONDA data cited on the current page, and Heelas & Woodhead's book itself were not read. No study of wellbeing among "spiritual but not religious" people was read.

## Q35 — Why fast? Why so many rules?

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via `src/data/quran.json` — 2:183, 2:187, 35:15; via legacy.quran.com — 2:184–185.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English), on 2:184, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-184-sora-2.html.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 1903 and 1904 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:1903, https://sunnah.com/bukhari:1904.
- The hadith qudsi of Abu Dharr (Nawawi's Forty, no. 24; Muslim), https://sunnah.com/nawawi40:24.
- "The Necessities — The Five Universal Objectives of the Sharia", ch. 2 of an explanatory edition of al-Shatibi's *al-Muwāfaqāt*, islam.wiki, https://islam.wiki/books/al-muwafaqat-shatibi/2.
- Ahmad Kutty, in "Making up for fasting days missed because of pregnancy", IslamOnline Fiqh (6 January 2023), https://fiqh.islamonline.net/en/making-up-for-fasting-days-missed-because-of-pregnancy/.
- Fernando, Zibellini, Harris, Seimon & Sainsbury, "Effect of Ramadan Fasting on Weight and Body Composition in Healthy Non-Athlete Adults: A Systematic Review and Meta-Analysis", *Nutrients* 11(2):478 (2019), https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6412279.
- 2 Minute Medicine summary (Wang & Trifoi, 5 March 2024) of an umbrella review in *Nutrition Reviews*, doi:10.1093/nutrit/nuae001, https://www.2minutemedicine.com/ramadan-fasting-was-associated-with-beneficial-changes-in-metabolic-profile/.
- Jahrami, Ammar, Glenn, Saif, Chtourou & Trabelsi, umbrella review and meta-analysis of meta-analyses of Ramadan fasting and the metabolic syndrome components, *Nutrition Reviews* (2024), doi:10.1093/nutrit/nuae001 — abstract, via the Mainz university bibliography, https://bibliographie.ub.uni-mainz.de/catalog/publication-173562.

### The question, at its strongest
"Why would the Creator of galaxies care whether I eat at 2 p.m.? If God needs nothing, my fasting does nothing for Him. If it is for my health, a doctor could prescribe something better. And fasting is only the start: there are rules for washing, eating, praying, dressing, inheriting. Some have obvious purposes; many seem arbitrary. Following rules I can't understand looks like blind obedience."

### Why people ask it
Ramadan is the most widely kept practice in the Muslim world, and also where many young Muslims first ask "why?". The question has two halves: why would God care, and why so many rules.

### Short answer
God does not need the fast: "O mankind, you are those in need of Allah, while Allah is the Free of need, the Praiseworthy" (35:15). The Prophet (pbuh) said that if a person does not give up false speech and evil actions, "Allah is not in need of his leaving his food and drink" ({hadith:bukhari:1903}). The Quran gives the fast's purpose: "that you may become righteous" (2:183) — *taqwā*, a constant awareness of God. Fasting is from dawn to sunset, with no food, drink or sexual relations (2:187); the ill and travellers make up the days later, and those who can fast only with great hardship feed a poor person instead (2:184–185). As for rules, al-Shatibi held that the Sharia as a whole serves five purposes — religion, life, intellect, family and wealth. That acts of worship keep their fixed form even where their full reason is not stated is a common framing, but no named source for it was read for this run. Health effects of Ramadan fasting are real but modest and temporary, and they are not its reason.

### The full answer

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The command and its purpose (2:183).** "O you who have believed, decreed upon you is fasting as it was decreed upon those before you that you may become righteous -" (2:183), Medinan. The fast is not new; it was "decreed upon those before you". Its stated purpose is *laʿallakum tattaqūn*, "that you may become righteous": *taqwā*, from a root meaning to guard oneself — living aware of God, and guarding against what displeases Him.

**Who fasts, and who is excused (2:184–185).** "[Fasting for] a limited number of days. So whoever among you is ill or on a journey [during them] - then an equal number of days [are to be made up]. And upon those who are able [to fast, but with hardship] - a ransom [as substitute] of feeding a poor person [each day]" (2:184). Then: "The month of Ramadhan [is that] in which was revealed the Qur'an… So whoever sights [the new moon of] the month, let him fast it; and whoever is ill or on a journey - then an equal number of other days. Allah intends for you ease and does not intend for you hardship" (2:185).

**What the fast is (2:187).** "And eat and drink until the white thread of dawn becomes distinct to you from the black thread [of night]. Then complete the fast until the sunset" (2:187). The same verse permits sexual relations during the nights of Ramadan, so the fast is from food, drink and sexual relations from dawn to sunset.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**God does not need it.** "Whoever does not give up forged speech and evil actions, Allah is not in need of his leaving his food and drink" ({hadith:bukhari:1903}, Abu Hurayra). Hunger alone is not the point.

**"Fasting is for Me."** In a hadith qudsi (a saying the Prophet (pbuh) attributes to God, in his own words): "All the deeds of Adam's sons (people) are for them, except fasting which is for Me". The same report calls fasting "a shield or protection from the fire and from committing sins", and tells the fasting person, if insulted, to say: "I am fasting" ({hadith:bukhari:1904}, Abu Hurayra).

**God gains nothing from any worship.** In another hadith qudsi: "you will not attain harming Me so as to harm Me, and you will not attain benefiting Me so as to benefit Me"; if every heart were as pious as the most pious, "this would not increase My Kingdom an iota"; "it is but your deeds that I account for you, and then recompense you for" (in the sunnah.com translation of al-Nawawi's Forty, no. 24, which cites Muslim; this is {hadith:muslim:2577}, where sunnah.com's Muslim translation reads "that would not increase My dominion in anything" and "it is but your deeds that I record for you").

#### fiqh — Exemptions in the four schools
**The ill and travellers.** Make up the days later (2:184–185). Ibn Kathir: "the same number ( should be made up ) from other days."

**The very old and chronically ill.** The early permission to feed a poor person instead of fasting was, according to Muʿadh and Salama ibn al-Akwaʿ, later replaced by the command of 2:185 ("this abrogated the previous Ayah", Ibn Kathir). But Ibn ʿAbbas held that it "was not abrogated, it is for the old man and the old woman" who can fast only with difficulty (Ibn Kathir). So those who can never fast pay *fidya* — feeding a poor person for each day — and do not make up (Kutty, IslamOnline).

**Pregnant and breastfeeding women.** Ibn Kathir: "The same ruling applies for the pregnant and breast-feeding women if they fear for themselves or their children or fetuses." The schools differ on what they owe afterwards (Kutty, IslamOnline):
- **Hanafi:** make up the days only, no *fidya*.
- **Shafiʿi and Hanbali:** if they did not fast out of fear for the child only, make up *and* pay *fidya* for each day; if out of fear for themselves (or both), make up only.
- **Maliki:** not covered in the source read (see Gaps).
- A third view, reported from Ibn ʿAbbas and Ibn ʿUmar, treats them like the elderly: *fidya* only.

#### scholars — Why so many rules? Purposes and devotion
**The purposes of the Sharia.** Al-Shatibi (an Andalusian jurist), in *al-Muwāfaqāt*, organised the law around "the Five Universals (al-Kulliyyat al-Khams)", each "a fundamental human interest that the Shariah is designed to protect, promote, and balance": religion, life, intellect, lineage and family, and wealth (islam.wiki's explanatory chapter). Many rules can be understood this way: the ban on intoxicants protects the intellect; inheritance rules protect family and wealth.

**Reasoned and devotional rules.** Jurists commonly distinguish rules whose reasons can be understood and extended (*maʿqūl al-maʿnā*) from rules accepted as acts of devotion (*taʿabbudī*) (a standard distinction; no named source for it was read for this run), where the form is fixed even if its full reason is not stated — the number of units in each prayer, for example. The current page says that for al-Shatibi, "in acts of worship, devotion is the default"; that statement was not found in the chapter read for this run (see Corrections and Gaps).

**How to tell which is which.** A practical rule of thumb (this page's, not a named scholar's): where a text gives a reason (2:183's *taqwā*; 2:185's "ease"), the rule is reasoned at least in part; where the form of a worship act is fixed without a stated reason (times, numbers, directions), it is devotional. Most acts of worship have both: a stated purpose and a fixed form.

#### reasoning — "Why would God care?" and "Isn't it blind obedience?"
**God's care is for us.** The Quran and hadith are clear that God gains nothing (35:15; Muslim 2577). If He commands something, it is not because He lacks it. The command is for the worshipper — "that you may become righteous". Like a parent who tells a child to sleep, the instruction serves the one instructed.

**What fasting does.** It interrupts the most basic habits — eating and drinking — for a month, by choice, for God. A person who can say no to food at noon learns that they can say no to other things. That is what Bukhari 1904's "shield… from committing sins" points to. The fast is also shared: everyone in a community is hungry at the same time, and breaks the fast together.

**Following a rule you don't fully understand is not blind.** A patient follows a doctor's exact dose without knowing the pharmacology, because they have reasons to trust the doctor. Obedience is blind when it has no reason to trust; it is reasoned when the one commanding has shown wisdom and care elsewhere. For a Muslim, many rules show their purpose openly, and that grounds trust in the ones that don't.

*Limit:* this works only for someone who already has reason to trust the source. For someone who doubts revelation itself, the question becomes the earlier one ("Why believe Muhammad was a prophet?").

#### science — What research shows
**Weight and body fat.** [Moderate] In a 2019 meta-analysis (Fernando et al., *Nutrients*; 70 publications, 90 comparison groups, 2,947 participants), weight fell by 1.34 kg (95% CI −1.61 to −1.07) by the end of Ramadan, and was still 0.59 kg lower (−0.99 to −0.20) two to five weeks later. Fat mass fell by 0.98 kg. At follow-up the paper is not consistent with itself: its Discussion says fat measures were back to baseline, but its results section (3.2.1) reports a small remaining fall in fat percentage (−0.94%, 95% CI −1.73 to −0.15). The authors describe "Consistent—albeit transient—reductions in weight and fat mass", especially in people with overweight or obesity.

**Metabolic markers.** [Low-quality evidence] An umbrella review of eleven meta-analyses (Jahrami, Ammar, Glenn, Saif, Chtourou & Trabelsi, *Nutrition Reviews*, 2024, doi:10.1093/nutrit/nuae001) found small changes after Ramadan fasting, given as standardised mean differences (SMD; about 0.2 is conventionally called "small"): waist circumference −0.30 (95% CI −0.33 to −0.27), body weight −0.34 (−0.39 to −0.29), HDL +0.20 (0.10 to 0.30), LDL −0.10 (−0.13 to −0.07), total cholesterol −0.15 (−0.21 to −0.09), triglycerides −0.16 (−0.24 to −0.08), systolic and diastolic blood pressure −0.20 each, fasting glucose −0.10 (−0.12 to −0.08). The authors warn that "the methodological quality of the included reviews ranged from low to critically low", "necessitating cautious interpretation of conclusions drawn from these data". Non-English studies were excluded (2 Minute Medicine summary).

**Risks.** The current page advises people with eating disorders or diabetes to take medical advice. No clinical guideline on fasting risks was read for this run (see Gaps). The Quran's exemption for the ill (2:184) covers anyone for whom fasting would cause harm.

**Self-mastery and empathy.** No study measuring changes in self-control or empathy during Ramadan was read for this run. These are the tradition's claims (Bukhari 1904) and a common experience, not measured findings here.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"If God needs nothing, my fast is pointless."** *Reply:* it is pointless for God; it is not pointless for you. That is the Quran's own claim (2:183; 35:15). *Limit:* if one doubts that the fast does anything for the soul, the reply rests on experience and trust, not proof.

**"If it's for health, a doctor could prescribe something better."** *Reply:* health is not its stated purpose; *taqwā* is. The health effects are modest and temporary (Fernando et al. 2019). *Limit:* someone who values only measurable outcomes will not be persuaded by an inner purpose.

**"The rules are arbitrary."** *Reply:* many rules serve stated or obvious purposes (al-Shatibi's five universals); others are fixed forms of devotion, which give a shared shape to worship across the world. *Limit:* the exact reason for some details is not known, and Muslims accept them on trust.

#### views — The range of views
- **Classical jurists** — fasting is obligatory for every adult Muslim able to fast; exemptions as in 2:184–185, with the schools' differences on *fidya*.
- **Al-Shatibi and the *maqāṣid* tradition** — the law serves five universal interests. (That worship keeps its fixed form is a common framing; no named source for it was read.)
- **Some modern writers** — emphasise health benefits (which the evidence supports only modestly).

### Questions readers ask next
**Does God need my fasting, or is it purely for me?** God does not need it (35:15; Bukhari 1903). It is for you: "that you may become righteous" (2:183).

**How do I tell which rules have clear purposes and which are "pure devotion"?** Where a text states a reason, the rule is at least partly reasoned. Where a worship act has a fixed form without a stated reason (numbers, times), it is devotional.

**If a rule has no reason I can see, why is following it not blind obedience?** Because trust can be reasoned: when a source has shown wisdom and care in what you can understand, it is reasonable to follow it in what you cannot.

**What does Ramadan fasting involve, and who must do it?** No food, drink or sexual relations from dawn to sunset (2:187), for the month of Ramadan (2:185), for adult Muslims who are able.

**What is *taqwā*?** Awareness of God that guards a person from what displeases Him; the stated purpose of the fast (2:183).

**Who is exempt, and what do they do instead?** The ill and travellers make up the days. The very old and chronically ill pay *fidya* (feeding a poor person each day). Pregnant and breastfeeding women may not fast if they fear harm; the schools differ on whether they make up, pay *fidya*, or both.

### Honest limits
Al-Shatibi's own text, and any statement of his on worship being devotional by default, were not read. The Maliki position on pregnant and breastfeeding women was not covered. The umbrella review was read through its abstract (Mainz university bibliography record), not its full text. No study of self-control or empathy during Ramadan, and no clinical guideline on fasting risks, was read.

## Q10 — Why five daily prayers, and why do I feel nothing?

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via `src/data/quran.json` — 20:14, 23:1–2, 2:186; via legacy.quran.com — 29:45.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 349 (hadithunlocked.com, with ʿAbd al-Baqi's "Agreed Upon"), https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:349 — the Miʿrāj.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 4937 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4937 — the reciter who struggles.
- Irshaad Sedick, "The Five Levels of Prayer", SeekersGuidance (1 September 2022), https://seekersguidance.org/articles/the-five-levels-of-prayer/ — Ibn al-Qayyim's five grades from *al-Wābil al-Ṣayyib*, in translation.
- Faraz Rabbani, "How Do I Revive a Heart That Feels Spiritually Numb?", SeekersGuidance (3 August 2026), https://seekersguidance.org/answers/general-counsel/how-do-i-revive-a-heart-that-feels-spiritually-numb/.

### The question, at its strongest
"Five times a day, in a language I barely understand, with fixed words and movements. I do it, and I feel nothing. If prayer is supposed to be a conversation with God and my heart isn't in it, isn't it hollow — even hypocrisy? And why five? The Quran never says 'five'. Some people say the Quran mentions only three prayer times."

### Why people ask it
Because almost everyone who prays regularly has dry periods. And because the number five is taught early, while the Quran's verses on prayer times are not, so a reader who opens the Quran looking for "five" is surprised.

### Short answer
Feeling nothing in prayer is common, has been discussed for a thousand years, and does not make the prayer worthless. The Quran's first purpose for prayer is remembrance: "establish prayer for My remembrance" (20:14). Ibn al-Qayyim described five grades of people in prayer, from the careless to those whose heart is fully before God; the one who keeps praying while struggling with distraction is not the lowest but in the middle, and the one who resists distraction is "redeemed". The Prophet (pbuh) said that the one who recites the Quran with difficulty gets a double reward ({hadith:bukhari:4937}). Presence of heart (*khushūʿ*) grows with understanding the words, slowing down and preparation. The number five comes from the report of the Miʿrāj in Bukhari and Muslim and from unbroken practice; the Quran names prayer times without counting them.

### The full answer

#### heart — If you feel nothing
**You are not a hypocrite for praying when you feel dry.** A hypocrite pretends to believe what he does not. You believe, and you pray even though it is hard. That is faithfulness, not pretence. Rabbani puts it plainly: "worship carried out without sweetness is frequently weightier than worship carried by feeling."

**A sign of hope in the hadith.** The Prophet (pbuh) said that the one who recites the Quran with difficulty, struggling with it, "will have a double reward" ({hadith:bukhari:4937}, narrated by ʿAʾisha (ra); paraphrased except for the quoted words) — reward for the struggle itself.

**Do not chase the feeling.** Rabbani advises not to chase it — "Chasing it is how people end up performing a state rather than living one" — to keep practice steady and small, and to pray, in his wording, "O Turner of hearts, make my heart firm upon Your religion" (the hadith is Tirmidhi 3522, narrated by Umm Salama (ra); al-Tirmidhi: *ḥasan*; al-Albani: *ṣaḥīḥ*, per hadithunlocked).

**When to get help.** If "feeling nothing" covers your whole life — food, friends, work — that may be depression, not a spiritual problem, and a doctor can help. If you repeat your ablution or prayer again and again from fear that it is invalid, that may be religious OCD (scrupulosity); the International OCD Foundation (iocdf.org) describes it and its treatment.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Prayer as remembrance (20:14).** "Indeed, I am Allah. There is no deity except Me, so worship Me and establish prayer for My remembrance" (20:14), Meccan — God's words to Moses (pbuh) at the fire in the valley of Ṭuwā (20:10–12). The first purpose named is *dhikr*, remembrance: turning the mind back to God, whether or not one feels moved.

**Humility (23:1–2).** "Certainly will the believers have succeeded: They who are during their prayer humbly submissive" (23:1–2), Meccan. *Khāshiʿūn*, "humbly submissive", is the root of *khushūʿ*. It is listed first among the believers' qualities: a goal to grow towards.

**What prayer does (29:45).** "Recite, [O Muhammad], what has been revealed to you of the Book and establish prayer. Indeed, prayer prohibits immorality and wrongdoing, and the remembrance of Allah is greater" (29:45), Meccan. Prayer's effect is described in behaviour — it holds a person back from wrong — not in feelings.

**Prayer times.** The Quran names times of prayer in several verses (e.g. 11:114; 17:78; 30:17–18, none quoted from verified data here) but does not state the number five.

#### sunnah — Where "five" comes from
**The Miʿrāj.** The *Miʿrāj* is the Prophet's (pbuh) ascension through the heavens, during the Night Journey. In the report of Anas ibn Malik from Abu Dharr (ra), God first enjoined fifty prayers; Moses (pbuh) told the Prophet (pbuh), "Go back to your Lord (and appeal for reduction) for your followers will not be able to bear it"; after several returns the number was reduced, and God said: "These are five prayers and they are all (equal to) fifty (in reward) for My Word does not change" ({hadith:bukhari:349}; graded "Agreed Upon" — in both Bukhari and Muslim — by ʿAbd al-Baqi). The man from Najd was likewise told: "You have to offer prayers perfectly five times in a day and night" ({hadith:bukhari:46}).

#### scholars — Ibn al-Qayyim's five grades of people in prayer
Ibn al-Qayyim (a 14th-century Damascene scholar), in *al-Wābil al-Ṣayyib*, describes five kinds of people in prayer (as presented by Sedick):
1. One who "wrongs his soul" (Sedick, citing 35:32): careless about ablution, times and essential pillars — "punished".
2. One who keeps the times, limits, outward pillars and ablution, but is carried away by whisperings he does not resist — "admonished".
3. One who keeps the limits and pillars and *struggles* against the whisperings — "redeemed".
4. One who perfects the prayer's rights, limits and pillars, with his heart absorbed in doing so — "rewarded".
5. One who prays like the fourth, but whose heart is before his Lord, who "beholds his Lord" — "drawn close to his Lord".

The person who feels dry but keeps praying, and keeps pulling the mind back, is in the third grade: "redeemed".

**Al-Qushayri on dry phases.** Rabbani reports al-Qushayri's account of *qabḍ* (contraction) and *basṭ* (expansion) as states of the present moment, which are "neither achievements nor failures. They are visitations, and they alternate."

**Al-Ghazali.** The current page cites al-Ghazali's *Asrār al-Ṣalāt* (in the *Iḥyāʾ*) for six inner meanings of prayer — presence, understanding, reverence, awe, hope and shame. That text was not read for this run (see Gaps).

#### reasoning — The three-prayers argument, and the reply
**The argument.** Some Quran-centred Muslims argue that the Quran mentions only three prayer times (no named holder or work was read for this run), so three prayers are enough; five, on this view, rests on hadith they do not accept.

**The reply.**
1. The five prayers are in the Miʿrāj report, which is in both Bukhari and Muslim ({hadith:bukhari:349}), and in other reports such as Bukhari 46.
2. More importantly, the five daily prayers have been practised by the whole Muslim community, in every region, from the earliest records — a practice passed on by millions, not by one chain of narrators.
3. The Quran's verses name times without claiming to list all of them.

*Limit:* for someone who rejects hadith altogether, the first point carries no weight, and the second becomes a question of whether community practice can be trusted (see "Are hadith reliable?").

#### heart — Building *khushūʿ*: concrete steps
(These are common teaching points; only the first two are drawn from sources read for this run.)
1. **Keep the rhythm, even dry.** Steady, small practice (Rabbani).
2. **Don't chase feelings; pray for a firm heart** (Rabbani).
3. **Learn the meaning of what you say.** Start with al-Fātiḥa, then the phrases of bowing and prostration. A prayer in a language you do not understand becomes different once each phrase has a meaning.
4. **Slow down.** Pause in each position long enough to say its words calmly.
5. **Prepare.** Make ablution without hurry; pause for a moment before starting; put the phone away.
6. **Pick one point of focus each prayer** — one verse, one phrase — rather than trying to feel everything.

#### fiqh — What a Muslim does in the five prayers (for newcomers)
The five prayers are at dawn (*fajr*), just after midday (*ẓuhr*), the afternoon (*ʿaṣr*), just after sunset (*maghrib*) and night (*ʿishāʾ*). Each prayer is made of units (*rakʿa*): standing and reciting al-Fātiḥa and other verses, bowing, standing again, two prostrations, and sitting. It is said in Arabic, which keeps the same words across the whole world. (This description is general; no source describing prayer step by step was read for this run.)

**The Arabic-language concern.** The fixed Arabic of the prayer is part of what makes it shared; personal prayer (*duʿāʾ*) can be in any language, at any time — "I respond to the invocation of the supplicant when he calls upon Me" (2:186).

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Prayer without feeling is hypocrisy."** *Reply:* hypocrisy is pretending to believe; praying while dry is keeping faith without reward of feeling. Ibn al-Qayyim's third grade ("redeemed") and Bukhari 4937 ("a double reward" for the one who struggles to recite) treat the struggle itself as rewarded, and better than giving in to distraction. They do not rank it highest: in Ibn al-Qayyim the present heart (grades 4–5) is above it, and the commentators on Bukhari 4937, as quoted by hadeethenc.com ("the Qadi", i.e. Qadi ʿIyad), say the skilled reciter is still better and has the greater reward. *Limit:* prayer that is *only* habit, with no attempt at presence, is Ibn al-Qayyim's second grade — "admonished" — so the reply is not an excuse for carelessness.

**"Five times is too much."** *Reply:* each prayer takes minutes; spread through the day, they are short returns to God. *Limit:* for a new Muslim or someone returning, five can feel heavy; many teachers advise building up gradually (no named source read).

#### science — What research shows
The current page says that structured ritual can lower anxiety and that studies of Muslim prayer show physiological changes in small samples. No study was read for this run, so no claim about prayer's measurable effects is made here (see Corrections and Gaps).

#### views — The range of views
- **Sunni and Shiʿi Muslims** — five daily prayers, as practised from the beginning (the Shiʿi practice of combining them into three time-slots is a timing difference, not a reduction; no source read).
- **Some Quran-centred Muslims** — three prayers, from the Quran's named times.
- **Sufi tradition (al-Qushayri)** — dry and full states alternate; steady practice through both.

### Questions readers ask next
**If prayer without feeling is acceptable, what does it actually do for me right now?** It keeps you returning to God several times a day (20:14), it holds you back from wrong (29:45), and the struggle is itself rewarded (Bukhari 4937; Ibn al-Qayyim's third grade).

**Why five and not three? How strong is the Miʿrāj evidence?** The Miʿrāj report is in both Bukhari and Muslim ("Agreed Upon"), and five prayers have been the practice of the whole community from the earliest times.

**What concrete steps build *khushūʿ*?** Keep the rhythm; learn the meaning of al-Fātiḥa and the main phrases; slow down; prepare; focus on one phrase at a time; pray for a firm heart.

**What does a Muslim do and say in the five prayers, and when?** See "What a Muslim does in the five prayers".

**What is the Miʿrāj?** The Prophet's (pbuh) ascension through the heavens, in which the five daily prayers were given (Bukhari 349).

**What are *qabḍ* and *basṭ*, and who was al-Qushayrī?** Contraction and expansion of the heart, states that come and go; al-Qushayri, author of the Sufi manual *al-Risāla*, described them (as reported by Rabbani).

### Honest limits
No technique guarantees feeling. Al-Ghazali's *Asrār al-Ṣalāt*, al-Qushayri's own text, the Quran's prayer-time verses (11:114; 17:78) in verified text, and any study of prayer's physiological effects were not read. The practical steps beyond Rabbani's are general teaching, not sourced here.

## Q9 — Why does God need our worship?

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via `src/data/quran.json` — 51:56–57, 35:15, 14:7, 79:40–41, 2:186.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English), and *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn* (English), on 51:56, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-56-sora-51.html.
- The hadith qudsi of Abu Dharr (Nawawi's Forty, no. 24; Muslim), https://sunnah.com/nawawi40:24.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 7404 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:7404.
- Akram Nadwi, "Servitude: Ibn Taymiyyah on Worship and Human Existence" (13 May 2026), https://akramnadwi.com/servitude-ibn-taymiyyah-on-worship-and-human-existence/.

### The question, at its strongest
"If God is perfect and needs nothing, why command worship at all? A human who demanded constant praise would seem insecure. And if worship is only for my benefit, why is not worshipping punished — even eternally — instead of just being a missed chance? That sounds less like medicine and more like a demand backed by a threat."

### Why people ask it
Because the Quran is full of praise of God and commands to worship Him, and a modern reader hears that as a need. And because many people were raised on worship taught through fear.

### Short answer
The Quran agrees that God needs nothing: "O mankind, you are those in need of Allah, while Allah is the Free of need, the Praiseworthy" (35:15). Right after "I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me", God says, "I do not want from them any provision, nor do I want them to feed Me" (51:56–57). In a hadith qudsi, God says: "you will not attain harming Me so as to harm Me, and you will not attain benefiting Me so as to benefit Me" (Muslim 2577). Worship is for the worshipper. In Islam it is also much wider than ritual: Ibn Taymiyya defined it as "everything that God loves and is pleased with" in word and deed, inward and outward — honesty, keeping family ties, kindness. The hardest part of the question is why neglecting it has consequences. One reading of the Quran's answer — a theological reading, not the verses' own words — is that consequences follow what a person becomes, and that God's mercy outweighs His wrath (Bukhari 7404). This page states that answer and its limit.

### The full answer

#### heart — If you were raised on fear
**God's own description of Himself puts mercy first.** "Verily My Mercy overcomes My Anger" ({hadith:bukhari:7404}, Abu Hurayra), written, the hadith says, in a Book "placed with Him on the Throne". If your picture of God is mainly of anger, it is not the picture God gives of Himself.

**God is near and answers.** "And when My servants ask you, [O Muhammad], concerning Me - indeed I am near. I respond to the invocation of the supplicant when he calls upon Me" (2:186). Worship can begin as conversation, not as fear of punishment.

**Practical steps.** Start with what draws you, not only with what you fear: thanking God for one thing each day; a short *duʿāʾ* in your own words; reading verses about mercy. If fear of God becomes constant anxiety, with checking and repeating, that may be religious OCD; the International OCD Foundation (iocdf.org) describes it and its treatment.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The purpose, and the denial of need (51:56–57).** "And I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me. I do not want from them any provision, nor do I want them to feed Me" (51:56–57), Meccan. In the same breath that names worship as the purpose of creation, God denies that He needs anything from it.

**Need is on our side (35:15).** "O mankind, you are those in need of Allah, while Allah is the Free of need, the Praiseworthy" (35:15), Meccan. *Al-Ghanī*, "the Free of need", and *al-Ḥamīd*, "the Praiseworthy": God is worthy of praise whether or not anyone praises Him.

**Gratitude (14:7).** "And [remember] when your Lord proclaimed, 'If you are grateful, I will surely increase you [in favor]; but if you deny, indeed, My punishment is severe'" (14:7), Meccan, in Moses' (pbuh) words to his people. Gratitude increases the one who is grateful.

#### scholars — How the commentators read "to worship Me"
**Ibn Kathir.** "I, Allah, only created them so that I order them to worship Me, not that I need them." He reports Ibn ʿAbbas: "So that they worship Me, willingly or unwillingly." He also cites a hadith in which God says: "O Son of Adam! Busy yourself in worshipping Me, and I will fill your chest with riches and dissipate your meekness" (recorded by Ahmad, al-Tirmidhi and Ibn Majah; Tirmidhi 2466, Abu Hurayra (ra); al-Tirmidhi: *ḥasan gharīb*; al-Albani: *ṣaḥīḥ*, per hadithunlocked).

**Al-Jalālayn.** It addresses the obvious objection — that many do not worship — and says "the fact that disbelievers do not worship God does not contradict this statement", comparing it to a pencil sharpened for writing: its purpose is writing, even if it is never used.

**Other readings on the current page.** The current page reports that Ibn ʿAbbas, via al-Tabari, glossed "to worship Me" as "to know Me". On the Ibn Kathir page read, that gloss does not appear; the phrase "to recognize Me, serve Me" appears in a different commentary (*al-Muntakhab*) on the same page (see Corrections).

**What *ʿibāda* covers.** Ibn Taymiyya, in his treatise *al-ʿUbūdiyya*, defined worship as "a collective term for everything that God loves and is pleased with, whether of outward or inward sayings and actions" (Nadwi's rendering). On this view, worship includes prayer, fasting, almsgiving and pilgrimage, but also truthfulness, trustworthiness, dutifulness to parents, keeping family ties, kindness and good character (Nadwi). Worship is not a slice of life; it is a way of living all of it.

**Kalām on God's purposes.** The current page reports a difference between the Ashʿaris, who deny that God acts for a purpose that would complete Him, and the Maturidis, who affirm wisdom (*ḥikma*) in His acts for creatures' benefit; both agree He gains nothing. (*Kalām* is Islamic rational theology.) No source on this difference was read for this run (see Gaps).

#### reasoning — Why worship, if God needs nothing?
**1. Praise that fits, not praise that is needed.** We praise a beautiful piece of music not because the composer needs it, but because praise is the fitting response to what is good. On the Islamic view, God is the source of every good; praising Him is seeing things as they are. That is why the Quran calls God *al-Ḥamīd*, "the Praiseworthy" — worthy of praise — while also saying He is "Free of need" (35:15).

**2. Worship serves the worshipper.** Like medicine, it is for the patient, not the doctor. Gratitude increases the grateful (14:7). Remembering God reorients a person away from the self. Ibn Kathir's hadith puts it in God's words: "I will fill your chest with riches".

**3. A God who invites worship differs from a human who demands it.** A human who demands constant praise is insecure because he needs it. God, by His own statement, gains nothing from it (Muslim 2577). The structure is the opposite: the human is the one in need.

*Limit:* this answer works for someone who already accepts that God is good. For someone who doubts that, the commands to worship can still sound like demands.

#### objection — "If it's for my benefit, why is not worshipping punished?"
**The objection in full.** If I skip medicine, I just stay ill; the doctor does not punish me. If worship is only for my good, why does the Quran attach severe, even eternal, consequences to neglecting it?

**The reply.**
1. *Consequences follow what a person becomes.* "But as for he who feared the position of his Lord and prevented the soul from [unlawful] inclination, Then indeed, Paradise will be [his] refuge" (79:40–41). On one reading (a modern theological reading; no named holder was read for this run), the Hereafter is the result of the self one has made: a person who turns away from God for a lifetime becomes someone turned away. The Quran itself also speaks of recompense (*jazāʾ*), so this reading does not exhaust it.
2. *Ingratitude is not neutral.* 14:7 sets gratitude and denial side by side. To deny the source of every good is not only to miss a benefit; it is to wrong the truth.
3. *Mercy is greater.* "Verily My Mercy overcomes My Anger" (Bukhari 7404). The Quran's warnings are real, but they sit inside a frame in which mercy prevails.
4. *The account is of your own deeds.* "it is but your deeds that I account for you, and then recompense you for. So he who finds good, let him praise Allah" (al-Nawawi's Forty, no. 24, from Muslim 2577).

*Limit:* the language of wrath and eternal punishment in the Quran is strong, and some readers will still find it hard to reconcile with the "medicine" picture. This page does not remove that difficulty; it shows how the tradition holds the two together.

**"What does 'neglect is self-harm' mean in concrete terms?"** A person who never pauses to thank, never turns from the self, never holds back from wrong for God's sake, gradually becomes harder, more anxious and more self-centred — the reverse of the "riches" of the chest Ibn Kathir's hadith promises. That is a moral and spiritual claim, not a measured one.

#### science — What research shows
The current page says gratitude and contemplative practice are associated with modest wellbeing benefits. No study was read for this run, so the claim is not repeated here (see Corrections and Gaps).

#### views — The range of views
- **Quran and hadith** — God is free of need; worship is for humans (35:15; 51:57; Muslim 2577).
- **Ibn Taymiyya** — worship covers all that God loves, inward and outward.
- **Ashʿaris and Maturidis** (as the current page reports) — differ on whether God's acts have purposes; agree He gains nothing.
- **Critics** — commanded worship backed by punishment looks like a demand, not a gift.

### Questions readers ask next
**If worship is only for my benefit, why is not worshipping punished?** Because, in the Quran's picture, the Hereafter is the outcome of the self one becomes (79:40–41), denying the source of good is a wrong (14:7), and mercy still outweighs wrath (Bukhari 7404). The difficulty does not disappear entirely.

**Why does the Quran call God "the Praiseworthy" if He does not want praise?** Because He is worthy of praise whether or not anyone praises Him (35:15). Praise is the fitting response to the good, not a supply God needs.

**What does "neglect is self-harm" mean in concrete terms?** That a life without gratitude, remembrance or restraint shapes a person into someone narrower and harder — the opposite of the "riches" of the chest God promises the worshipper (Ibn Kathir's hadith).

**What does *ʿibāda* cover?** Ibn Taymiyya: "everything that God loves and is pleased with", outward and inward — from prayer to honesty and kindness to parents.

**What are Ashʿarīs, Māturīdīs, *kalām* and a hadith qudsi?** *Kalām*: Islamic rational theology. The Ashʿaris and Maturidis: its two main Sunni schools. A hadith qudsi: a saying the Prophet (pbuh) attributes to God, in the Prophet's own words, distinct from the Quran.

**What does *taʿabbudī* mean?** An act of worship whose form is followed as devotion, even where its full reason is not stated (e.g. the number of units in a prayer).

### Honest limits
The answer to "why punishment?" is the tradition's, and it leaves some readers unsatisfied. The Ashʿari–Maturidi difference on God's purposes, al-Ghazali's *Kitāb al-Maḥabba*, al-Razi on 51:56, Nursi and Tim Winter (all cited on the current page) were not read for this run. No wellbeing study was read.
