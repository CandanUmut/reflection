## Q2 — If everything is written, how am I free?

### The question, at its strongest
"The Quran says God created 'all things with predestination' (54:49), that every disaster is 'in a register before We bring it into being' (57:22), and that 'you do not will except that Allah wills' (81:29). Muslim theologians go further: God does not only *know* what I will do; He *creates* my acts. If God creates my act, and wrote it before I was born, in what sense is it mine? I could not have done otherwise. Then punishing me looks unjust, and my effort looks pointless. The usual answer — 'knowing is not causing' — only answers the easy half of the problem."

### Why people ask it
Because the doctrine of *qadar* (divine decree) is part of the creed, and because ordinary people meet it at hard moments. Some fear they are "written" for Hell whatever they do. Some use "it is written" to excuse themselves. Some freeze. For a person with religious OCD, the question can become a loop of fear.

### Short answer
Islam affirms two things at once: God's decree covers everything, and human beings really choose and are really answerable — but only within their capacity: "Allah does not charge a soul except [with that within] its capacity" (2:286). There are two problems here, not one. The first — God *knows* what I will do — is the easier: knowing is not forcing. The second — God *creates* what I do — is the hard one, and Muslim theologians answered it in different ways. The Ashʿaris said God creates the act and the human "acquires" (*kasb*) it; the Maturidis said the human really chooses, with a will God created, and is the real doer. *Encyclopaedia Britannica* notes that *kasb* "remained one of the most vague theories in Islāmic theology". When the Companions asked the Prophet (pbuh), "Shall we not depend upon what has been written for us and give up deeds?", he answered: "Carry on doing (good) deeds, for everybody will find easy to do such deeds as will lead him to his destined place" ({hadith:bukhari:4949}). No one knows what is written for them; what they know is the choice in front of them.

### The full answer

#### heart — If you are afraid you are "written" for Hell
**You cannot read the register, and no one else can either.** The fear says, "Maybe it is already decided against me." But the hadith that speaks of each person's written place ends with an instruction, not a verdict: keep doing good. The Prophet (pbuh) recited, "As for he who gives and fears Allah And believes in the best [reward], We will ease him toward ease" (92:5–7). The sign of what is written for you is what you do next — and that is still open to you today.

**If the thought will not leave you alone.** If the fear of damnation comes back again and again, against your will, and you cannot stop checking, praying for certainty, or asking others for reassurance, this may be scrupulosity — "a subtype of obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) involving religious or moral obsessions" (International OCD Foundation). Its listed obsessions include "Fear of going to hell or being punished by God". It is treatable: cognitive behaviour therapy, with exposure and response prevention, is the main psychological treatment, and treatment "may also include consultation with leaders of the patient's faith tradition" (IOCDF).

> **Where to find help.** The International OCD Foundation (iocdf.org) has a section on faith and OCD. Find A Helpline (findahelpline.com) lists free, confidential support lines in many countries. A trusted imam or teacher who understands OCD can help you separate the illness from your faith.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Decree.** "Indeed, all things We created with predestination" (54:49), from a Meccan passage warning those who denied the Hour. "No disaster strikes upon the earth or among yourselves except that it is in a register before We bring it into being - indeed that, for Allah, is easy" (57:22), Medinan; the next verse gives its purpose: "In order that you not despair over what has eluded you and not exult [in pride] over what He has given you" (57:23).

**Choice.** "And say, 'The truth is from your Lord, so whoever wills - let him believe; and whoever wills - let him disbelieve'" (18:29), Meccan. Read in context, this is not neutral permission: the verse goes straight on to the fire prepared for the wrongdoers. Ibn Kathir: "This is a type of threat and stern warning". Al-Jalālayn says it is addressed to ʿUyayna b. Ḥiṣn and his companions (named in its note on 18:28), and "this is meant as a threat to them". But a threat assumes a real choice; one does not warn a stone.

**Both together.** 81:28 says the Quran is a reminder for whoever among you wills to go straight (paraphrased; not quoted from verified data). The next verse adds: "And you do not will except that Allah wills - Lord of the worlds" (81:29). Ibn Kathir reports, through Sulayman ibn Musa, that when 81:28 was revealed Abu Jahl said, "The matter is up to us. If we wish, we will stand straight", and that 81:29 was revealed in answer (no chain is given on the page read). His gloss: "the will is not left to you all". Al-Jalālayn: "you will not wish to go straight along the path of truth unless God the Lord of the Worlds" wills it. So the pair says: you do will — and your willing is not outside God's will.

**Responsibility.** "Allah does not charge a soul except [with that within] its capacity. It will have [the consequence of] what [good] it has gained, and it will bear [the consequence of] what [evil] it has earned" (2:286). The two verbs, *kasabat* ("gained") and *iktasabat* ("earned"), share the root k-s-b of the theologians' term *kasb*. And: "He has succeeded who purifies it, And he has failed who instills it [with corruption]" (91:9–10).

**Created acts.** Abraham (pbuh) says to his people, who carve idols: "While Allah created you and that which you do?" (37:96). The verse is often cited in the debate on created acts. The *Mukhtasar* commentary (on Quranpedia) reads it in context: "the making of these idols are also from your actions", and God "is the one who created your bodies and your actions", so He alone deserves worship. Whether "that which you do" means the idols or the acts of making them, the verse alone does not settle the theology.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**The hadith of the funeral (Bukhari 4949).** ʿAli (ra) reports that the Prophet (pbuh) was "in a funeral procession". He "picked up something and started scraping the ground with it" and said: "There is none among you but has his place written for him either in the Hell Fire or in Paradise." They asked: "Shall we not depend upon what has been written for us and give up deeds?" He replied: "Carry on doing (good) deeds, for everybody will find easy to do such deeds as will lead him to his destined place", and recited 92:5–10: "As for he who gives and fears Allah And believes in the best [reward], We will ease him toward ease. But as for he who withholds and considers himself free of need And denies the best [reward], We will ease him toward difficulty."

**What it teaches.** The Companions asked the fatalist's question — why act? — and the Prophet (pbuh) refused the fatalist's conclusion. The verses he recited describe a two-way relation: the person gives, fears God and believes; God eases. The person withholds and thinks himself self-sufficient; God eases him the other way. The decree is not presented as a wall in front of the person, but as working through what the person does.

#### scholars — The classical map
**Why there is a debate at all.** Two things the Quran affirms seem to pull apart: God is the Creator of everything, and human beings are answerable for what they do. Each school protected one of these first.

- **The Jabriyya (determinists).** On their view, as summarised by Yavuz, will and power play no role in how an act comes about, so the act cannot truly be attributed to the human (TDV, "Kesb"). They protect God's sole agency. The usual objection (this page's summary, not from a named source) is that the view makes commands and warnings pointless.
- **The Muʿtazila.** The human is the real doer and creator of his own act: "her kâsib aynı zamanda fâildir" — every acquirer is also the doer (TDV). They protect God's justice: God punishes only what humans themselves did. The usual objection (this page's summary) is that this makes humans creators alongside God.
- **The Ashʿaris.** God creates the act; the human "acquires" it. For Ashʿari theologians, the act comes about through two powers: God's eternal power, the main factor ("asıl etken"), on which "the act's existence and origination depend" ("fiillerin vücudu ve hudûsü bu kudrete bağlıdır"), and the human's created power, whose effect on the act is what *kasb* means (TDV). The human is called a "kâsib" (acquirer) in the real sense and a "fâil" (doer) only figuratively (TDV, citing al-Baghdadi, *Uṣūl al-dīn*, p. 137). Fakhr al-Din al-Razi read *kasb* as having no effect at all on the act, which, Yavuz says, brought the Ashʿari theory close to determinism (TDV). Britannica describes *kasb* as al-Ashʿari's "mean between predestination and free will". Later, al-Juwayni is said to have given *kasb* the main role in how the act comes about, moving closer to the Muʿtazila (TDV).
- **The Maturidis.** *Kasb* is the human using the will and power that God has created in him; "the real doer of the act is the human"; *kasb* is not the creation of the act but the human's turning towards it. Yavuz reports the Maturidi formula that *kasb* happens "with an instrument" while creation happens "without an instrument" (TDV). This gives human choice a stronger, more real role than the classical Ashʿari view.

**Is *kasb* clear?** Not entirely, and Muslim writers have said so. Britannica: many theologians regarded it as "indistinguishable from pure predetermination", and it "remained one of the most vague theories in Islāmic theology".

**Kasb and *cüz'î irade*, in plain words.** *Cüz'î irade* ("partial will") is the Turkish name, used especially in the Maturidi tradition, for the small but real power of choice God gives each person. An everyday picture: when you decide to stand up, you did not make your muscles, your nerves, the floor or the law of gravity. All of that is given — in the theologians' language, created. What is yours is the turning of your will: the decision to stand. That small turning is what you are answerable for. Nursi puts the asymmetry this way: in good acts "we have only a minute share", while "we are completely responsible for our sins, for only we will and commit them"; and "willing and committing evil deeds is evil, whereas creating them is not" (Twenty-Sixth Word).

#### reasoning — How philosophers frame it
**The foreknowledge problem (the easier one).** If God knows today that I will lie tomorrow, must I lie? A common answer (this page's summary; no named classical source was read): knowing is not causing; God's knowledge follows what I will freely do, it does not push me. Some add that God is outside time, so He does not "fore"-know at all; He knows. This answers fatalism ("it will happen whatever I do") but not the harder problem.

**The creation-of-acts problem (the harder one).** Here the claim is not just that God knows my act, but that He brings it into being. Two kinds of answer:
1. **The Maturidi answer.** God creates my capacity and the outward act, but the choosing is truly mine; God creates the act *according to* my choice. The weight of responsibility falls on that choice. *Limit:* the critic asks whether my choosing is itself created; if yes, the problem returns; if no, something escapes God's creation. Yavuz reports Maturidi scholars as saying that although acts are created by God, "the kasb that affects their coming about is not created by God" ("bunların meydana gelişine tesir eden kesb Allah tarafından yaratılmış değildir", TDV). Whether that solves the problem or only names it is the point in dispute.
2. **A compatibilist answer.** *Compatibilism* is the view that freedom and responsibility are compatible with everything being determined. On this view, an act is free if it comes from your own reasons and desires, without force — not if it is uncaused. Applied here: God's creating my act and my freely choosing it are two descriptions of one event, at different levels. *Limit:* incompatibilists reply that if I could never have done otherwise, I am not truly responsible.

**Frankfurt cases (for non-specialists).** The *principle of alternate possibilities* says: "An agent is responsible for an action only if said agent could have done otherwise" (*Wikipedia*). In 1969 Harry Frankfurt imagined a hidden controller, Black, ready to make Jones choose X if Jones started to choose otherwise. Jones chooses X on his own; Black never acts. Jones could not have done otherwise — yet, Frankfurt argues, he "will bear precisely the same moral responsibility for what he does" as if Black did not exist. So responsibility may not require the ability to do otherwise. *Limit:* critics (Widerker, Ginet, Kane) answer with a dilemma: if Jones's choice was determined, the case assumes what it should prove; if not, Jones could have done otherwise after all (*Wikipedia*). The debate is live.

#### science — What neuroscience shows
**What Libet found.** In Benjamin Libet's 1983 experiments, people flexed a wrist whenever they liked and reported, by a clock, when they first felt the urge. A brain signal called the *readiness potential* began "approximately five hundred milliseconds before" the movement, while the reported urge came about 200 ms before it — so the brain signal led conscious awareness by roughly 300 ms (*Wikipedia*). Many took this to show that the brain "decides" before we do. Libet himself proposed that consciousness can still veto an action ("free won't").

**Why later work does not take it to settle free will.** [Moderate]
- Schurger, Sitt and Dehaene (2012) proposed that when the drive to move is weak, "the precise moment at which the decision threshold is crossed […] is largely determined by spontaneous subthreshold fluctuations in neuronal activity"; averaging many trials makes these fluctuations look like a gradual build-up. On this view, the readiness potential may not be a hidden decision at all.
- A 2019 study (reported on *Wikipedia*) found the readiness potential absent for deliberate decisions and present only for arbitrary ones.
- Daniel Dennett and others questioned the timing method itself, which relies on people's reports of when they noticed an urge (*Wikipedia*).
- Above all, the task — flexing a wrist at a random moment, with nothing at stake — is very different from the moral choices religion is about.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"If God creates my act, it is not mine."** *Reply:* the Maturidi answer locates ownership in the choosing; the Ashʿari answer in the acquiring; the compatibilist in the act's coming from your own reasons. All three deny that "created by God" means "forced on you".

**"Then punishment is unjust."** *Reply:* the Quran ties judgement to capacity (2:286) and to what a person "earned"; a person is not judged for what was outside their power. *Limit:* a skeptic can still ask why God created some people whom He knew would choose evil — the question of theodicy, which this page does not settle.

**"Then effort is pointless."** *Reply:* this is exactly the question put to the Prophet (pbuh), and his answer was the opposite: act ({hadith:bukhari:4949}). Effort is one of the means through which the decree works. *Limit:* this is a practical answer more than a metaphysical one.

#### views — The range of views
- **Jabriyya** — humans have no real power; acts are God's alone (rejected by Sunni theology).
- **Muʿtazila** — humans create their own acts; God's justice requires it.
- **Ashʿaris** — God creates acts and is the main factor in them; human created power plays the role called *kasb*; al-Razi's reading gave it no effect at all.
- **Maturidis** — God creates acts and the capacity; the human really chooses (*cüz'î irade*) and is the real doer.
- **Some modern philosophers** — compatibilism; others libertarian free will; others hard determinism.

### Questions readers ask next
**If God creates my acts, in what sense are they mine?** For the Maturidis, because you really choose them: God creates the capacity and the act, but the turning of the will is yours. For the Ashʿaris, because you "acquire" them. For compatibilists, because they come from your own reasons, without force.

**Can I be "written" for Hell no matter what I do?** No one knows what is written for them, and the Prophet (pbuh), when asked this, told people to act, because each is eased towards what they were made for (92:5–10; {hadith:bukhari:4949}). What you do next is the only sign you have, and it is still in your hands. If the fear keeps returning against your will, see "If you are afraid" above.

**What does Bukhari 4949 say in full, and how is "eased toward what they were created for" read?** See "What the Prophet (pbuh) taught". The verses he recited tie the easing to what a person does: giving, fearing God, believing — or withholding and thinking oneself free of need.

**What is *kasb*, and what is *cüz'î irade*, in plain words?** *Kasb* ("acquisition") is the theologians' word for the human share in an act God creates. *Cüz'î irade* ("partial will") is the small but real power of choice God gives each person. You did not make your body or the world; you do make the decision.

**Do Muslims believe in fatalism ("it is written") or not?** No. The Quran and the hadith affirm decree and command action together. Using "it is written" to stop trying is the conclusion the Prophet (pbuh) rejected in Bukhari 4949.

**What are "Frankfurt cases" and "compatibilism"?** Compatibilism: freedom and responsibility can exist even if everything is determined. Frankfurt cases: thought experiments meant to show that a person can be responsible even when they could not have done otherwise (see "How philosophers frame it").

### Honest limits
The hardest version of the problem — that God creates acts, not only knows them — has answers, but no answer that removes all difficulty; Britannica calls *kasb* "one of the most vague theories in Islāmic theology". Al-Maturidi's *Kitāb al-Tawḥīd*, al-Ghazali's *al-Iqtiṣād*, the TDV articles "Kader" and "Cebriyye", and Elmalılı have not yet been consulted directly. The neuroscience shows that Libet's experiments do not settle free will; it does not show that free will exists.

## Q5 — Is evolution compatible with Islam?

### Sources read

### The question, at its strongest
"Biology shows that all living things, humans included, share common ancestors. The evidence is not one fossil but genetics, anatomy and the fossil record together. The Quran says Adam was made from clay, that angels bowed to him, and that all people come from one soul. Population genetics says human genetic diversity is too great to come from one couple in the last few hundred thousand years. So either the Quran is wrong about Adam, or the science is wrong — and the science is very strong."

### Why people ask it
Students meet evolution in biology class while hearing at home or in the mosque that it is atheism. Many are told they must choose. Some leave faith over it; others avoid science.

### Short answer
The question is narrower than it looks. The Quran says nothing about how plants and animals came to be in their present forms; its creation verses about humans concern Adam. Shoaib Ahmed Malik (*Islam and Evolution*, 2021), applying al-Ghazali's rules of interpretation, sorts Muslim views into four positions — creationism, human exceptionalism, Adamic exceptionalism and "no exceptions" — and concludes that the first three are compatible with scripture and the fourth is not. The real dispute is whether Adam himself had biological parents. Common descent is very well supported by evidence such as the fused human chromosome 2. Population genetics rules out a single founding couple in the recent past, but not, on later analyses, one more than about 500,000 years ago. Traditional scholarly answers are cautious: one read (SeekersGuidance, 2020) holds that Adam was certainly created directly, while leaving the evolution of other species open.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the student caught in the middle
**You do not have to choose between your faith and your biology class on the terms you were given.** Many people have been told that accepting any part of evolution is unbelief, and many others that faith is anti-science. Both are too simple. Learning that serious Muslim scholars hold several positions — and that the real disagreement is about one person, Adam — often brings relief. Study the science honestly, and study the texts honestly; the two questions are different.

#### quran — What the Quran says about Adam's creation
**Adam like Jesus (3:59).** "Indeed, the example of Jesus to Allah is like that of Adam. He created Him from dust; then He said to him, 'Be,' and he was" (3:59), Medinan, in a passage about Jesus (pbuh) (its occasion of revelation has not yet been checked). The comparison is that both came into being in an unusual way, by God's command.

**From clay, shaped and given the spirit (38:71–76).** "[So mention] when your Lord said to the angels, 'Indeed, I am going to create a human being from clay. So when I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My [created] soul, then fall down to him in prostration'" (38:71–72), Meccan. God asks Iblis: "what prevented you from prostrating to that which I created with My hands?" Iblis answers: "I am better than him. You created me from fire and created him from clay" (38:75–76). The parallel passage in 15:28–29 has the same order: proportioned, then "breathed into him of My [created] soul" (15:29).

**A successor, taught the names (2:30–31).** "Indeed, I will make upon the earth a successive authority" (2:30); "And He taught Adam the names - all of them" (2:31), Medinan.

**One soul (4:1).** "O mankind, fear your Lord, who created you from one soul and created from it its mate and dispersed from both of them many men and women" (4:1), Medinan.

**The human line (23:12).** "And certainly did We create man from an extract of clay" (23:12), Meccan, followed by the stages of each person's development in the womb (see S4).

**What the verses say and do not say.** They describe Adam as made from dust or clay, proportioned, given the spirit, honoured before the angels, and as the origin of "many men and women". They say nothing about other species, about how long it took, or about whether other human-like beings existed.

#### scholars — Malik's four positions
Malik's book maps Muslim positions on whether humans share common ancestry with other species (Arrozy's review, p. 111). In plain words:

1. **Creationism.** Rejects evolution, including for other species. (Bigliardi describes this end of the range as "crude creationism".)
2. **Human exceptionalism.** Accepts evolution for other living things, but "humanity started with the miraculous creation of Adam and Eve" (Bigliardi): no human has non-human ancestors. *Reading of the Adam verses:* literal.
3. **Adamic exceptionalism.** Developed by David Solomon Jalajel; Bigliardi describes Malik as praising it. "Adam and Eve were created miraculously, yet in such a way that their makeup and appearance did not differ from humans that hitherto evolved" (Bigliardi). Other human-like beings may have existed and evolved; Adam and Eve were created directly; their descendants may have mixed with others. *Reading of the Adam verses:* literal for Adam; the Quran is silent on others.
4. **No exceptions.** "Total acceptance of evolution (including for humans)" (Bigliardi): Adam too had biological ancestors. *Reading of the Adam verses:* non-literal or symbolic.

**Al-Ghazali's rule, and how Malik applies it.** "Chapter 9 outlines Al-Ghazālī's hermeneutics principles" (Arrozy). Bigliardi describes them as several "layers" of reading, starting from "acceptance at face value"; metaphorical readings, he reports, "may be maintained, yet in conjunction with the reading of Adam as a real individual" (citing p. 308). Applying these principles, Malik concludes that they "would agree with creationism, human exceptionalism, and Adamic exceptionalism", but not with "no exceptions" (Arrozy, citing p. 330), because its metaphorical reading of Adam's story "seems to be dominantly influenced by scientific measures" (p. 306). The exact wording of al-Ghazali's rule has not yet been consulted.

**Ashʿari occasionalism, explained.** *Occasionalism* is the view that God is the direct cause of every event, and what we call natural causes are only the occasions on which He acts. Al-Ghazali's famous example: "the cotton is burned directly by God rather than by the fire" (*Wikipedia*); the regular order of nature expresses God's will, not a necessity in things. Arrozy summarises: "The Ash'arite theology, i.e. occasionalism" holds that God is "the primary cause of everything" and "is not bound by the law of nature", and the four positions "could align well with the Ash'arite theology". From this, this page draws its own point (not attributed to Malik): "unguided" evolution is a metaphysical claim, not a biological one, since on occasionalism every mutation and every selection is God's act.

**Not every claimed precedent is real.** Arrozy reports Malik's caution against reading Darwin back into classical authors such as Ibn Khaldūn, Rūmī and al-Jāḥiẓ. A Turkish example of the same caution: Süleyman Ateş claimed in 1975 that the commentator Elmalılı Hamdi Yazır accepted the evolution of Adam; Gördük (2019) argues that Ateş quoted selectively and that Elmalılı held Adam was created as "kusursuz bir ilk insan" — a flawless first human.

#### science — What the evidence shows
**Common descent.** [Established] One clear example: humans have 23 pairs of chromosomes, while "all other extant members of Hominidae have twenty-four pairs". "Human chromosome 2 is a result of an end-to-end fusion of two ancestral chromosomes": it carries "remnants of a second centromere" and "additional telomere sequences in the q13 band, far from either end of the chromosome" — telomeres normally sit only at chromosome ends — "the relic of an ancient telomere-telomere fusion" (*Wikipedia*, citing Ijdo et al. 1991 and others). Chimpanzees have "nearly identical DNA sequences to human chromosome 2, but they are found in two separate chromosomes". This is one line of evidence among many (fossils, anatomy, shared genetic "mistakes"), which this page does not survey.

**A single founding couple: the numbers.** [Debated]
- Estimates of past human population size put it at "never fewer than around 10,000 people" (Craig's summary of the critics). Craig points out that these are averages over long periods, not minimums.
- Joshua Swamidass dated the most recent time a single couple could be the ancestors of everyone (TMR4A) to 495 ± 100 thousand years ago; Richard Buggs agreed this was the first real test. Swamidass later said "a bottleneck anytime before 700 kya is undetectable in genetic data", while "the dust has yet to settle".
- So a founding pair in the last few hundred thousand years is ruled out by the genetics; a pair further back, or a pair whose descendants mixed with others (the "genealogical" view), is not (Craig, *Peaceful Science*, 2020).

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Population genetics rules out Adam and Eve."** *Reply:* it rules out a sole founding couple of all humans in the recent past. Adamic exceptionalism answers that other human-like beings could have coexisted with Adam, and that descent from Adam need not mean descent from Adam alone. *Limit:* Bigliardi asks "whether Adamic exceptionalism fully lives up to the promise of harmonizing Islamic faith with a scientific outlook".

**"Accepting miracles is unscientific."** *Reply:* Malik, following al-Ghazali, holds that God can act outside the usual order; science describes regularities, it does not prove that nothing else can happen. *Limit:* Bigliardi calls accepting miracles "a major concession".

**"Why not simply read Adam's story as symbolic?"** *Reply:* on Malik's reading of al-Ghazali, metaphorical readings are allowed only alongside "the reading of Adam as a real individual" (Bigliardi, citing p. 308); a reading driven "dominantly" by science is not (Arrozy, p. 306). *Limit:* Bigliardi himself asks of a metaphorical reading, "wouldn't this be a capitulation to science?" — and doubts the alternative fully succeeds. The disagreement is about method.

**"Evolution means no God."** *Reply:* "unguided" is a philosophical addition, not a finding of biology; for an Ashʿari, every natural process is God's act. *Limit:* the critic says that if God acts through the same processes that would occur without Him, His action is not detectable — which is true, and is also what occasionalism says.

#### views — What Muslims and institutions actually believe
- **Popular opinion varies widely.** In Pew's 2013 survey of Muslims, the share saying humans and other living things evolved over time ranged from 79% in Kazakhstan and 78% in Lebanon down to 30% in Pakistan, among the figures the report states. "In just four countries" — Iraq (67%), Afghanistan (62%), Indonesia (55%) and Tajikistan (55%) — did at least half say humans have always existed in their present form; in Turkey this was the most common answer (49%) (Pew; survey years and samples are in the report's methodology, not yet consulted).
- **A traditional scholarly answer.** A SeekersGuidance answer, approved by Faraz Rabbani, says: "We are certain that Adam… did not evolve", that his direct creation "is clearly established", while "the process of creation for anything other than humans is not addressed in the Qur'an" (2020). This is human exceptionalism.
- **Academic theologians.** Malik and Jalajel defend Adamic exceptionalism as compatible;
- **"No exceptions."** Judged incompatible with scripture by Malik; its Muslim holders were not identified in the sources read.

### Questions readers ask next
**What exactly does each of the four positions say?** Creationism: no evolution. Human exceptionalism: evolution for all life except humans, who begin with Adam and Eve. Adamic exceptionalism: other human-like beings may have evolved, but Adam and Eve were created directly. No exceptions: Adam too had ancestors.

**If humans evolved, how do I read the verses saying Adam was made from clay?** On Adamic exceptionalism, you read them as they are: Adam was made from clay and given the spirit; the Quran is silent on other beings. On "no exceptions", they are read symbolically — which Malik, following al-Ghazali, judges not acceptable.

**Do I have to believe Adam had no parents to be a Muslim?** Malik's analysis treats Adam as "a real individual" created miraculously (Bigliardi), and the SeekersGuidance answer read here calls his direct creation certain. Other institutions have not yet been checked. A Muslim can accept evolution for other species, and on Adamic exceptionalism even for other human-like beings, while holding this.

**What do "human exceptionalism" and "Adamic exceptionalism" mean?** Human exceptionalism: all humans descend only from Adam and Eve, who were created directly. Adamic exceptionalism: only Adam and Eve are the exception; other humans may have evolved.

**What is "Ashʿari occasionalism"?** The view that God directly causes every event; natural causes are the occasions of His action. Al-Ghazali: the cotton burns by God's act, not by the fire.

**What do most Muslims and Muslim institutions actually believe?** Opinion varies a great deal by country (Pew, 2013). The one scholarly answer read (SeekersGuidance) holds that Adam was created directly, while leaving the evolution of other species open; Diyanet, al-Azhar and fatwa councils have not yet been checked.

### Honest limits
Malik's book itself, Jalajel's work and Guessoum's book were read only through reviews; al-Ghazali's *Qānūn al-Taʾwīl* and *Fayṣal al-Tafriqa* have not yet been consulted, so his rule is described only in the reviewers' words. The survey of evidence for common descent here gives one example; it is not a full account. Institutional positions (Diyanet, al-Azhar, fatwa councils) beyond the one answer read have not yet been checked.

## Q17 — Who created God?

### The question, at its strongest
"If everything needs a cause, then God needs one too. If you say God doesn't, you are making an exception just for Him — special pleading. And if something can exist without a cause, why not the universe itself? It is simpler to stop at the universe than to add a God who explains nothing about how He exists. As Bertrand Russell said to Copleston, the universe may simply be 'without explanation'. And Dawkins adds that any designer would have to be more complex than what it designs, so it would need explaining even more."

### Why people ask it
It is often a child's first philosophical question, and it comes back in adulthood as a test: is faith afraid of thinking? Sometimes it is not a question at all but an intrusive thought that will not go away — the very whisper the Prophet (pbuh) foresaw.

### Short answer
The classical arguments do not say "everything has a cause". They say that whatever *begins to exist*, or whatever *could fail to exist*, needs a cause outside itself. A being that is *necessary* — that could not fail to exist and never began — is not the kind of thing that can be caused. Asking "who created the uncreated?" is like asking who drew the corners of a circle. So the regress objection misses its target. The real question is the next one: why stop at God rather than at the universe? Here the arguments are strong but not decisive. The universe looks like a thing that could have been otherwise, and current cosmology fits a universe with a beginning, though physics has not settled this. Even if the arguments succeed, they reach a necessary first cause; showing that this cause is the one, personal God of the Quran takes further steps. Among professional philosophers, about 19% accept or lean towards theism (PhilPapers 2020). The question is a serious one, and it is fine to think it through. If it comes as a compulsive, frightening loop, that is a different matter (see below).

### The full answer

#### heart — If the question keeps intruding
**The Prophet (pbuh) knew this thought would come, and did not call the person who has it a disbeliever.** "Satan comes to one of you and says, 'Who created so-and-so?' 'till he says, 'Who has created your Lord?' So, when he inspires such a question, one should seek refuge with Allah and give up such thoughts" ({hadith:bukhari:3276}, narrated by Abu Hurayra (ra)). In another report: "Men will continue to question one another till this is propounded: Allah created all things but who created Allah? He who found himself confronted with such a situation should say: I affirm my faith in Allah" ({hadith:muslim:134a}).

**Being troubled by a thought is a sign of faith, not its loss.** When Companions told the Prophet (pbuh), "Verily we perceive in our minds that which every one of us considers it too grave to express", he asked, "Do you really perceive it?" and said: "That is the faith manifest" ({hadith:muslim:132a}). The distress is the faith reacting.

**Is it allowed to keep thinking about it?** In this page's reading (no commentator on the hadith was read), the hadith address the whisper — the thought that comes as an attack and a loop — not philosophy: Muslim scholars from al-Ghazali to Mulla Sadra wrote at length on exactly this question (below). A useful test: if thinking it through brings understanding, it is inquiry. If it brings only fear, repetition and a need to check again and again, it is the whisper, and the advice is to stop engaging it.

> **If it is OCD.** Unwanted, intrusive religious thoughts that "trigger intensely distressing feelings", and fears such as "Fear of committing blasphemy, or offending/angering God", can be scrupulosity, a form of OCD (International OCD Foundation). It responds to treatment: cognitive behaviour therapy with exposure and response prevention, sometimes with medication, and sometimes with help from a faith leader. See iocdf.org, or findahelpline.com for free support lines in many countries.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**"Created by nothing?" (52:35).** "Or were they created by nothing, or were they the creators [of themselves]?" (52:35), Meccan, in a series of questions put to the Quraysh. Ibn Kathir: "Allah asks them, were they created without a maker or did they create themselves? Neither is true." Al-Jalālayn: "It makes no sense for a created thing to have no creator." Ibn Kathir also reports that Jubayr ibn Mutʿim, hearing the Prophet (pbuh) recite this sura in the sunset prayer before he became Muslim, said: "I felt my heart would fly away" (Ibn Kathir, citing al-Bukhari and Muslim).

**The verse's logic.** It lists the options for anything that came into existence: from nothing, from itself, or from another. The first two are rejected as absurd; the third is left for the listener to draw. The argument applies to what was *created* — to "them".

**God is not of that kind (112).** "Say, 'He is Allah, [who is] One, Allah, the Eternal Refuge. He neither begets nor is born, Nor is there to Him any equivalent'" (112:1–4), Meccan. *Al-Ṣamad*, "the Eternal Refuge", is the one on whom all depend, who depends on none. "He neither begets nor is born" denies that God has an origin.

#### scholars — What the Muslim philosophers and theologians argued
**Three key terms, in everyday words.**
- **Contingent** (*mumkin*): something that exists but could have not existed. You, this table, the Sun. Its existence is "borrowed": it needs something else to explain why it exists.
- **Necessary** (*wājib al-wujūd*, "necessary of existence"): something whose existence is not borrowed — it could not fail to exist. It needs no cause, not because it is an exception, but because "needing a cause" is what being contingent means.
- **Begins** (*ḥudūth*, "origination"): coming into existence after not existing. Whatever begins is contingent.

**The kalām argument (al-Ghazali).** *Kalām* is Islamic rational theology. Al-Ghazali's version, in *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*: "Every being which begins has a cause for its beginning; now the world is a being which begins" (*Wikipedia*'s translation). He also argued that a beginningless universe and an actual infinity of past events are absurd. This is the origin of what is now called the "kalām cosmological argument", whose modern form is: "Everything that begins to exist has a cause"; "The universe began to exist"; so the universe has a cause (*Wikipedia*).

**Ibn Sina's "proof of the truthful" (*burhān al-ṣiddīqīn*).** In the fourth part of *al-Ishārāt wa al-tanbīhāt*, and in *al-Najāt* and *al-Shifāʾ*, Ibn Sina argues from contingency, not from a beginning. A contingent thing needs an external cause. Apply this to the whole collection (*jumla*) of contingent things — even if it were infinite: the collection too needs a cause outside itself, and since that cause cannot be contingent, it must be necessary (*Wikipedia*, "Seddiqin argument"). When critics object that a whole need not share its parts' properties, Ibn Sina treats this as a concession: "in a certain way, this is the very thing that is sought". He also argued that a thing's essence (*what* it is) does not explain its existence (*that* it is), so existence must be given to it by a cause (*Wikipedia*, "Cosmological argument").

**Al-Ghazali against the philosophers — and Ibn Rushd against al-Ghazali.** Al-Ghazali accepted a creator but held that the philosophers (above all Ibn Sina) could not *demonstrate* their metaphysics with the logic they used elsewhere; three of their theses he judged unbelief, the first being "The theory of a pre-eternal world" (*Wikipedia*). Ibn Rushd's *Incoherence of the Incoherence* "defends the doctrines of the philosophers and criticizes al-Ghazali's own arguments" (*Wikipedia*). They agreed that God exists; they disagreed on whether the world had a beginning and how God's causation works.

**Mulla Sadra's version.** Mulla Sadra, in *al-Ḥikma al-mutaʿāliya* (the *Asfār*), starts not from contingent things but from existence itself. He treats existence as one reality that differs among its instances "only in perfection and imperfection and in intensity and weakness", and calls the Necessary Existent "Pure Existence than which nothing is more complete" (*Wikipedia*, "Seddiqin argument"). The idea: existence, followed to its most complete degree, is God.

**Divine simplicity, in plain words.** Classical theism holds that God has no parts — not physical parts, and not even a distinction between what He is and that He is. Something made of parts depends on its parts and on whatever put them together, so it would be contingent. That is why, on this view, a necessary being must be simple. This is also the reply to Dawkins: the argument is not for a complex designer who would need explaining, but for a simple ground of existence.

#### reasoning — The strongest counter: why not a necessary or brute-fact universe?
**The objection in full.** Hume and Russell press two related points.
- *The universe could be the necessary thing.* If something must exist without a cause, why not the universe (or its basic stuff, or its laws)? Adding God seems to add a second mystery.
- *Or nothing needs to be necessary.* In the 1948 debate, Copleston argued that the totality of things needs "a being which contains within itself the reason for its own existence". Russell refused the premise: "I think it is without explanation", and called "necessary" "a useless word, except as applied to analytic propositions, not to things" (transcript). On this view the universe is a *brute fact*: it just is.
- *The parts-and-whole point.* The "Hume–Edwards principle" says: "If the existence of every member of a set is explained, the existence of that set is thereby explained" (*Wikipedia*). If each thing in the universe is explained by earlier things, nothing more is needed for the whole.

**The reply, as an argument.**
1. *What a necessary thing would have to be like.* A necessary being could not have been otherwise; whatever properties it has, it has necessarily. But the universe looks exactly like something that could have been otherwise: its amount of matter, its laws and constants, even its existence seem to be one way among many possible ways; as far as anyone knows, none of them is required by logic. A thing whose features could have been different is, by definition, contingent. So the universe is a poor candidate for the necessary being.
2. *Why a brute fact is a costly answer.* We never accept "it is just there" for anything inside the universe; we look for causes. To stop at the universe is to make one exception — the very special pleading the objection charged against theism — at the point where the question is largest.
3. *Against the Hume–Edwards principle.* Explaining each member of an infinite chain by an earlier member never explains why there is a chain at all, rather than none. Ibn Sina's point was that the whole collection is still contingent and so still needs a cause.

*Limit:* each step can be resisted. One can deny that there must be any explanation for the existence of things (Russell's position), or that our sense that the universe "could have been otherwise" tracks real possibility. The argument then rests on the principle that existence needs explaining — a principle many find compelling and some reject.

#### science — What cosmology does and does not show
**The Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem (2003).** It shows that a universe that has, on average, been expanding "cannot be infinite in the past but must have a past spacetime boundary" (*Wikipedia*). [Established, within its assumptions]

**Its assumptions and limits.** [Debated]
- It applies to classical spacetime; it "does not allow to tell when the singularity takes place", and "does not tell if it is associated to any other event in the past" (*Wikipedia*).
- Sean Carroll argues it "may not hold under consideration of a complete theory of quantum gravity"; Lesnefsky, Easson and Davies have built classical models that satisfy its condition yet are past-complete (*Wikipedia*).
- Its authors disagree on what it means: Vilenkin thinks the universe had a beginning; Guth, according to Carroll, does not (*Wikipedia*).

**The quantum-causation challenge.** Critics such as Paul Davies doubt that cause and effect can be defined consistently in fundamental physics; Quentin Smith cites virtual particles as uncaused; Lawrence Krauss proposes that quantum fluctuations could produce a universe. William Lane Craig replies that such events still have "many physically necessary conditions" (*Wikipedia*, "Kalam cosmological argument"). *Limit:* this debate is unresolved; and Vilenkin himself, replying to Craig in 2015, said of the universe's origin, "No cause is needed" (*Wikipedia*, "Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem").

**The upshot.** Cosmology fits a beginning but does not prove one. The kalām argument gains support from it; the contingency argument (Ibn Sina's) does not depend on it at all, since it works even for a beginningless universe.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Special pleading: you exempt God from the rule."** *Reply:* the rule was never "everything has a cause". It is "whatever begins, or could fail to exist, has a cause". God, as defined, does neither. *Limit:* the critic can ask why anything should be necessary at all; that is the real debate (above).

**"A designer must be more complex than its design" (Dawkins).** *Reply:* the classical arguments are not design arguments, and classical theism holds that God is simple, not a complex mechanism. *Limit:* critics find divine simplicity itself hard to make sense of — how can a simple being know and will many things?

**"Even if there is a necessary being, why the God of the Quran?"** *Limit:* these steps are weaker than the first. Michael Martin argues the kalām argument shows at most that some agent or agents caused the universe (*Wikipedia*).

**"Is this argument specific to Islam?"** *Reply:* no. *Wikipedia* traces the kalām argument to John Philoponus (6th century, a Christian) and to medieval Islamic kalām, including al-Ghazali; its best-known modern defender is the Christian philosopher William Lane Craig, and the modern name "kalām cosmological argument" keeps its Islamic source. In the 1948 debate, Copleston argued from contingency in much the way Ibn Sina did. It is a shared argument of monotheism.

#### views — The range of views
- **Kalām theologians (al-Ashʿari, al-Maturidi, al-Ghazali)** — the world began; whatever begins has a cause.
- **Ibn Sina and the philosophers** — even a beginningless world needs a necessary cause.
- **Ibn Rushd** — defended the philosophers against al-Ghazali.
- **Mulla Sadra** — the argument from existence itself.
- **Hume, Russell and many modern philosophers** — the universe may be a brute fact; "necessary being" may be meaningless.
- **Professional philosophers today** — 18.93% accept or lean towards theism, 66.95% towards atheism, 14.63% other (PhilPapers Survey 2020, N = 1,770).

### Questions readers ask next
**Why can't the universe itself be the necessary, uncaused thing?** Because a necessary thing could not have been otherwise, while the universe — its laws, constants and contents — looks like something that could have been different. See "The strongest counter" for the full argument and its limit.

**What does "necessary being" mean, and why can't it have a cause?** A being that could not fail to exist. A cause is what explains why something exists rather than not; a necessary being has no "rather than not", so there is nothing for a cause to do.

**If the hadith says seek refuge from this question, am I allowed to keep thinking about it?** Yes, as inquiry; the scholars did. The hadith address the whisper that comes as an attack and a loop. If it brings understanding, keep going; if it brings only fear and compulsion, stop engaging it and seek help.

**What do "contingent" and "necessary" mean, in everyday words?** Contingent: could have not existed (a person, a mountain). Necessary: could not have not existed.

**What are *kalām*, *ḥudūth* and *wājib al-wujūd*?** *Kalām*: Islamic rational theology. *Ḥudūth*: the beginning of something's existence. *Wājib al-wujūd*: the being whose existence is necessary.

**Is this argument specific to Islam, or the same one Christian philosophers use?** It is shared: its best-known modern form, the "kalām cosmological argument", keeps the name of its Islamic source, and its best-known modern defender, William Lane Craig, is a Christian philosopher.

### Honest limits
The regress objection fails against the classical arguments, but the arguments themselves are respected rather than decisive: most professional philosophers are not persuaded. Getting from a necessary being to the one personal God takes further, weaker steps. Ibn Sina, al-Ghazali, Ibn Rushd and Mulla Sadra were read here only through encyclopedia summaries, not in their own texts; Hume's *Dialogues* and al-Ghazali's *al-Iqtiṣād* have not yet been consulted; Ṭabāṭabāʾī's version was not covered. The explanation of divine simplicity is a summary of the classical view; no source on it was read.

### The question, at its strongest
"Everything we know about the mind says it depends on the brain. Damage the brain and memory goes, personality changes, consciousness switches off under anaesthesia. When the brain dies, what could be left? Near-death experiences look like what a starved, dying brain would produce. And the moral argument — 'justice requires another life' — is a wish, not evidence: the universe is not obliged to be fair."

### Why people ask it
Usually because someone they love has died. Sometimes because of injustice: the cruel prosper, the innocent suffer, and nothing in this life sets it right. Sometimes because science classes make the soul sound like an old superstition.

### Short answer
There is no proof of an afterlife that would satisfy a sceptic, and this page does not claim one. There are reasons, of different kinds. The Quran argues that the One who created you the first time can create you again (36:78–79), and that a world in which the righteous and the corrupt end the same would be pointless (38:28). Philosophers note that no one has explained why brain activity is accompanied by experience at all — the "hard problem" — so the dependence of mind on brain, though real, does not show that the mind *is* nothing but the brain. Kant argued that morality itself points beyond this life. Near-death research is intriguing — in AWARE II, 6 of 28 interviewed survivors (21%) reported a "transcendent recalled experience of death", and normal EEG activity consistent with consciousness appeared as late as 35–60 minutes into resuscitation — but no one has yet identified a hidden visual target, and a dying-brain explanation remains open. Islam teaches that after death comes the *barzakh* (an intermediate state), then bodily resurrection, judgement, and reunion.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the one who is grieving
**Your grief is a measure of your love, and it does not mean your faith is weak.** The question "Is there anything after?" is often not abstract; it is about one person. The Quran's answer is not only an argument. It is a promise that the person you lost is not lost to God, and that justice and mercy have the last word.

**What helps.** Grief comes in waves, and there is no correct timetable. Talk about the person; pray for them; let others carry some of the weight. If, months later, grief still stops you from living, or you feel you cannot go on, please talk to a doctor or counsellor. That is care, not a lack of trust in God.

> **Where to find help.** Find A Helpline (findahelpline.com) lists "free, anonymous and confidential" support lines in many countries, including for grief and loss. If you are in danger, contact your local emergency number.

#### quran — The Quran's arguments for resurrection
**1. From the first creation (36:78–79).** "And he presents for Us an example and forgets his [own] creation. He says, 'Who will give life to bones while they are disintegrated?' Say, 'He will give them life who produced them the first time; and He is, of all creation, Knowing'" (36:78–79), Meccan. *Occasion:* Mujahid, ʿIkrima and others say "Ubayy bin Khalaf… came to the Messenger of Allah… with a dry bone in his hand"; Ibn ʿAbbas names "Al-'As bin Wa'il", who "took a bone from the bed of a valley and crumbled it in his hand"; Ibn Kathir concludes that the verses "apply to all those who deny the resurrection after death". *The argument spelled out:* you exist now, made from what was not you; making something the first time is not easier than remaking it. Ibn Kathir: "Man forgets about himself, that Allah created him from nothing and brought him into existence."

**2. From fire in the green tree (36:80).** "[It is] He who made for you from the green tree, fire, and then from it you ignite" (36:80). Qatada, quoted by Ibn Kathir: "the One Who brought forth this fire from this tree is able to resurrect him." The point: God brings opposites out of each other — fire from moist wood, life from dry bones.

**3. From precision (75:3–4).** "Does man think that We will not assemble his bones? Yes. [We are] Able [even] to proportion his fingertips" (75:3–4), Meccan.

**4. From justice and purpose (38:28).** "Or should we treat those who believe and do righteous deeds like corrupters in the land? Or should We treat those who fear Allah like the wicked?" (38:28), Meccan, after 38:27, which Ibn Kathir's translator renders: "And We created not the heaven and the earth and all that is between them without purpose!" (not Saheeh; 38:27 was not available in verified data). Ibn Kathir: "There must inevitably be another realm in which those who obey Allah will be rewarded and the wicked will be punished", since in this world the wicked often prosper. Al-Jalālayn says the verse answered Meccan disbelievers who claimed they would fare as well as the believers in the Hereafter.

#### sunnah — What happens after death, in Islamic teaching
**The barzakh.** When a dying person asks to be sent back, the answer is: "No! It is only a word he is saying; and behind them is a barrier until the Day they are resurrected" (23:100). *Barzakh* ("barrier") is the name for the state between death and resurrection.

**The grave.** Anas (ra) reports the Prophet (pbuh): "When a human being is laid in his grave and his companions return and he even hears their foot steps", "two angels come to him and make him sit and ask him", "What did you use to say about this man, Muhammad?" The believer answers, "I testify that he is Allah's slave and His Apostle", and is told: "Look at your place in the Hell-Fire. Allah has given you a place in Paradise instead of it." The one who says "I do not know, but I used to say what the people used to say!" fares otherwise ({hadith:bukhari:1338}).

**Resurrection, judgement, reunion.** The Quran and Sunna teach a bodily resurrection on the Last Day, a judgement in which each person sees their deeds, and then Paradise or Hell.

**The soul.** "And they ask you, [O Muhammad], about the soul. Say, 'The soul is of the affair of my Lord. And mankind have not been given of knowledge except a little'" (17:85). And: "Allah takes the souls at the time of their death, and those that do not die [He takes] during their sleep" (39:42). The Quran treats the soul as real and as God's to take and return, while saying little about its nature.

#### scholars — What Muslim scholars argued
**Al-Ghazali: bodily resurrection.** In *The Incoherence of the Philosophers*, al-Ghazali counted as unbelief the philosophers' thesis that "Bodily resurrection will not take place in the hereafter" (*Wikipedia*). His target was above all Ibn Sina. In the *Tahāfut*, to refute the philosophers on their own premises, he argued that even if the soul survives, God could return it to a body, "ister bu beden insan bedeninin aynısı isterse de gayrısı olsun" — whether the same human body or another. But in *al-Iqtiṣād fī al-iʿtiqād* he says that this concession "bizim inancımıza uygun değildir" — "is not in accordance with our belief" — because that book was written to refute, not to prove (Yılmaz's Turkish rendering). Yılmaz judges that al-Ghazali stayed deliberately ambiguous about the soul's nature. For him, bodily resurrection is a matter "vahiyle vurgulanmış ve gerçekleşmesi imkânsız olmayan" — stressed by revelation and not impossible (Yılmaz). His point against the philosophers was that they had not *proved* it impossible.

**Nursi: twelve "gates".** In the Tenth Word (*The Resurrection and the Hereafter*), Said Nursi argues from God's names. The "SECOND TRUTH: The gate of Munificence and Mercy" rests on an observation, which the "THIRD TRUTH: The gate of Wisdom and Justice" develops: "This world's oppressors die with their oppressive power intact, while the oppressed die still subjected to humiliation." A just and merciful God would not leave it there. In the "Ninth Truth", spring is a daily sample of resurrection: "Each spring we see countless animal and plant species assembled in a few days and then scattered" ("Twelve Truths"). The Tenth Word opens with the verse "Look, then, to the signs of God's mercy - how He restores life to the earth after its death" (tr. Algar).

**Mutahhari.** His work has not yet been consulted.

#### reasoning — The mind–brain objection, answered
**What the objection gets right.** The mind depends on the brain in this life: injuries, drugs and illness change memory, mood and personality. Any honest answer must accept this.

**What it does not show.** Dependence is not identity. The "hard problem" is "the problem of explaining why any physical state is conscious rather than nonconscious": after a complete account of what the brain does, one can still ask, with Chalmers, "Why is the performance of these functions accompanied by experience?" Joseph Levine "argues that there is a special 'explanatory gap' between consciousness and the physical" (IEP). Philosophers divide: some say consciousness will be explained physically; some (dualists) that it is distinct from the physical; some that it is a basic feature of reality. The IEP article itself does not discuss survival after death — the hard problem does not prove the mind survives; it shows that "the mind is just the brain" is not established either.

**An analogy, with its limit.** A radio's sound changes when the radio is damaged; that does not by itself show whether the sound is made in the radio or received by it. (This analogy is often used in these debates; no named source for it was read.) *Limit:* it is only an analogy; it shows that the evidence of dependence is compatible with survival, not that survival is true.

**The Islamic picture is not only "a soul floating free".** Islam teaches bodily resurrection: what continues is the person, whom God raises with a body. The afterlife, on this teaching, does not rest on the soul being independent of all bodies.

**Kant's moral argument, in a few sentences.** Kant held that morality commands us to pursue the "highest good": a world in which happiness is "in conformity with the purest morality", proportioned to "the worthiness of a subject". We cannot see how that is possible in nature alone, so practical reason "postulates" God, who can bring virtue and happiness together, and a future life in which this is realised. Kant insisted this is not a theoretical proof: "I must not even say 'It is morally certain that there is a God', but rather 'I am morally certain'" (SEP). *Limit:* critics answer that the demand for cosmic justice may simply go unmet.

**Is there any reason beyond "justice seems to require it"?** Yes, three, of different strengths: the argument from the first creation (if you exist once, you can exist again), the unsolved hard problem (which leaves room for more than the brain), and — for believers — trust in the One who promised it. None is a proof; together they make the hope reasonable rather than wishful.

#### science — Near-death research
**AWARE I (2014).** Of 2,060 cardiac arrests, 140 survivors completed a first interview and 101 a second; 46% had memories, 9% near-death experiences (NDEs), and 2% explicit awareness of "'seeing' and 'hearing' actual events" related to resuscitation. "One had a verifiable period of conscious awareness during which time cerebral function was not expected" (Parnia et al., *Resuscitation* 85, 2014). [Moderate, for the frequency; Debated, for interpretation]

**AWARE II (2023).** Of 567 in-hospital cardiac arrests at 25 sites, 53 patients survived (9.3%) and 28 were interviewed (52.8%). Of these 28, 11 (39.3%) reported memories or perceptions suggesting consciousness: CPR-induced consciousness 2/28 (7.1%), post-resuscitation 2/28 (7.1%), dream-like experiences 3/28 (10.7%), and a "transcendent recalled experience of death" 6/28 (21.4%). Nobody identified the hidden visual image; 1/28 (3.5%) identified the auditory stimulus. Despite severe lack of oxygen (mean rSO₂ 43%), in monitored patients, normal EEG activity consistent with consciousness appeared as late as 35–60 minutes into CPR (the abstract gives no count of patients with EEG). The authors conclude that "Consciousness. awareness and cognitive processes may occur during CA" (cardiac arrest) (Parnia et al., *Resuscitation* 191, 2023). *Denominators matter:* the 21% is of 28 interviewed survivors, not of 567 patients.

**The dying-brain account.** In a University of Michigan study (Xu, Borjigin et al., *PNAS*, 2023), four comatose patients were monitored by EEG as life support was withdrawn; in two of the four, gamma-wave activity surged in the junction of the temporal, parietal and occipital lobes, an area linked in other studies to dreaming and altered states. The researchers stress the small sample: "it's impossible to know in this study what the patients experienced because they did not survive." This supports the idea that a dying brain can produce intense activity, which could underlie NDEs. [Mixed]

**What the research shows.** It shows that experiences during cardiac arrest are more common, and brain activity more persistent, than once thought. It does not show that the mind leaves the body: the key test — identifying a hidden image — has not been passed.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"The mind depends on the brain, so it dies with the brain."** *Reply:* dependence in this life does not establish identity, and the hard problem remains unsolved; in any case, Islam's hope rests on resurrection by God, not on the brain. *Limit:* to someone who is a physicalist, the reply will look like an appeal to a gap.

**"NDEs are just the dying brain."** *Reply:* that may be so; the page does not rest the case for an afterlife on NDEs. *Limit:* NDEs neither prove nor disprove an afterlife.

**"Justice is a wish, not evidence."** *Reply:* Kant's point is that we cannot act morally without treating justice as real; the Quran's point is that a purposeless creation would contradict its Creator (38:27–28). *Limit:* both arguments assume something — the authority of morality, or a purposeful Creator — that the sceptic may deny.

#### views — The range of views
- **Islamic teaching** — barzakh, bodily resurrection, judgement, Paradise and Hell.
- **Ibn Sina (as al-Ghazali read him)** — the soul survives; bodily resurrection rejected — counted unbelief by al-Ghazali.
- **Al-Ghazali** — bodily resurrection is "stressed by revelation and not impossible"; the philosophers did not prove otherwise.
- **Kant** — immortality is a postulate of practical reason, not a theoretical proof.
- **Physicalist philosophers** — the mind ends with the brain.
- **NDE researchers** — divided between those who see evidence of consciousness beyond the brain and those who see a dying-brain process.

### Questions readers ask next
**If brain damage changes personality, what survives death?** In this life, the person's mind works through the brain, and damage changes how it shows itself. Islamic teaching is that the person — the soul, which God takes at death (39:42) — continues in the barzakh and is re-embodied at the resurrection. Whether the hard problem points to a non-physical mind is debated.

**What is the Quran's argument from the first creation, spelled out?** You exist, made from what was not you. The One who did that can do it again: "He will give them life who produced them the first time" (36:79).

**Is there any reason to believe beyond "justice seems to require it"?** Yes: the argument from first creation, the unsolved hard problem of consciousness, and trust in the promise. None is a proof.

**What exactly do Muslims believe happens after death?** The questioning in the grave ({hadith:bukhari:1338}); the barzakh until the Day of Resurrection (23:100); bodily resurrection; judgement; Paradise or Hell.

**What does "bodily resurrection" mean, and what did al-Ghazali argue?** That the dead will be raised with bodies, not only as souls. Al-Ghazali held that bodily resurrection is taught by revelation and that the philosophers had not shown it impossible. His argument that the soul might return to "the same human body or another" was a concession for argument's sake, which he said "is not in accordance with our belief" (Yılmaz).

**What is Kant's moral argument, in a few sentences?** Morality commands us to seek a world where happiness matches goodness; that is not possible in nature alone; so we must postulate God and a future life — as a practical commitment, not a proof.

### Honest limits
None of these arguments is a proof, and the page should not present them as one.
