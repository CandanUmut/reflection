## Q8 — Is doubting a sin? I keep having blasphemous thoughts.

### The question, at its strongest
"A terrible thought about God comes into my head — an insult, a doubt, an image I would never choose. I try to push it away and it comes back stronger. I wash again for prayer because I'm not sure I did it right, and then again. I'm afraid that having these thoughts means I am not really a believer, or that I have already left Islam without meaning to. A real Muslim wouldn't think like this. Would he?"

### Why people ask it
Unwanted, intrusive thoughts are very common, and in religious people they often attach to the things they care about most. Many people suffer in silence for years because they are ashamed to say what the thoughts are, and because they think the thoughts themselves are sins.

### Short answer
Unwanted thoughts are not beliefs, and you are not held to account for them. The Prophet (pbuh) said that God has forgiven his community what occurs in their minds as long as they do not act on it or speak it ({hadith:bukhari:5269}; {hadith:muslim:127a}). When his Companions told him they had thoughts too terrible to say aloud, he called their distress "the faith manifest" ({hadith:muslim:132a}). Jurists taught people troubled by misgivings not to dwell on them, because "certainty is not lifted by a doubt" (Ibn Nujaym, via Rabbani). If the thoughts come in loops that you feel you must neutralise — by repeating, checking or seeking reassurance — this may be scrupulosity, a form of obsessive-compulsive disorder (OCD). It is treatable, and the main treatment fits closely with what the jurists advised. Honest intellectual doubt is different: it is a question to think through, not a thought to push away.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**The fact that the thought horrifies you is the clearest sign of what you actually believe.** A person who has stopped believing is not tormented by a thought that insults God. The torment comes from the love.

**You do not have to argue with the thought, answer it, or prove to yourself that you don't believe it.** Trying to win that argument is what keeps it going. You can let it be there, the way you let a car alarm in the street be there, and carry on with what you were doing.

**You are not alone, and you are not uniquely bad.** Awad cites an international study (Radomsky et al., 2014) estimating that "about 94% of the human population worldwide experience intrusive thoughts"; the study's own sample has not yet been checked. What differs is how much they stick.

**If you are in distress right now:** speak to someone you trust today. In North America, Naseeha (1-866-627-3342) is a confidential Muslim helpline; in the UK, the Muslim Youth Helpline and Samaritans (116 123); in Türkiye, call 112 in an emergency; elsewhere, findahelpline.com lists free services by country. If you ever have thoughts of harming yourself, call your local emergency number.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**God does not burden a soul beyond its capacity.** "Allah does not charge a soul except [with that within] its capacity… Our Lord, do not impose blame upon us if we have forgotten or erred… and burden us not with that which we have no ability to bear" (2:286). This is the closing verse of Sura al-Baqara (Medinan), a prayer the believers are taught to say. A thought that comes against your will is not within your choosing, and the verse says you are charged only with what is.

**The Quran names a "whisperer".** The last sura asks refuge "From the evil of the retreating whisperer — Who whispers [evil] into the breasts of mankind" (114:4–5). The word for whispering is *waswās*. In Muslim belief, some unwanted thoughts are attributed to this whispering (Awad cites 20:120, where Satan whispers to Adam). The verse teaches a response: seeking refuge in God, not arguing with the whisper. Reading it as a description of OCD is a modern application; the verse itself does not describe a mental illness.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Thoughts are forgiven unless acted on or spoken.** The Prophet (pbuh) said: "Allah has forgiven my followers the evil thoughts that occur to their minds, as long as such thoughts are not put into action or uttered" ({hadith:bukhari:5269}, narrated by Abu Hurayra). The narrator Qatada drew a legal conclusion from it: a divorce pronounced only in the mind has no effect. Sahih Muslim has the same teaching: "Verily Allah forgave my people the evil promptings which arise within their hearts as long as they did not speak about them or did not act upon them" ({hadith:muslim:127a}).

**The Companions had these thoughts too.** Abu Hurayra (ra) reports that some Companions came to the Prophet (pbuh) and said: "Verily we perceive in our minds that which every one of us considers it too grave to express." He asked: "Do you really perceive it?" They said yes. He said: "That is the faith manifest" ({hadith:muslim:132a}). The *Companions* (*ṣaḥāba*) are the people who knew the Prophet (pbuh) and believed in him; *Bukhari* and *Muslim* are the two collections of hadith that Sunni Muslims regard as most reliable. The point of this report is that the Companions — the generation Muslims regard as the best — had the very experience you are having, and the Prophet (pbuh) did not treat it as unbelief.

#### fiqh — What the jurists hold
**Certainty is not removed by doubt.** A basic maxim of Islamic law is: "Certainty is not lifted by a doubt." Rabbani attributes it to the Hanafi jurist Ibn Nujaym (d. 1563) in *al-Ashbāh wa-l-Naẓāʾir* and to the Ottoman civil code, the *Majalla*. It means: if you know you made ablution, a doubt about whether you broke it does not count. Rabbani adds that "mere doubts and suppositions are of no legal consequence", and that until you are certain a prayer was invalid, "you do not have any prayers to make up."

**Following misgivings is itself blameworthy.** Rabbani, summarising Ibn ʿAbidin, the leading late Hanafi authority (no work named), writes that following misgivings is "highly blameworthy in the Shariah: it is from the Shaytan." In other words, the law does not reward the extra checking; it discourages it.

**The other schools.** The Maliki, Shafiʿi and Hanbali positions on *waswasa* have not yet been consulted in their own works.

#### science — What research says
**Scrupulosity is a recognised form of OCD.** [Established.] The International OCD Foundation's packet for faith leaders describes scrupulosity as OCD centred on religious or moral concerns: people are "overly concerned that something they thought or did might be a sin." It distinguishes it from a tender conscience: such a person can "find comfort when advised by a religious leader", whereas people with scrupulosity "cannot have their fear or concern resolved by competent explanation."

**How common it is.** [Moderate.] İnözü, Keser and Karancı (2017) summarise Western studies as finding scrupulosity in "between 10.0% and 33.0% of patients diagnosed with OCD" — that is, a percentage of people who already have OCD, not of the general population — and note that "this rate can vary across different cultures".

**Waswasa and OCD.** Awad (Yaqeen) writes: "*Waswasah* is not a Muslim disease — it is a religious presentation of OCD in Muslims", and notes that scrupulosity is "found in all religions".

**Exposure and response prevention (ERP).** [Established as the main psychological treatment.] The IOCDF packet calls ERP the most important form of cognitive behavioural therapy for OCD. *Exposure* means the person "systematically confronts the thoughts, images, objects, and situations that cause anxiety"; *response prevention* means choosing not to perform the compulsion (re-washing, re-praying, asking for reassurance), so that the anxiety falls by itself — "the decrease in anxiety with nothing but the passage of time" (IOCDF packet). Awad calls ERP "compatible with the Islamic approach" and says "the Islamic advice to ignore the obsession and resist the compulsion is the layperson's form of ERP." She also reports that about 25% of people drop out of ERP and a further 5–22% refuse it (Twohig et al., 2010), so it is not easy — which is why it is best done with a therapist.

#### reasoning — Honest doubt or a compulsive thought?
**They feel similar, but they behave differently.** Al-Ghazali's doubt (see "How do I rebuild faith after a crisis of doubt?") was an inquiry: he asked how we know anything, followed the question for two months, and came out with an answer. That kind of doubt is helped by thinking, reading and talking with someone knowledgeable. A compulsive thought is different:
- It comes against your will and feels alien, even disgusting, to you.
- Answering it brings relief for minutes, then it returns, often stronger.
- It fixes on certainty: you cannot rest until you are *sure*, and you never are.
- It drives rituals: repeating ablution or prayer, mentally reviewing, asking others again and again.

**A rule of thumb.** If a good answer from a knowledgeable person settles the question for a long time, it was a question. If no answer ever settles it, and you find yourself asking the same thing again, it is probably the compulsion — and the remedy is not another answer, but less answering. *Limit:* only a qualified clinician can diagnose OCD, and some people have both real questions and compulsive doubts at once.

**"If part of me half-agrees with the thought, is it still unchosen?"** That "half-agreeing" feeling is very common in OCD; the mind asks "what if I actually believe this?", and the question feels like evidence. The hadith set the line at acting on the thought or speaking it, not at how it feels ({hadith:bukhari:5269}). If you are unsure, the jurists' maxim applies: you were a believer with certainty, and a doubt does not remove it.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Calling it OCD is a way to avoid real questions."** *Reply:* the distinction above keeps real questions open: they deserve answers, and this site tries to give them. What it rejects is treating a compulsion as if it were a question. *Limit:* a person may use the OCD label to avoid a real question, and a good therapist or scholar will notice.

**"Satan's whispering is a spiritual matter; therapy is secular."** *Reply:* the Islamic remedy and the clinical one point the same way: don't engage the whisper, don't repeat the act (Rabbani; Awad). Awad argues that a therapist "will likely need to defer to and coordinate with a person of Islamic knowledge", and that the imam should teach principles such as "doubt can not negate certainty" rather than answer repeated questions. *Limit:* not every therapist understands religious clients; look for one who does (Khalil Center in North America, the Muslim Mental Health directory, or ask the IOCDF for providers experienced with scrupulosity).

### Practical steps for the moment it happens
1. **Name it.** "This is a whisper / an intrusive thought. It is not me and it is not my belief."
2. **Say a short refuge and move on.** For example, the words of 114:1, "I seek refuge in the Lord of mankind" — once, not repeatedly.
3. **Do not repeat the act.** If you finished the prayer, it stands unless you are certain it was invalid: until then "you do not have any prayers to make up" (Rabbani). The same principle applies, by illustration, to ablution: if you know you made it, a doubt does not undo it.
4. **Do not ask for reassurance.** Each "are you sure I'm still Muslim?" brings relief and feeds the loop (IOCDF packet).
5. **Let the anxiety rise and fall.** It will fall by itself if you do not feed it.
6. **Choose one teacher.** Bring your questions to one knowledgeable person rather than to many people whenever the urge comes (the IOCDF packet suggests seeking "religious counsel from a single faith leader or confessor").
7. **See a clinician** if this takes up a lot of your day, stops you praying, or makes you dread worship. (Clinicians use a time threshold — more than an hour a day — among the signs of OCD; that criterion has not yet been checked in a source.)

### Questions readers ask next
**What exactly does Muslim 132 say?** Companions said: "we perceive in our minds that which every one of us considers it too grave to express." The Prophet (pbuh) asked, "Do you really perceive it?" and said, "That is the faith manifest."

**How do I tell real doubt from a compulsive thought?** A real question is settled, at least for a while, by a good answer. A compulsion returns no matter what answer you get, and drives you to check and repeat.

**What is waswasa?** Arabic *waswasa*, "whispering" (Turkish *vesvese*). In the Quran it is the whispering of the "retreating whisperer" (114:4–5); in everyday Muslim speech it also means obsessive doubts about worship.

**What is exposure and response prevention?** A therapy in which you face the trigger and deliberately do not perform the compulsion, so the anxiety fades on its own.

**Who are the Companions, and what are Bukhari and Muslim?** The Companions are the people who knew the Prophet (pbuh) and believed in him. Bukhari (d. 870) and Muslim (d. 875) compiled the two most trusted hadith collections.

**Did I leave Islam because of a thought?** No. In the hadith, thoughts are forgiven unless acted on or spoken ({hadith:bukhari:5269}), and a doubt does not remove certainty (Ibn Nujaym).

### Honest limits
A website cannot diagnose OCD. The Maliki, Shafiʿi and Hanbali works on *waswasa* have not yet been consulted here, and the clinical prevalence figures for Muslim and Turkish samples could not be traced. The overlap between the jurists' advice and ERP is a modern observation (Awad), not a claim that classical jurists knew of OCD.

## Q38 — How do I rebuild faith after a crisis of doubt?

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 2:260, 2:286, 94:5–6.
- W. Montgomery Watt (trans.), al-Ghazali, *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal*, in *The Faith and Practice of al-Ghazali* (1953), https://www.ghazali.org/works/watt3.htm — sections I, II and the section on the mystics.
- Sahih Muslim 132a (sunnah.com).
- M. F. Bockrath, K. I. Pargament, S. Wong et al., "Religious and Spiritual Struggles and Their Links to Psychological Adjustment: A Meta-Analysis of Longitudinal Studies", *Psychology of Religion and Spirituality* (online first 29 March 2021), doi 10.1037/rel0000400, https://kennethpargament.com/_files/ugd/bc40fb_9b0350ce9a704b62b5af34acc5d69477.pdf.
- J. Sedlar et al., "Spiritual Struggles among Atheists: Links to Psychological Distress and Well-Being", *Religions* 9 (2018), 242, https://core.ac.uk/download/234762495.pdf — uses the Religious and Spiritual Struggles (RSS) scale of Exline et al. (2014).
- Najwa Awad, "Clinicians, Imams, and the Whisperings of Satan", Yaqeen Institute (2017/2024).
- *Scrupulosity and OCD: Information Packet for Faith Leaders* (IOCDF and others).

### The question, at its strongest
"I used to be sure. Then something broke — an argument I couldn't answer, a tragedy, the behaviour of religious people, or just a slow fading. Now when I pray I feel I'm pretending. People tell me to 'have faith', but faith is exactly what I've lost. I don't want to fake certainty, and I don't want to throw everything away. Is there an honest way back?"

### Why people ask it
Crises of faith are common and often private. The person may be ashamed to tell family or a mosque, and may feel that the only options are pretending or leaving.

### Short answer
Yes, there is an honest way back, and it does not start with recovering your old certainty all at once. It starts with telling the truth about where you are, finding one or two people who can hear your questions without shaming you, keeping small practices without demanding that they feel full, and taking the hardest questions one at a time. Al-Ghazali, one of the greatest Muslim scholars, went through two crises — one of the mind, one of the heart — and came out with a deeper faith; he wrote that his first doubt ended "by a light which God most high cast into my breast", and his second only after he changed how he lived. Research suggests that spiritual struggles predict somewhat more distress over time, on average, so getting support matters. Not everyone returns, and that should be respected.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**You are not a hypocrite for praying while you doubt.** A person who keeps turning toward God while unsure is doing something honest: keeping the door open. Abraham (pbuh), the friend of God, asked to see how God gives life to the dead. Asked, "Have you not believed?", he answered: "Yes, but [I ask] only that my heart may be satisfied" (2:260). Faith and the longing for more certainty can sit together.

**You don't have to solve everything this month.** Faith that comes back is often slower and quieter than the faith that left. That is not weaker; it is often more your own.

**If the crisis comes with hopelessness** — you can't sleep, can't eat, or think life is not worth living — that is a reason to see a doctor or counsellor now, not later. Naseeha (North America, 1-866-627-3342), Samaritans (UK, 116 123), 112 in Türkiye in an emergency, or findahelpline.com elsewhere.

#### scholars — Al-Ghazali's two crises, told as a story
**The first crisis: can I know anything?** Al-Ghazali (d. 1111) was the most famous teacher in Baghdad. In *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal* (*Deliverance from Error*), written late in life, he tells how he began to ask whether anything he believed was certain. He noticed that "Christian youths always grew up to be Christians, Jewish youths to be Jews and Muslim youths to be Muslims", and tested even his senses and reason. He fell into a doubt that "lasted almost two months". It did not end by argument: "but by a light which God most high cast into my breast" (Watt, sections I–II).

**What that "light" meant in practice.** He describes it as the return of trust in the basic truths of reason — the things one cannot prove but must rely on to prove anything. Many people in a crisis find something similar: not a new argument, but the slow return of trust in what they had stopped trusting — that the world makes sense, that goodness is real, that their own mind is not lying to them.

**The second crisis: knowing is not enough.** Years later, with his intellectual questions answered, al-Ghazali realised that his life did not match what he knew. He describes being pulled between two forces: "Worldly desires were striving to keep me by their chains just where I was." The struggle lasted "for nearly six months beginning with Rajab 488 A.H. (=July 1095 A.D.)". Then "God caused my tongue to dry up so that I was prevented from lecturing", and "I could hardly swallow or digest a single mouthful of food." The doctors said: "the only method of treatment is that the anxiety which has come over the heart should be allayed." He left Baghdad — telling people he was going to Mecca while privately planning to go to Syria — gave away most of his wealth, and spent "nearly two years" in Damascus in solitude and worship (Watt, section on the mystics).

**What he found.** He concluded that the Sufis "were men who had real experiences, not men of words", and that their path is "something which cannot be apprehended by study" — only by living it. *Dhawq* ("tasting") is his word for this direct experience.

**What this means for you.** Al-Ghazali's two crises are two kinds that people still have. One is a crisis of the mind, which needs honest answers and time. The other is a crisis of the heart and of how one lives, which needs changes in practice, company and attention more than arguments. Many people have both.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Ease comes with hardship.** "For indeed, with hardship [will be] ease. Indeed, with hardship [will be] ease" (94:5–6). Sura al-Sharh is Meccan and addressed to the Prophet (pbuh) in a hard period; the repetition is its own reassurance. It does not promise when the ease comes.

**God does not burden beyond capacity.** 2:286 (see "Is doubting a sin?") includes the prayer: "Our Lord, do not impose blame upon us if we have forgotten or erred."

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Distress at doubt is a sign of faith.** When Companions told the Prophet (pbuh) they had thoughts "too grave to express", he said: "That is the faith manifest" ({hadith:muslim:132a}). This applies most directly to unwanted thoughts (see "Is doubting a sin?"), but it also shows that the Prophet (pbuh) did not meet his Companions' inner struggles with condemnation.

#### science — What research says
**Spiritual struggles predict a little more distress later, on average.** [Moderate.] A meta-analysis by Bockrath and colleagues (published online in 2021; a 2022 print citation has not yet been checked) combined 32 longitudinal studies with 5,729 participants. Struggles at the start "significantly predicted increases in negative psychological adjustment" (Zr = 0.08; the paper gives the 95% CI as 0.04–0.10 in the abstract and 0.05–0.10 in the results). That is a small effect: struggles are a risk factor, not a sentence. The link to *positive* adjustment (well-being, growth) was not significant (12 studies; Zr = −0.04).

**Doubt itself is not always the harmful part.** [Mixed.] The Religious and Spiritual Struggles (RSS) scale (Exline et al., 2014) measures six kinds of struggle: divine (with God), demonic, interpersonal, moral, ultimate meaning, and doubt. In one sample of atheist undergraduates, Sedlar and colleagues (2018) found that doubt struggles *negatively* predicted depressive symptoms after other factors were controlled (β about −0.18), though not in a second sample of adults. (2014) has not yet been checked against that paper itself; the effect, where found, is small and not consistent.

**What this means.** Struggling is common, it is not a sign that you are broken, and it carries a modest risk of distress. Support — a trusted person, a counsellor, a community — is a sensible response to a real risk.

#### In practice — What to do
**Be honest about where you are.** Write down, privately, what you actually believe now, what you doubt, and what you miss. Many people find that the doubt is narrower than it felt: one question, not everything.

**Find a guide who won't shame your questions.** Look for someone who: listens to the whole question before answering; says "I don't know" when they don't; does not threaten you; points you to sources rather than demanding trust; and is happy for you to talk to others. Avoid anyone who treats questions as betrayal. A university chaplain, a scholar with experience of young people, or a Muslim counsellor (for example through the Khalil Center in North America) may be a good start.

**Keep small practices, and let them be small.** Pray the obligatory prayers if you can, even if they feel empty. Read a little of the Quran with a translation. Make one short du'a in your own words each day. These keep the connection open without pretending to feel what you don't.

**"What if I go back to prayer and it feels empty?"** That is normal, and it is not proof that the prayer is empty. Al-Ghazali's second crisis was about exactly this gap between knowing and feeling. Keep the form; let the feeling come when it comes; and tell God honestly how it is — the Quran preserves Jacob's (pbuh) words, "I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allah" (12:86).

**Take the questions one at a time.** This site's Ask pages try to give honest answers to the hardest questions. Read the one that troubles you most, then talk about it with your guide.

**Keep good company.** Spend time with people whose faith is calm and kind, not anxious or harsh.

**Get therapy if needed.** If the doubt comes in loops you cannot stop (see "Is doubting a sin?"), or with depression, a therapist can help — ideally one who respects your faith.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"This is just a way of talking myself back into belief."** *Reply:* honesty means following the evidence where it leads, and this site does not ask you to ignore it. But it also means noticing that a crisis is often driven by pain, exhaustion or other people's behaviour, not only by arguments; dealing with those first lets you see the arguments more clearly. *Limit:* some people, after honest searching, do not return. Their search was honest too.

**"Al-Ghazali's 'light' is not something I can do."** *Reply:* that is true; he says himself it was not an argument. What one can do is what he did around it: keep searching, change what needs changing in one's life, and ask. *Limit:* there is no method that guarantees the outcome.

### Questions readers ask next
**What were al-Ghazali's two crises?** A crisis of knowledge (about two months of radical doubt) and, years later, a crisis of how to live (about six months, ending with his leaving Baghdad in 1095).

**What is the *Munqidh*?** *Al-Munqidh min al-Dalal* (*Deliverance from Error*), al-Ghazali's short account of his search for certainty.

**How do I find a guide I can trust?** Look for someone who listens fully, admits what they don't know, never threatens, and is happy for you to read and ask others.

**Do Muslims see doubt as sinful?** Unwanted doubts are not sins ({hadith:bukhari:5269}). Honest questions are not sins either; choosing to reject God after the truth is clear is what the Quran condemns.

**What is "religious OCD"?** Scrupulosity: obsessive doubts and compulsive checking about religious matters. It is a treatable condition (see "Is doubting a sin?").

**What if I never get my old certainty back?** Many people find a different, quieter faith instead. Some do not return. Either way, honesty and kindness to yourself matter.

### Honest limits
Not everyone returns, and that should be respected. The research shows small average effects, not individual destinies. Tedeschi and Calhoun (1996), Behr (2018), the APA material and the Templeton white paper have not yet been consulted. Exline et al. (2014) was read only through a later paper that uses its scale.

### The question, at its strongest
"The Quran says, 'Call upon Me; I will respond to you' (40:60). I prayed every night for my mother to recover. I fasted, I gave charity, I begged. She died. Now I'm told that God 'answered' anyway — by storing a reward for me, or by turning away some other harm I'll never see. But if every outcome counts as an answer, then the promise can never fail, and a promise that can never fail tells me nothing. Either God doesn't keep His word, or He doesn't care, or no one was listening."

### Why people ask it
Usually because of a loss: a loved one who died, a marriage that ended, a child who did not come. The pain of the loss is joined by the pain of feeling unheard by God.

### Short answer
The Quran promises that God *responds* (*ajāba*/*istajāba*), and the Prophet (pbuh) explained that every sincere du'a is answered in one of three ways: what you asked for, a reward stored for the Hereafter, or the turning away of an equal harm. Classical scholars from al-Tabari to al-Razi faced exactly your objection, and many concluded that the promise is of a response, not of granting every request as asked. That does not make the promise empty: it is a promise about God's care, which you can trust or not, but which was never a prediction you could check like a medicine. Your prayers for your mother were not wasted and were not too weak, and her death was not your fault. Grief and even anger can be brought to God; the Quran preserves the prophet Jacob's complaint to God in his grief.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the one whose loved one died despite their prayers
**Your prayers were not wasted.** In the Prophet's (pbuh) teaching, no sincere du'a is lost (Musnad Ahmad 11133, below). What you said to God in those nights was heard and is kept.

**It was not your fault.** You did not pray too little, or with too little faith, or with the wrong words. People die because every life has an end, and the most beloved people of God — prophets among them — lost those they loved.

**You are allowed to be angry, and to say so to God.** The prophet Jacob (pbuh), after losing Joseph, said: "I only complain of my suffering and my grief to Allah, and I know from Allah that which you do not know" (12:86). He complained — to God, not about God. Lament is a form of faith.

**If the grief is overwhelming**, talk to someone. Bereavement counselling helps many people, and grief that does not ease after many months, or that comes with thoughts of not wanting to live, deserves professional care (see the support box on this page).

#### quran — What the Quran says
**"Call upon Me; I will respond to you."** "And your Lord says, 'Call upon Me; I will respond to you.' Indeed, those who disdain My worship will enter Hell [rendered] contemptible" (40:60). Sura Ghafir is Meccan. The second half links du'a to worship: the people condemned are those too proud to call on God at all, not those whose requests were refused.

**"I am near."** "And when My servants ask you, [O Muhammad], concerning Me — indeed I am near. I respond to the invocation of the supplicant when he calls upon Me. So let them respond to Me [by obedience] and believe in Me that they may be [rightly] guided" (2:186). This Medinan verse sits in the middle of the verses on fasting in Ramadan. It uses the same word twice: God *responds* (*ujību*), and people should *respond* to Him (*fal-yastajībū*).

**What "respond" means.** The Arabic verb *ajāba* and its form *istajāba* mean to answer or respond to a call. Saheeh International translates them as "respond". Classical scholars discussed whether this means granting the request or something wider. Khalil (2011) shows that al-Tabari, al-Razi and al-Qurtubi all raised the problem of "many calling upon God without receiving a response", and that several exegetes explained the "response" (*ijāba*) as God's reward (*thawāb*) for the act of calling — given "even though He may not grant the request" (Khalil, p. 96).

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Three ways every du'a is answered.** Abu Saʿid al-Khudri (ra) reports the Prophet (pbuh) as saying that no Muslim calls on God, without sin or cutting family ties, except that God gives him one of three: "He will quickly fulfill his supplication", "He will store it for him in the Hereafter", or "He will divert an evil from him similar to it". The Companions said: "In that case we will ask for more." He replied: "Allah has even more" (Musnad Ahmad 11133; al-Arnaʾut: *isnāduhu jayyid*, "its chain is good"; authenticated by al-Hakim; al-Mundhiri: "with good chains"; also in al-Adab al-Mufrad 710 from the same Companion, graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by al-Albani — as reported on hadithunlocked.com. Al-Tirmidhi 3573, from ʿUbada ibn al-Samit, is a partial parallel with two outcomes — granting, or turning away a similar harm — graded *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ gharīb* by al-Tirmidhi).

**What "graded *jayyid* by al-Arnaʾut" means.** Hadith scholars judge each report by its chain of narrators. *Ṣaḥīḥ* (sound) means every narrator is reliable and the chain is connected; *ḥasan* (fair, good) means the chain is acceptable but one narrator is slightly less precise. Shuʿayb al-Arnaʾut (d. 2016) and Muhammad Nasir al-Din al-Albani (d. 1999) were modern hadith scholars who graded large collections. *Jayyid* ("good") is used for a chain at or near the *ṣaḥīḥ* level. A *ḥasan* or *jayyid* hadith is accepted as evidence; the agreement of several graders and parallel chains strengthens this one.

**Don't give up.** "The invocation of anyone of you is granted (by Allah) if he does not show impatience (by saying, 'I invoked Allah but my request has not been granted')" ({hadith:bukhari:6340}).

**What can block a du'a.** The Prophet (pbuh) described "a person who travels widely, his hair disheveled and covered with dust", who "lifts his hand towards the sky (and thus makes the supplication): "O Lord, O Lord," whereas his diet is unlawful, his drink is unlawful, and his clothes are unlawful and his nourishment is unlawful. How can then his supplication be accepted?" ({hadith:muslim:1015}). Sins and unlawful earnings are named as obstacles; the hadith of the three outcomes also names sin and cutting family ties.

**Du'a and salah.** *Salah* is the ritual prayer, five times a day, with set words and movements. *Du'a* is calling on God in your own words, at any time, for anything. The saying "du'a is worship" (*al-duʿāʾ huwa al-ʿibāda*) is widely cited by the exegetes as a hadith (Khalil, p. 96); its collection and grade have not yet been checked. Its point is that calling on God is itself an act of worship, whatever the outcome.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Ibn al-Qayyim: du'a is a cause within God's decree.** Ibn al-Qayyim (d. 1350), in *al-Dāʾ wa-l-Dawāʾ*, calls du'a "the enemy of distress and calamity; it fights them off and then treats them." He describes three cases: when du'a is stronger than the calamity, "it repels it"; when it is weaker, "the calamity overpowers the du'a but its effects are weakened"; when they are equal, "neither one is able to overpower the other" (trans. Umarji). In plain words: God decrees outcomes together with their causes, and du'a is one of those causes — like medicine for illness. Asking does not fight God's decree; it is part of how the decree unfolds.

**Ibn ʿAtaʾillah: in what He chooses, when He chooses.** Ibn ʿAtaʾillah al-Iskandari (d. 1309), the Egyptian Sufi master, wrote in his *Ḥikam*: "If, in spite of intense supplication, there is a delay in the timing of the Gift, let that not be the cause for your despair. For He has guaranteed you a response in what He chooses for you, not in what you choose for yourself, and at the time He desires, not the time you desire" (aphorism 6, as quoted by Abu Aaliyah; translator not named).

**The Sufi emphasis: nearness, not results.** Khalil reports that al-Razi held the purpose of petition to be "proximity" (*qurba*) to God rather than obtaining the request, and quotes an early sage: the "permission (from God) to make a prayer of petition is better for the servant than the gift" (p. 104). Others listed conditions: Sahl al-Tustari counted "lawful food" among them, and Ibn ʿAtaʾ called presence of heart the "wings of du'a" (pp. 98–99).

#### reasoning — Is an untestable promise meaningful?
**The objection at full strength.** If granting, storing and averting all count as answers, then no possible outcome counts against the promise. A claim that rules nothing out says nothing about the world. So "God answers every prayer" is empty.

**Reply 1: it is a promise of care, not a prediction.** "I will look after you" from a parent is not falsified when the parent refuses a request; it would be falsified if the parent stopped caring. The three-outcome hadith says what kind of care God's response takes — it does not claim that you can see which one happened. It is a claim about a relationship, and its truth depends on whether God exists and is good, which is argued for elsewhere (see the pages on God's existence and on suffering).

**Reply 2: the promise does rule some things out.** It rules out that a sincere prayer is ignored, that it is wasted, and that God is indifferent. It also says what a believer should *do*: keep asking ({hadith:bukhari:6340}), and leave the form of the answer to God. A promise that shapes how you live is not empty, even if you cannot measure its outcomes.

**Reply 3: the tradition did not hide the problem.** Al-Tabari, al-Razi and al-Qurtubi all asked why many prayers seem unanswered (Khalil, p. 94). Al-Qurtubi concluded that the divine response "does not require a response in an absolute sense" (p. 97). Taking the promise as "everything you ask, as you ask it" is not how these classical exegetes read it.

*Limit:* none of these replies will persuade someone who does not already have reasons to trust God. For a grieving person, the honest position is that the promise is to be trusted, not verified.

#### science — What research says
**Trials of intercessory prayer found no measurable effect.** [Established for the trials; their meaning is debated.] In the STEP trial (Benson et al., 2006), 1,802 heart-bypass patients in six US hospitals were randomly assigned. Complications occurred in 52% (315/604) of patients who received prayer and 51% (304/597) of those who did not (relative risk 1.02, 95% CI 0.92–1.15). Patients who were told they would definitely be prayed for had *more* complications than those who received prayer but were told it was uncertain: 59% (352/601) against 52% (RR 1.14, CI 1.02–1.28). A Cochrane review (Roberts, Ahmed and Davison, 2009) of ten trials with 7,646 patients found "no clear effect of intercessory prayer on death" (RR 1.00) and called the findings "equivocal".

**What this means for Muslims.** Islamic theology does not describe du'a as a mechanism that works the same way every time, regardless of who prays, for whom and with what wisdom; it describes it as a request to a Person who chooses (Ibn ʿAtaʾillah; Khalil). Trials test whether prayer works like a drug. That they found no drug-like effect is consistent with the classical view, though a skeptic will say it is also consistent with no one listening. *Limit:* no theological source read here explicitly discusses these trials; this link is the site's own reasoning.

#### In practice — What Muslims do when a prayer seems unanswered
- **Keep asking**, without saying "I prayed and was not answered" ({hadith:bukhari:6340}).
- **Ask for what is good**, not only for a fixed outcome — leaving the form to God (Ibn ʿAtaʾillah).
- **Look at the obstacles** that are in one's control: unlawful income, unresolved wrongs, broken family ties ({hadith:muslim:1015}; Musnad Ahmad 11133).
- **Turn the du'a into presence**: tell God honestly what you feel, including grief and anger (12:86).
- **Do what can be done** alongside the du'a — medical care, practical help — since du'a is one cause among others (Ibn al-Qayyim).

### Questions readers ask next
**If any outcome counts as an answer, how is that different from no answer at all?** The promise rules out being ignored or wasted, and it tells you what to do — keep asking, trust God's choice. It is not a prediction you can check; it is a claim about God's care that depends on whether God is real and good.

**What does *istajāba* mean?** "To respond" to a call. Classical scholars said the response can be granting, reward or protection, not always the thing asked (Khalil).

**Are there things that block du'a?** The hadith names unlawful food, drink and clothing ({hadith:muslim:1015}), sin and cutting family ties (Musnad Ahmad 11133), and impatience ({hadith:bukhari:6340}).

**How is du'a different from salah?** Salah is the five set daily prayers; du'a is calling on God in your own words, any time.

**What does "graded *jayyid* by al-Arnaʾut" mean?** A modern hadith scholar judged the chain good (*isnāduhu jayyid*). Several graders and parallel versions support this hadith.

**Was it my fault that my prayer wasn't answered?** No. The best of people lost those they loved despite their prayers.

### Honest limits
The three-outcome hadith comforts, but it cannot be tested; it is a promise to be trusted. The scientific trials say nothing about God's existence one way or the other. Pargament's work on religious coping and the Yaqeen "Modern Pathways" report have not yet been consulted.

## Q16 — Earthquakes and disasters: are they punishment? (Türkiye, February 2023)

### Sources read

### The question, at its strongest
"On 6 February 2023 two earthquakes struck south-eastern Türkiye and northern Syria and killed tens of thousands of people, many of them children, in their sleep. The Quran says, 'No disaster strikes except by permission of Allah' (64:11) and 'whatever strikes you of disaster — it is for what your hands have earned' (42:30). So either this was punishment — and then what was the children's crime? — or it was God's decree, and then why do we put contractors on trial? And if God could have stopped it and did not, how is He just?"

### Why people ask it
Survivors and their families, and everyone who watched, asked it. In Türkiye, the framing of the deaths as fate (*kader*) provoked theological pushback, including from a former head of the country's religious authority (the source for who first used the phrase "kader planı" has not yet been checked). The same question follows every great disaster, from the 1755 Lisbon earthquake to the 2004 tsunami.

### Short answer
The Quran says that nothing happens without God's permission, and that some suffering follows human wrongdoing. It does not say that every disaster is a punishment of the people it strikes, and the Prophet (pbuh) said the opposite of the victims: those who die under a collapsed building are counted as martyrs. Ibn Kathir reads 42:30 as saying that disasters come because of past sins, most of which God pardons, and then cites hadith that hardship wipes away a believer's sins; neither the verse nor his reading names any particular victims as deserving. Earthquakes follow the natural order God placed in creation; whether a building stands or falls also depends on how people built it — and the evidence from 2023 shows that many collapsed buildings did not meet the codes of their time. So God's decree and human responsibility do not cancel each other. Why God permits such suffering at all, especially for children, is the hardest question, and the honest answer is that Islam offers trust and reasons, not a full explanation.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the survivor
**If you lost someone, their death is counted as an honour, not a verdict on them.** The Prophet (pbuh) counted those who die under a falling building among the martyrs ({hadith:bukhari:2829}). In Islamic belief, a child who dies before puberty is not held to account and is in God's mercy (see below).

**Survivor's guilt is common, and it is not a verdict.** Many survivors ask why they lived. That question has no answer you are required to find; you are not alive because you were more deserving.

**Anger at God is something you can bring to God.** See "Why are prayers unanswered?" for Jacob's complaint (12:86).

**Help.** In Türkiye, AFAD (the Disaster and Emergency Management Authority) and the Ministry of Family and Social Services provide psychosocial support, and 112 handles emergencies. Elsewhere, findahelpline.com lists services. Trauma that does not ease — nightmares, numbness, panic — responds to professional care.

#### quran — What the Quran says, including the hard verses
**64:11 — permission, and guidance for the heart.** "No disaster strikes except by permission of Allah. And whoever believes in Allah — He will guide his heart. And Allah is Knowing of all things" (64:11), from Sura al-Taghabun (usually counted as Medinan). The verse is not about why the disaster happened, but about how a believer meets it. Ibn Kathir reports Ibn ʿAbbas: "By the command of Allah", i.e. by His decree; and, on "He will guide his heart": "Allah will guide his heart to certainty." Ibn Kathir explains that the person who suffers, knows it came by God's decree, and patiently awaits the reward is the one whose heart is guided.

**42:30 — "what your hands have earned".** "And whatever strikes you of disaster — it is for what your hands have earned; but He pardons much" (42:30), from Sura al-Shura, Meccan, addressed to people in general. Ibn Kathir reads it: "whatever disasters happen to you, O mankind, are because of sins that you have committed in the past", and on "He pardons much": "He does not punish you for them, rather He forgives you." He then cites hadith that turn the verse toward mercy: "no believer is stricken with fatigue, exhaustion, worry or grief, but Allah will forgive him for some of his sins thereby", and a report in Imam Ahmad: "No physical harm befalls a believer, but Allah will expiate for some of his sins because of it." So Ibn Kathir reads the verse first as causal — disasters come because of sins, most of which God pardons — and then, through the hadith, as a promise that hardship wipes away a believer's sins. Neither names any particular victims as deserving. The two hadith he cites have not yet been checked for collection or grade here.

**What 42:30 does not say.** It speaks of what strikes "you" in general; it does not say that every person struck by a given disaster is being punished for their own sins. It cannot apply to children, who are not accountable (see below). Applying it to the victims of a particular earthquake, as a verdict, is something the verse does not do and the hadith on martyrs contradicts.

**57:22–23 — written, so that you do not despair.** "No disaster strikes upon the earth or among yourselves except that it is in a register before We bring it into being — indeed that, for Allah, is easy — In order that you not despair over what has eluded you and not exult [in pride] over what He has given you" (57:22–23), from Sura al-Hadid. The purpose given for knowing that disasters are decreed is to protect the heart from despair, not to blame the victims.

**2:155–157 — testing.** "And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger and a loss of wealth and lives and fruits, but give good tidings to the patient, Who, when disaster strikes them, say, 'Indeed we belong to Allah, and indeed to Him we will return.' Those are the ones upon whom are blessings from their Lord and mercy" (2:155–157). Here loss is a test, and those who suffer it are promised blessing and mercy.

**30:41 — human corruption.** "Corruption has appeared throughout the land and sea by [reason of] what the hands of people have earned…" (30:41). That is a modern application: the verse speaks of corruption (*fasād*) caused by people. It is better used as a general reminder that people answer for the harm they cause than as a statement about the 2023 earthquake.

**The punished peoples (ʿĀd, Thamūd and others).** The Quran describes the destruction of specific peoples after they rejected a prophet sent to them.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Victims of collapse are martyrs.** "Five are regarded as martyrs: They are those who die because of plague, abdominal disease, drowning or a falling building etc., and the martyrs in Allah's Cause" ({hadith:bukhari:2829}, narrated by Abu Hurayra). A *martyr* (*shahīd*) is someone whose death God counts as a witness to faith and rewards greatly; this hadith extends the status beyond battle to deaths by disaster and disease.

**Plague: once a punishment, now a mercy for believers.** ʿAʾisha (ra) asked the Prophet (pbuh) about plague, and he told her: "Plague was a punishment which Allah used to send on whom He wished, but Allah made it a blessing for the believers. None (among the believers) remains patient in a land in which plague has broken out and considers that nothing will befall him except what Allah has ordained for him, but that Allah will grant him a reward similar to that of a martyr" ({hadith:bukhari:5734}). The same event can be punishment for one people in one setting, and mercy for believers in another.

**Children.** "Any Muslim whose three children died before the age of puberty will be granted Paradise by Allah because of His mercy to them" ({hadith:bukhari:1381}). IslamWeb's fatwa states that a Muslim child who dies before puberty "will enter Paradise as he or she is not held accountable", and reports that scholars differ about the children of non-Muslims — some say they are in Paradise (citing a report in Bukhari that the Prophet (pbuh) saw the children of Muslims and non-Muslims with Abraham), others that they will be tested on the Day of Judgement, and others decline to judge.

**"Tie your camel".** A man asked whether to tie his camel and trust God or leave it loose and trust God; the Prophet (pbuh) said: "Tie it and rely (upon Allah)" (al-Tirmidhi 2517, narrated by Anas). Its grading is disputed: al-Tirmidhi called it *gharīb* (known through one route), Yahya al-Qattan is reported to have called it *munkar* (rejected), and a modern grader (Zubayr ʿAli Zaʾi) called it good (hadithunlocked.com). It should not be relied on alone; the principle it expresses — take precautions and trust God — is supported by the scholars below.

#### scholars — Decree and human responsibility
**Kader and tedbir.** *Kader* (Arabic *qadar*) is God's eternal knowledge and determination of all events; *tedbir* is precaution, taking the means. Faruk Sancar (SETA, 2023) summarises the mainstream Sunni view: *tevekkül* (trust in God) means taking every precaution and then submitting to God's will, and giving up all action in its name is rejected — he cites al-Ghazali's *Iḥyāʾ* — and "most scholars regard it as haram". He concludes that disasters follow the natural laws God set (*sünnetullah*, Arabic *sunnat Allāh*, "God's way" in creation) and become catastrophes through human error and negligence.

**Why contractors can be blamed.** God's decree includes causes and their effects, including human choices. A builder who used poor concrete chose to do so; that the collapse was known to God does not make the builder's choice any less his. Görmez put it this way: "Problem imar planlarımızın kader planlarımıza uygun olmamasıdır" — "the problem is that our zoning plans are not in line with our plans of destiny", and "Tedbiri bırakıp, takdire yüklenirsek doğru olmaz" — "it is not right to abandon precaution and load everything onto [God's] decree" (Habertürk, 16 March 2023).

**Görmez on punishment.** Mehmet Görmez, president of Diyanet (Türkiye's state Presidency of Religious Affairs) from 2010 to 2017, said that "her afeti sadece ilahi ceza ve azap olarak gören teolojik anlayış da o kadar sığ, yanlış ve eksiktir" — a theology that sees every disaster only as divine punishment is "just as shallow, wrong and incomplete" as a purely mechanical one; and that if it were divine punishment, "masum çocukların suçunun ne olduğu sorgulanır" — one would have to ask what the innocent children's crime was (*sol*). In Habertürk he said that depicting God as "raining punishment from the sky" for people's sins "is not right" ("doğru değildir"), and that classical Islamic culture produced many books on earthquakes but "Tabiat bilgimiz eksik" — "our knowledge of nature was incomplete".

**Diyanet's own ruling.** That ruling could not be found.


#### science — What the evidence shows on buildings
**The buildings that fell were largely those that did not meet the codes.** [Moderate; one engineering study.] Mertol and colleagues (Atılım University) investigated reinforced-concrete buildings after the earthquakes (Mw 7.7 and 7.6, about nine hours apart). They report about 51,000 deaths in Türkiye, 11 severely affected provinces and some 215,000 buildings destroyed or severely damaged as of 15 April 2023. They found that design and construction "did not fully comply with the codes valid at the time", that "most of the collapsed and severely/moderately damaged buildings" were built between 1975 and 2000, and listed specific failures such as improper stirrup detailing and violations of the strong-column principle. They also note that ground shaking at some sites exceeded code values by nearly three times — so the earthquakes themselves were extraordinarily strong. *What this means:* both nature and human choices shaped the death toll; the second is what human beings answer for.

#### reasoning — Why would God permit it, and to children?
**The question at full strength.** A just and all-powerful God could have made a world without lethal earthquakes, or protected the children. That He did not looks like either injustice or powerlessness.

**What Islamic thought offers (the site's own summary, drawing on the pages linked).** First, a world of stable natural laws — in which plates move, mountains rise and the earth stays geologically alive — is a world in which knowledge, planning and responsibility are possible; the same laws that make earthquakes make it possible to build safely. Second, death in Islam is not the end or the worst evil: those who die under rubble are martyrs, children are in God's mercy, and the next life is the place of final justice (see "Is there an afterlife?"). Third, the Quran asks for trust where the full reason is not shown — as when the angels asked why God would create beings who shed blood, and He answered, "Indeed, I know that which you do not know" (2:30; see "Why would a self-sufficient God create?"). The fuller treatment is on the page "If God is good, why is there suffering?".

*Limit:* these are reasons and a call to trust, not a complete explanation. For a parent who lost a child, no argument removes the loss, and the honest response is to say so.

### Questions readers ask next
**If 64:11 and 42:30 say disasters come by God's permission and because of what people earned, how is it not punishment?** Permission means nothing happens outside God's knowledge and decree. Ibn Kathir reads "what your hands have earned" as disasters coming because of past sins, most of which God pardons, and cites hadith that hardship expiates a believer's sins; and the Prophet (pbuh) called the victims of collapse martyrs. A general link between sin and hardship is not a verdict on particular victims.

**If it is kader, how can contractors be blamed?** God's decree includes human choices and their effects. The builder chose; he answers for his choice.

**Why did God permit it to hit children?** Islam holds that children are not accountable and are in God's mercy, and that the next life is the place of justice. It does not claim to show the full reason in this life.

**What are Diyanet, AFAD, kader and musibet?** Diyanet: Türkiye's state Presidency of Religious Affairs. AFAD: Türkiye's Disaster and Emergency Management Authority. Kader: God's decree. Musibet: calamity, a misfortune that strikes.

**What does "martyr" mean if the person did not die fighting?** A death God counts as witness to faith and rewards greatly; the hadith extends it to death by plague, disease, drowning and collapse ({hadith:bukhari:2829}).

**What do ordinary Turkish Muslims believe about the 2023 earthquake?** No survey of believers' views was found.

### Honest limits
The question of why God permits disasters has no complete answer in Islam or any other tradition; this page offers reasons and trust. The engineering findings come from one study's abstract. Diyanet's 2023 ruling, al-Suyuti, Nursi and the death-toll figures have not yet been checked.
