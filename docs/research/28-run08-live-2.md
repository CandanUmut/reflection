## M9 — Amāna: trust, honesty, keeping promises

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via the site's verified Tanzil data (`src/data/quran.json`) — 4:58, 23:8, 17:34, 8:27, 33:72.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English), on 33:72, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-72-sora-33.html, and on 4:58, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-58-sora-4.html.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 33 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:33 — the signs of a hypocrite.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 3 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3 — Khadija's description of the Prophet (pbuh).
- S. Knack and P. Keefer, "Does Social Capital Have an Economic Payoff? A Cross-Country Investigation", *Quarterly Journal of Economics* 112:4 (1997), pp. 1251–1288 — abstract, https://econpapers.repec.org/RePEc:oup:qjecon:v:112:y:1997:i:4:p:1251-1288.
- "Transparency International reports high rates of corruption worldwide", *Jurist* (February 2025) — Corruption Perceptions Index 2024, https://www.jurist.org/news/2025/02/transparency-international-reports-high-rates-of-corruption-worldwide.

### In brief
*Amāna* — trust — covers what others leave with you, your word, your office, and the whole responsibility of being human: "we offered the Trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, and they declined to bear it and feared it; but man [undertook to] bear it" (33:72). Betraying a trust is, in the Prophet's (pbuh) words, a sign of hypocrisy.

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Amāna and amīn.** The root *ʾ-m-n* gives *amn* (safety), *īmān* (faith) and *amāna* (trust). A trustworthy person (*amīn*) is someone with whom others feel safe. Before his prophethood, the Prophet (pbuh) was known among the Quraysh as *al-Amīn*, "the trustworthy" (reported widely in the *sīra*; the specific report was not checked for this page).

**ʿAhd.** A commitment or promise (17:34). Keeping it is part of *amāna*.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The great Trust (33:72).** "Indeed, we offered the Trust to the heavens and the earth and the mountains, and they declined to bear it and feared it; but man [undertook to] bear it. Indeed, he was unjust and ignorant" (33:72), the second-to-last verse of Sura al-Ahzab, Medinan. Ibn Kathir reports several readings of the Trust: Ibn ʿAbbas (via al-ʿAwfi): "obedience"; Ibn ʿAbbas (via ʿAli ibn Abi Talha), and Mujahid, Saʿid ibn Jubayr, al-Dahhak and al-Hasan al-Basri: "the obligatory duties" (*al-farāʾiḍ*); Qatada: "religion, obligatory duties and prescribed punishments"; Ubayy ibn Kaʿb: part of it is that a woman "was entrusted with her own chastity". On why the heavens and earth declined, Ibn ʿAbbas said they "were afraid of it, not because their intention was sinful, but because of their respect for the religion of Allah". "Unjust and ignorant" he explained as: "he underestimated the command of Allah." So the verse is about the moral responsibility of being human: to carry duties freely, which the rest of creation does not.

**Returning trusts and judging justly (4:58).** "Indeed, Allah commands you to render trusts to whom they are due and when you judge between people to judge with justice" (4:58), Medinan. Ibn Kathir reports from Ibn Jurayj (via Ibn Jarir) that it "was revealed concerning ʿUthman bin Talhah from whom the Messenger of Allah took the key of the Kaʿbah" at the conquest of Mecca and then returned it, and says: "It is popular that this is the reason behind revealing the Ayah"; no grading of the report is given. He adds: "Yet, the application of the Ayah is general", citing Ibn ʿAbbas and Muhammad ibn al-Hanafiyya: "This Ayah is for the righteous and wicked." The trusts include God's rights (prayer, zakāh, fasting) and people's rights over each other.

**Believers keep trusts and promises (23:8; 17:34; 8:27).** "And they who are to their trusts and their promises attentive" (23:8), Meccan, in the list of qualities of successful believers. "And fulfill [every] commitment. Indeed, the commitment is ever [that about which one will be] questioned" (17:34), Meccan. "O you who have believed, do not betray Allah and the Messenger or betray your trusts while you know [the consequence]" (8:27), Medinan.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**The signs of a hypocrite.** "The signs of a hypocrite are three: 1. Whenever he speaks, he tells a lie. 2. Whenever he promises, he always breaks it (his promise). 3. If you trust him, he proves to be dishonest. (If you keep something as a trust with him, he will not return it.)" ({hadith:bukhari:33}, narrated by Abu Hurayra). The hadith does not say such a person has left Islam; it names behaviour that contradicts faith.

**Do not betray even the betrayer.** Ibn Kathir cites a hadith from al-Hasan via Samura: "Return the trust to those who entrusted you, and do not betray those who betrayed you", saying that "Imam Ahmad and the collectors of Sunan recorded this Hadith"; its grade was not checked for this page.

**Character before prophethood.** When the Prophet (pbuh) came home shaken after the first revelation, his wife Khadija (ra) reassured him: "Never! By Allah, Allah will never disgrace you. You keep good relations with your kith and kin, help the poor and the destitute, serve your guests generously…" ({hadith:bukhari:3}, narrated by ʿAʾisha). Her argument was his character.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**A broad notion.** The classical readings above show *amāna* stretching from a deposit left with you to the whole of one's religious duties. The current page also cites al-Ghazali on lying and the *maqāṣid*; on lying see "Ethics of speech", where the narrow permitted cases are set out ({hadith:muslim:2605a}).

#### research — What research shows
**Trust and economic performance.** [Moderate; correlational.] Knack and Keefer (1997), using World Values Survey data from 29 market economies, found that trust and civic norms "matter for measurable economic performance", while formal group membership "is not associated with trust or with improved economic performance". Trust was stronger where incomes were higher and more equal, institutions constrained rulers, and people were better educated. This is a correlation across countries, not proof that individual honesty causes growth.

**Corruption today.** [Established for the figures.] In Transparency International's Corruption Perceptions Index 2024, "the global average" was "43 points out of 100", and "more than 120 of the listed countries score below 50 points" (*Jurist*, 2025). Scores for Muslim-majority countries specifically were not checked for this page.

#### practice — In daily life
**Return what you borrow, on time**, and in the condition you received it.

**Keep your word**, or say early and honestly that you cannot.

**Treat your job as a trust.** Your working hours, your employer's resources, and the people who depend on your work are *amāna* (4:58, read generally, as Ibn Kathir does).

**Keep secrets** that are told to you in confidence, unless keeping them would harm someone (see al-Nawawi's cases on "Ethics of speech").

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If someone betrayed your trust**, the Quran treats what was done as a serious wrong (8:27). You are not wrong to grieve it, or to be careful in future. The hadith Ibn Kathir cites asks you not to betray them in return; it does not ask you to trust them again.

**If you have broken a trust**, return what you can, apologise, and make it right. Repentance includes repair.

### Questions readers ask next
**What is the "Trust" in 33:72?** Classical commentators read it as obedience and the obligatory duties of religion (Ibn ʿAbbas, Mujahid, al-Hasan, as reported by Ibn Kathir) — the moral responsibility humans accepted.

**Is 4:58 only about the keys of the Kaʿba?** Ibn Kathir reports that occasion as "popular" but says "the application of the Ayah is general".

**Does breaking promises make someone a non-Muslim?** The hadith calls these signs of hypocrisy in conduct ({hadith:bukhari:33}); it does not declare such a person outside Islam.

**Why are many Muslim-majority countries rated as corrupt?** Corruption has many causes — institutions, poverty, history. The teaching condemns it; the gap between teaching and practice is real.

**What does "trustworthy" (*al-Amīn*) mean for the Prophet (pbuh)?** It was his reputation in Mecca before prophethood; Khadija (ra) appealed to his character when he was afraid ({hadith:bukhari:3}).

### Honest limits
The specific report of the title *al-Amīn* and the grade of "do not betray those who betrayed you" were not checked. Cross-country trust research is correlational. Corruption scores for Muslim-majority countries were not checked.

## M10 — Orphans, the poor, travelers and neighbors

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 93:9–10, 4:36, 107:1–3, 2:177, 76:8–9.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 6015 (sunnah.com), https://www.sunnah.com/bukhari:6015 — the neighbour.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 5304 (via hadithunlocked.com), https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:5304 — the guardian of an orphan.
- J. Holt-Lunstad, T. B. Smith and J. B. Layton, "Social Relationships and Mortality Risk: A Meta-analytic Review", *PLOS Medicine* 7:7 (2010), e1000316, https://journals.plos.org/plosmedicine/article/info:doi/10.1371/journal.pmed.1000316.
- C. A. Nelson, C. H. Zeanah, N. A. Fox et al., "Cognitive Recovery in Socially Deprived Young Children: The Bucharest Early Intervention Project", *Science* 318 (2007), pp. 1937–1940, as reported by *Medical News Today*, https://www.medicalnewstoday.com/articles/92486/.

### In brief
The Quran describes faith in social terms: the one "who denies the Recompense" is "the one who drives away the orphan And does not encourage the feeding of the poor" (107:1–3). Care reaches out in rings — parents, relatives, orphans, the needy, near and far neighbours, the traveller (4:36).

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Yatīm, miskīn, ibn al-sabīl, jār.** *Yatīm*: an orphan, a child who has lost the father. *Miskīn*: someone in need, whose income does not cover their needs. *Ibn al-sabīl*: "child of the road", a traveller cut off from home. *Jār*: neighbour.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The orphan and the one who asks (93:9–10).** "So as for the orphan, do not oppress [him]. And as for the petitioner, do not repel [him]" (93:9–10). Sura al-Duha is Meccan and addressed to the Prophet (pbuh), who was himself an orphan: the sura first reminds him that God found him an orphan and gave him refuge (93:6; not quoted, as no verified text was available), then tells him how to treat orphans.

**Religion denied by neglect (107:1–3).** "Have you seen the one who denies the Recompense? For that is the one who drives away the orphan And does not encourage the feeding of the poor" (107:1–3), usually counted as Meccan. Denying the Day of Judgement is shown not by a statement but by how one treats the weak.

**The rings of care (4:36).** "Worship Allah and associate nothing with Him, and to parents do good, and to relatives, orphans, the needy, the near neighbor, the neighbor farther away, the companion at your side, the traveler, and those whom your right hands possess. Indeed, Allah does not like those who are self-deluding and boastful" (4:36), Medinan.

**Feeding without wanting thanks (76:8–9).** "And they give food in spite of love for it to the needy, the orphan, and the captive, [Saying], 'We feed you only for the countenance of Allah. We wish not from you reward or gratitude'" (76:8–9). In the Prophet's (pbuh) time the captives were non-Muslim prisoners of war, so the verse includes feeding enemies in captivity.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**The neighbour.** Ibn ʿUmar (ra) reports the Prophet (pbuh) as saying: "Gabriel kept on recommending me about treating the neighbors in a kind and polite manner so much so that I thought that he would order (me) to make them (my) heirs" ({hadith:bukhari:6015}).

**The orphan's guardian.** Sahl (ra) reports the Prophet (pbuh) as saying: "I and the guardian of an orphan will be in Jannah like this" ({hadith:bukhari:5304}) — the full report describes him holding up two fingers close together.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Who is a neighbour?** The current page cites al-Qurtubi on 4:36 for a wide definition (forty houses in each direction) including non-Muslim neighbours, and al-Ghazali on neighbours' rights. Neither was read for this run (see Gaps).

#### research — What research shows
**Strong social ties are linked to longer life.** [Established; correlational.] Holt-Lunstad, Smith and Layton (2010) combined 148 studies with 308,849 participants followed for an average of 7.5 years. People with stronger social relationships had about 50% greater odds of survival (odds ratio 1.50, 95% CI 1.42–1.59) — an effect the authors call "comparable with quitting smoking" and larger than many other risk factors.

**Children do better in families than in institutions.** [Established.] In the Bucharest Early Intervention Project, 136 young children living in Romanian orphanages were randomly assigned either to stay or to move to foster families. By 42 and 54 months, the foster-care group had significantly higher developmental and IQ scores; the earlier the placement, the better. Even the youngest placed children remained "nearly 10 points below" never-institutionalised children (Nelson et al., 2007, as reported). This supports the Islamic preference for caring for orphans within families (guardianship, *kafāla*) rather than in large institutions.

#### practice — In daily life
**Know your neighbours' names**, greet them, share food, notice when someone is ill or alone.

**Sponsor an orphan** through a trusted organisation — or, if you can, take one into your home under *kafāla* (guardianship).

**Feed people** without expecting thanks (76:9): a meal for a family in need, a donation to a food bank.

**Help travellers and newcomers**: refugees, students far from home, new arrivals in your city.

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you grew up as an orphan or in care**, the Prophet (pbuh) was an orphan too, and the Quran addresses him as one (93:6–9). Your experience is not a mark against you.

**If you are lonely**, you are not failing at faith. Loneliness is a real health risk (Holt-Lunstad et al.). Reaching out — to a mosque, a neighbour, a community group — is itself part of what these verses ask.

### Questions readers ask next
**Who counts as a neighbour, and does it include non-Muslims?** The current page cites al-Qurtubi for a wide definition including non-Muslims; that was not checked here. 4:36 names "the near neighbor" and "the neighbor farther away" without restriction.

**Who were "the captive" in 76:8?** Prisoners of war, who in the Prophet's (pbuh) time were non-Muslims.

**Why does 107 link denying religion to neglecting the poor?** Because, in the Quran, belief in judgement shows in conduct toward the weak.

**What is *kafāla*?** Guardianship of an orphan, raising them without changing their lineage. Its legal rules were not covered here.

**Is institutional orphan care bad?** The Bucharest study found that family-based foster care produced better development than institutions, especially when started early.

### Honest limits
Al-Qurtubi and al-Ghazali on neighbours were not read. The Bucharest results are reported from a news summary of the *Science* paper. Care for orphans and refugees in Muslim-majority countries varies widely; no data on this were read.

## M11 — Humility and avoiding arrogance

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 31:18–19, 25:63, 17:37, 49:13, 28:83.
- Hadith of arrogance (Sahih Muslim 91), English as given by HadeethEnc, https://hadeethenc.com/en/browse/hadith/6209 (the number 91 is from the current page and sunnah.com's listing; sunnah.com itself could not be opened).
- T. Porter, A. Elnakouri, E. A. Meyers, T. Shibayama, E. Jayawickreme and I. Grossmann, "Predictors and consequences of intellectual humility", *Nature Reviews Psychology* 1 (2022), pp. 524–536, https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9244574/.

### In brief
The Prophet (pbuh) defined arrogance precisely: "Arrogance is to reject the truth and look down on people." Humility is therefore not looking shabby or thinking badly of yourself; it is being open to the truth and never looking down on others.

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Kibr and tawāḍuʿ.** *Kibr* (arrogance) is from the root "to be big": making oneself big over others. *Tawāḍuʿ* (humility) is from "to put down, to lower": lowering oneself, not in self-hatred but in honesty about one's place before God and others.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Luqman's advice (31:18–19).** "And do not turn your cheek [in contempt] toward people and do not walk through the earth exultantly. Indeed, Allah does not like everyone self-deluded and boastful. And be moderate in your pace and lower your voice; indeed, the most disagreeable of sounds is the voice of donkeys" (31:18–19). This is part of the wise man Luqman's advice to his son, in a Meccan sura. It is about everyday bearing: the turned cheek, the swagger, the loud voice.

**The servants of the Most Merciful (25:63).** "And the servants of the Most Merciful are those who walk upon the earth easily, and when the ignorant address them [harshly], they say [words of] peace" (25:63), Meccan. Humility shows in how one answers rudeness.

**You cannot reach the mountains (17:37).** "And do not walk upon the earth exultantly. Indeed, you will never tear the earth [apart], and you will never reach the mountains in height" (17:37). The verse uses gentle mockery: pride is out of proportion to what a human is.

**The Hereafter for those who seek no exaltation (28:83).** "That home of the Hereafter We assign to those who do not desire exaltedness upon the earth or corruption. And the [best] outcome is for the righteous" (28:83). It comes at the end of the story of Qarun (Korah), a rich man destroyed by his pride (28:76–82).

**Rank by righteousness (49:13).** "Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you" (49:13) — not the richest, most learned or best-born.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**The definition.** ʿAbdullah ibn Masʿud (ra) reports the Prophet (pbuh) as saying: "Anyone has an atom's weight of arrogance in his heart will not enter Paradise." When a man asked about wanting to look good, he said: "Verily, Allah is beautiful and He loves beauty. Arrogance is to reject the truth and look down on people" (Sahih Muslim 91, English as given by HadeethEnc; the man's question is summarised here). This is the key to the whole topic: caring how you look is not arrogance; refusing the truth and despising people is.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Iblis as the first arrogance.** The current page cites Iblis's refusal to bow to Adam, in which he claims to be better than Adam (7:12), as the classical first example of *kibr*. The verse is not quoted here because no verified text of it was available for this run.

**Al-Ghazali on the sources of arrogance.** The current page cites al-Ghazali's book on condemning pride (*Iḥyāʾ*, Book 29, *Dhamm al-kibr wa-l-ʿujb*) for a list of what people take pride in — knowledge, worship, lineage, beauty, wealth, strength, followers. That book was not read for this run (see Gaps).

#### research — What research shows
**Intellectual humility.** [Moderate; mostly correlational.] Porter and colleagues (2022) define intellectual humility as "a meta-cognitive ability to recognize the limitations of one's beliefs and knowledge". They review evidence that it is linked to less "myside" bias, more exposure to opposing views, less hostility toward other groups, and more motivation to learn. They caution that "much of this work is correlational", that questionnaires "rely on subjective judgements", that intervention studies are small and "have not been subject to replication", and that "intellectual humility is not a panacea". This fits the hadith's definition: the first mark of arrogance is "to reject the truth".

**Other claims on the current page** (narcissism and aggression; humble leaders and team engagement) were not checked for this run.

#### practice — In daily life
**When you are corrected, say "you may be right" and check.** Rejecting the truth because of who says it is the first half of the hadith's definition of arrogance.

**Notice whom you ignore**: the cleaner, the new person, the one with less status. Greet them first.

**Lower your voice** in an argument (31:19).

**Answer rudeness with peace** (25:63), without giving up the truth.

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you think badly of yourself, that is not humility.** Humility in the hadith is about how you treat truth and other people, not about despising yourself. You are among "the children of Adam" whom God honoured (17:70).

**If religious people have looked down on you** for being less observant, that contempt is what the hadith calls arrogance — in them, not in you.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is caring about my appearance arrogant?** No. The Prophet (pbuh) said, "Allah is beautiful and He loves beauty"; arrogance is rejecting truth and looking down on people.

**How do I know if I am arrogant?** Ask: do I resist being corrected? Do I feel better than others because of my knowledge, piety, family or money?

**Is confidence arrogance?** No. Confidence in what is true, with openness to being wrong, is compatible with humility.

**Why does Luqman mention the voice of donkeys?** It is a vivid image: loudness used to dominate is unpleasant, not impressive (31:19).

**Can religious knowledge make people arrogant?** Yes; the current page cites al-Ghazali listing knowledge and worship among sources of pride (not checked here).

### Honest limits
Al-Ghazali's book on pride, and research on narcissism and humble leadership, were not read for this run. The hadith's number in Sahih Muslim was not confirmed on sunnah.com. Religious arrogance is one of the reasons people give for leaving faith; this page names the teaching, not a claim that communities live it.

## M12 — Care for the earth, moderation, and avoiding corruption on earth

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 7:31, 30:41, 6:141, 55:7–9, 2:205, 2:30.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English), on 6:141, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-141-sora-6.html; on 2:204–205, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-205-sora-2.html; on 55:7–9, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-8-sora-55.html.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 2320 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:2320; Sahih al-Bukhari 2370 (via hadithunlocked.com), https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari/42/12.
- Al-Adab al-Mufrad 479 (via hadithunlocked.com), https://hadithunlocked.com/adab:479, with al-Albani's grade.
- Lynn White Jr., "The Historical Roots of Our Ecologic Crisis", *Science* 155 (1967) — as summarised in course notes by N. Hettinger (College of Charleston), https://hettingern.people.charleston.edu/Philosophy_of_Religion/White_Historical_Roots_of_Ecologic_Crisis.htm (the article itself was not read).
- "Quarter of global population faces extremely high water stress each year", *Down To Earth* (17 August 2023), reporting the World Resources Institute's Aqueduct data, https://www.downtoearth.org.in/water/quarter-of-global-population-faces-extremely-high-water-stress-each-year-91215.

### In brief
"Eat and drink, but be not excessive. Indeed, He likes not those who commit excess" (7:31). The Prophet (pbuh) taught that when a person plants a tree and a bird, a person or an animal eats from it, it is "regarded as a charitable gift for him" — a teaching of care for living things, though whether the Quran's "balance" (55:7–9) is about ecology is a modern reading, not the classical one.

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Isrāf and fasād.** *Isrāf* is excess, going beyond the right measure. *Fasād* is corruption or ruin — in people, society or the land. **Khalīfa** (2:30) means "successor" or "deputy"; many modern writers translate it as "steward". **Ḥimā** is a protected area, closed to private grazing or use.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Do not be excessive (7:31; 6:141).** "O children of Adam, take your adornment at every masjid, and eat and drink, but be not excessive" (7:31). And: "Eat of [each of] its fruit when it yields and give its due [zakah] on the day of its harvest. And be not excessive. Indeed, He does not like those who commit excess" (6:141), Meccan. Ibn Kathir reports two readings of "be not excessive" in 6:141: one, that it forbids "excessive charity beyond normal amounts", with Ibn Jurayj's report of Thabit ibn Qays giving away his whole harvest until "he ended up with no dates"; and the "apparent meaning", which he prefers, that it "refers to eating, meaning, do not waste in eating because this spoils the mind and the body". On "its due on the day of its harvest", Mujahid said: "When the poor people are present (on the day of harvest), give them some of the produce", and Saʿid ibn Jubayr said this "was before Zakah became obligatory".

**Destroying crops and animals (2:205).** "And when he goes away, he strives throughout the land to cause corruption therein and destroy crops and animals. And Allah does not like corruption" (2:205), Medinan. Ibn Kathir reports al-Suddi that 2:204–205 "were revealed about al-Akhnas bin Shariq al-Thaqafi", but considers correct the view of Qatada, Mujahid and al-Rabiʿ ibn Anas that the verses concern "the hypocrites and the believers in general". He explains the aim as "to destroy the crops and the offspring, including what the animals produce", and cites Mujahid: God then "prevents the rain from falling and thus the crops and the offspring perish". The verse is about people who spread ruin, with harm to crops and livestock as its result.

**Corruption from people's hands (30:41).** "Corruption has appeared throughout the land and sea by [reason of] what the hands of people have earned so He may let them taste part of [the consequence of] what they have done that perhaps they will return [to righteousness]" (30:41), Meccan. Reading this as a statement about modern pollution is a modern application; the verse speaks of corruption caused by human wrongdoing, with a warning purpose.

**The balance (55:7–9).** "And the heaven He raised and imposed the balance That you not transgress within the balance. And establish weight in justice and do not make deficient the balance" (55:7–9), Sura al-Rahman. Ibn Kathir reads *al-mīzān* as "the justice" (citing 57:25) and 55:9 as "do not cheat in the weights and measures, but rather observe justice and fairness". He gives no ecological reading. Reading "the balance" as ecological balance is a modern extension and should be labelled as such.

**Successor on earth (2:30).** "Indeed, I will make upon the earth a successive authority" (2:30). The current page notes that classical tafsir mostly read *khalīfa* as succession of generations or as ruling with justice, and that "stewardship of the earth" is a modern reading; this was not re-checked for this run.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Planting is charity.** Anas (ra) reports: "There is none amongst the Muslims who plants a tree or sows seeds, and then a bird, or a person or an animal eats from it, but is regarded as a charitable gift for him" ({hadith:bukhari:2320}).

**Plant even at the end.** "If the Hour comes while one of you has a seedling in his hand", he should plant it (al-Adab al-Mufrad 479, narrated by Anas; graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by al-Albani; the rest of the text is summarised here).

**Protected land.** Al-Ṣaʿb ibn Jaththama reports the Prophet's (pbuh) ruling, "No Hima except for Allah and His Apostle"; he designated al-Naqiʿ as a protected area, and later ʿUmar designated al-Sharaf and al-Rabadha for grazing the animals of zakāh ({hadith:bukhari:2370}). The ruling ended private, exclusive enclosures of common pasture, and reserved protection for the public good.

#### scholars — The hardest critique: Lynn White
**The critique at full strength.** In 1967 the historian Lynn White argued in *Science* that the Western ecological crisis has religious roots: that Christianity, by teaching that humans are made to rule nature and by stripping nature of its sacredness, allowed people to exploit it "with indifference". In the summary read for this page, he calls Christianity "the most anthropocentric religion the world has ever seen", says it "bears a huge burden of guilt", and proposes Francis of Assisi as a model. The critique can be applied to Islam too: the Quran says God "subjected" what is in the heavens and earth to humans, and makes humans *khalīfa*.

**A reply.** First, in the Quran humans are not owners. The earth and what is in it belong to God; humans receive it as a trust (*amāna*, 33:72; see "Amāna") and will be asked about it. Second, the Quran repeatedly condemns excess (7:31; 6:141) and ruin of crops and animals (2:205), and the Prophet (pbuh) made planting a charity and protected land from private seizure (Bukhari 2320, 2370). Third, the Quran does not empty nature of meaning: creation is full of "signs" (*āyāt*) pointing to God (see the Reflect pages), which is closer to reverence than to indifference.

*Limit:* the reply shows what the texts teach; it does not show that Muslims have lived this way. Muslim-majority countries have serious problems of waste and pollution, and the classical commentators read some key verses (55:7–9; 2:30) as about justice and succession, not ecology. "Islam has always been green" would overstate the case.

#### research — What research shows
**Water stress.** [Established for the figures.] The World Resources Institute's Aqueduct data (2023) find that "25 countries or a quarter of the world's population are currently exposed to extremely high water stress annually", and that in West Asia and North Africa "83 per cent of the population is exposed to extremely high water stress" (*Down To Earth*, 2023). Many of these countries are Muslim-majority; 7:31 and 6:141 speak directly to waste.

**Religion and environmental behaviour.** The current page says the effect of religion on pro-environmental behaviour is "mixed". No named study could be opened for this run (two were blocked), so no figures are given (see Gaps). *Time in nature* (White et al., 2019) was not re-checked.

#### practice — In daily life
**Use water as if it were scarce**, because for most people in the region it is: shorter showers, fix leaks, take only what you need in ablution.

**Eat without waste** (7:31): cook what you will eat, share or store leftovers.

**Plant something** — a tree, a garden, a pot on a balcony (Bukhari 2320).

**Support public goods** — parks, protected areas, clean water — as the *ḥimā* did.

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you feel despair about climate change**, the hadith of the seedling speaks to you: even at the end, plant it. Your action matters even if you cannot see its result.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is "the balance" in 55:7–9 about ecology?** Ibn Kathir reads it as justice and honest weights. The ecological reading is modern.

**What does "be not excessive" mean in 6:141?** Ibn Kathir prefers "do not waste in eating"; another reading is "do not give away everything".

**Is 2:205 about environmental destruction?** Classically it describes hypocrites who spread ruin, with crops and livestock destroyed as a result (Ibn Kathir); using it for environmental harm is an extension.

**How should a Muslim answer Lynn White?** By pointing to trust, accountability, the condemnation of excess and the protected *ḥimā* — and by admitting that practice has often fallen short.

**What is a *ḥimā*?** A protected area. The Prophet (pbuh) ruled that only public authority, not private owners, could create one ({hadith:bukhari:2370}).

### Honest limits
Lynn White's article itself was not read, only a summary. No study of religion and environmental behaviour could be opened. The classical reading of *khalīfa* was not re-checked. Waste and pollution are serious in many Muslim-majority countries; this page does not claim Muslims are naturally better environmentalists.

## M13 — Marriage: love and mercy between spouses

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 30:21, 4:19, 2:187, 2:229, 4:128.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English), on 30:21, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-21-sora-30.html, and on 4:19, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-19-sora-4.html.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 5199 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:5199.
- Jamiʿ al-Tirmidhi 3895 (via hadithunlocked.com), with grades.
- T. F. Robles, R. B. Slatcher, J. M. Trombello and M. M. McGinn, "Marital quality and health: a meta-analytic review", *Psychological Bulletin* 140:1 (2014), https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/23527470/ — abstract.
- The Gottman Institute, "The Magic Relationship Ratio, According to Science", https://www.gottman.com/blog/the-magic-relationship-ratio-according-science/.

### In brief
Marriage is called one of God's signs: "He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy" (30:21). Spouses are "clothing" for each other (2:187), and if a marriage ends it must end "with good treatment" (2:229).

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Sakan, mawadda, raḥma.** *Sakan* (30:21) is tranquillity, the calm of being at home. *Mawadda* is affection, love that shows itself. *Raḥma* is mercy, compassion that carries people through weakness.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**A sign of God (30:21).** "And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy. Indeed in that are signs for a people who give thought" (30:21), Meccan. Ibn Kathir explains why spouses stay together: a man stays with a woman "because he loves her", or "he feels compassion towards her if they have a child together", or because "she needs him to take care of her". The commentary attributed to Ibn ʿAbbas (*Tanwīr al-Miqbās*, printed with it) reads *mawadda* as the "love of the wife for her husband" and *raḥma* as the "mercy of the husband towards his wife", adding that "it is also said" the terms can mean love between young and old.

**Live with them in kindness (4:19).** "O you who have believed, it is not lawful for you to inherit women by compulsion… And live with them in kindness. For if you dislike them — perhaps you dislike a thing and Allah makes therein much good" (4:19), Medinan. Ibn Kathir cites Ibn ʿAbbas (recorded by al-Bukhari) on the occasion: before Islam, "when a man dies, his male relatives used to have the right to do whatever they wanted with his wife"; the verse forbade it. He explains "live with them in kindness" as "saying kind words to them, treating them kindly and making your appearance appealing for them, as much as you can", and describes the Prophet (pbuh) as "kind, cheerful, playful with his wives", who used to race with ʿAʾisha. On "perhaps you dislike a thing", Ibn ʿAbbas said God may give him a child with her, "and this child carries tremendous goodness".

**Clothing for each other (2:187).** "They are clothing for you and you are clothing for them" (2:187), in the Medinan verses on fasting. The image is mutual: each covers, protects and is close to the other.

**Settlement and divorce (4:128; 2:229).** "And if a woman fears from her husband contempt or evasion, there is no sin upon them if they make terms of settlement between them — and settlement is best" (4:128). "Divorce is twice. Then, either keep [her] in an acceptable manner or release [her] with good treatment" (2:229).

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Your wife has a right over you.** When ʿAbdullah ibn ʿAmr (ra) fasted every day and prayed all night, the Prophet (pbuh) said: "Do not do that! Observe the fast sometimes and also leave them (the fast) at other times; stand up for the prayer at night and also sleep at night. Your body has a right over you, your eyes have a right over you and your wife has a right over you" ({hadith:bukhari:5199}). Even worship must not crowd out one's spouse.

**The best to his family.** "The best of you is the best to his wives, and I am the best of you to my wives" (al-Tirmidhi 3895; al-Tirmidhi: *ḥasan gharīb ṣaḥīḥ*; al-Albani: *ṣaḥīḥ*).

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Al-Ghazali's book on marriage.** The current page cites al-Ghazali's *Ādāb al-Nikāḥ* (*Iḥyāʾ*, Book 12) on the benefits and dangers of marriage; it was not read for this run (see Gaps). The legal rights of spouses by school were not read either.

#### research — What research shows
**Marital quality and health.** [Established; small effects.] Robles and colleagues (2014) reviewed 126 studies with more than 72,000 people over 50 years. "Greater marital quality was related to better health", with effects of about r = .07 to .21 (r = .11 for mortality) — small, similar in size to the links between health behaviours such as diet and health. The designs limit conclusions about cause.

**The "5 to 1" ratio.** [Moderate; disputed.] The Gottman Institute reports that in studies by John Gottman and Robert Levenson, couples were asked to "solve a conflict in their relationship in 15 minutes", and that stable, happy couples showed about five positive interactions for every negative one; it claims "over 90% accuracy" in predicting divorce nine years later. That claim comes from the Institute itself; independent criticism of its prediction method (often said to overfit the data) was not read for this run, so the figure should be treated cautiously.

#### practice — In daily life
**Say kind words, and say them often** (Ibn Kathir on 4:19).

**Make time.** No duty — work, worship, even good works — should crowd out your spouse ({hadith:bukhari:5199}).

**In conflict, keep the ratio high**: notice and say what is good, not only what is wrong.

**If you dislike something in your spouse**, remember 4:19: "perhaps you dislike a thing and Allah makes therein much good".

**If the marriage is failing**, try settlement (4:128) and arbitration (4:35) — and if it ends, end it "with good treatment" (2:229).

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you are in an abusive marriage, your safety comes first.** No verse requires you to stay in danger. See the help box on "Does Islam treat women unequally?", or findahelpline.com.

**If your marriage is lonely**, that is a real loss. A counsellor — ideally one who respects your faith — can help couples rebuild; many Muslim family-support organisations exist.

**If your marriage ended**, divorce is permitted, and the Quran asks that it end with good treatment (2:229), not with shame.

### Questions readers ask next
**What is the difference between *mawadda* and *raḥma*?** *Mawadda*: affection, love that shows. *Raḥma*: mercy, compassion. Ibn Kathir explains that spouses stay together through love, through compassion (for example, through a child), and through need.

**What does "clothing" in 2:187 mean?** Each spouse covers, protects and is close to the other; the image is mutual.

**Do I have to love my spouse all the time?** 4:19 expects that you may dislike things in your spouse, and asks for kindness anyway.

**Is the 5:1 ratio scientific?** It comes from Gottman's research, reported by his institute; the strong prediction claims are debated.

**What rights do spouses have in Islamic law?** Not covered in detail here; see the run 5 research and a future page on marriage law.

### Honest limits
Al-Ghazali's book on marriage and the schools' rules on spouses' rights were not read. The Gottman figures come from the Gottman Institute. Domestic violence and rising divorce are serious problems in many societies, Muslim-majority ones included; no data on this were read for this run.

## M14 — Knowledge and seeking it

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 96:1–5, 20:114, 39:9, 58:11, 35:28.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 3 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:3 — the first revelation.
- Sunan Ibn Majah 224, with its grade, via thehouseofislam.com, https://www.thehouseofislam.com/hadiths/ibnmajah?n=224.
- Shaykh Dr. Muhammad Abu Bakr Badhib, "Is Seeking Knowledge an Individual or a Communal Obligation?", SeekersGuidance, https://seekersguidance.org/answers/seeking-knowledge/is-seeking-knowledge-an-individual-or-a-communal-obligation/.
- Ibn Rushd, *The Decisive Treatise* (*Faṣl al-Maqāl*), trans. George F. Hourani, https://users.manchester.edu/Facstaff/SSNaragon/Online/texts/316/ibn-Rushd,%20DecisiveTreatise.pdf — opening.
- M. J. Gruber, B. D. Gelman and C. Ranganath, "States of curiosity modulate hippocampus-dependent learning via the dopaminergic circuit", *Neuron* (2014), via the Cell Press press release on EurekAlert (2 October 2014), https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/500062.

### In brief
The first word revealed was "Recite" (96:1), and the Prophet (pbuh) was told: "say, 'My Lord, increase me in knowledge'" (20:114). The Quran asks: "Are those who know equal to those who do not know?" (39:9).

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**ʿIlm.** Knowledge, from the root *ʿ-l-m*, "to know, to mark". In classical usage it covers both religious knowledge and knowledge of the world. *Farḍ ʿayn* is what each individual must know; *farḍ kifāya* is what the community must have, so that if enough people know it, the rest are excused.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The first revelation (96:1–5).** "Recite in the name of your Lord who created — Created man from a clinging substance. Recite, and your Lord is the most Generous — Who taught by the pen — Taught man that which he knew not" (96:1–5), Meccan. ʿAʾisha (ra) tells how, in the cave of Hira, "The angel came to him and asked him to read", and he replied, "I do not know how to read"; after the angel pressed him, the verses came. He returned "with his heart beating severely" and said to Khadija, "Cover me! Cover me!" ({hadith:bukhari:3}). The first revelation is about reading and writing, given to a man who could not read.

**Increase me in knowledge (20:114).** "And, [O Muhammad], do not hasten with [recitation of] the Qur'an before its revelation is completed to you, and say, 'My Lord, increase me in knowledge'" (20:114), Meccan. The setting is revelation: be patient with it, and ask for more understanding.

**Those who know (39:9; 58:11; 35:28).** "Say, 'Are those who know equal to those who do not know?' Only they will remember [who are] people of understanding" (39:9). "Allah will raise those who have believed among you and those who were given knowledge, by degrees" (58:11). "Only those fear Allah, from among His servants, who have knowledge" (35:28) — the verse comes right after a description of the different colours of mountains, people and animals (35:27–28), so the "knowledge" in view includes knowledge of creation.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**"Seeking knowledge is a duty upon every Muslim."** This often-quoted saying is in Sunan Ibn Majah 224, narrated by Anas (ra): "Seeking knowledge is a duty upon every Muslim, and he who imparts knowledge to those who do not deserve it…" — the rest compares that to putting jewels on swine. Its chain is graded *ḍaʿīf* (weak) by the Darussalam editors (thehouseofislam.com); other scholars have judged it acceptable through its many chains, but that was not checked here. Under this site's rules, no claim rests on it alone. The duty to learn what one needs for faith and practice is supported by the scholars below.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Individual and communal knowledge.** Shaykh Muhammad Abu Bakr Badhib explains that individually obligatory knowledge is "the knowledge that every Muslim, male or female, must acquire" — the meaning of the testimony of faith and the "essential rulings on purity, prayer, fasting, zakat, and pilgrimage" — and that communally obligatory knowledge includes "worldly sciences like medicine, engineering": "if some members of the community pursue it, others are exempt." He refers readers to al-Ghazali's *Iḥyāʾ* for detail; al-Ghazali's Book of Knowledge itself was not read for this run.

**Ibn Rushd: the Law commands reasoned study.** Ibn Rushd (Averroes, d. 1198), the Andalusian judge and philosopher, opens his *Decisive Treatise* by arguing that the Quran commands reflection on created things — citing 59:2 — which Hourani's translation of Ibn Rushd renders as "Reflect, you have vision" (not the Saheeh wording) and verses on studying "the kingdom of the heavens and the earth" — and concludes that "we are under an obligation to carry on our study of beings by intellectual reasoning", so that "the Law commands philosophy": its study is "either obligatory or recommended".

#### research — What research shows
**Curiosity helps learning.** [Moderate.] In Gruber, Gelman and Ranganath (2014), people rated their curiosity about trivia answers. When they were curious, they learned the answers better and also remembered unrelated faces shown during the wait, even after 24 hours; brain scans showed more activity in the dopamine reward circuit and the hippocampus (Cell Press press release; numbers not given there).

**Other claims on the current page** — education's link to health and income, and small "growth mindset" effects — were not re-checked for this run; nor were UNESCO literacy figures (see Gaps).

#### practice — In daily life
**Learn what you need for your faith first** — prayer, fasting, purity, the meaning of what you recite — then go further.

**Pursue a field that serves people** — medicine, engineering, teaching, research — as a communal duty.

**Ask "increase me in knowledge"** (20:114) and mean it: read, ask questions, admit what you don't know.

**Follow your curiosity.** It is a gift that helps you learn.

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you struggle with reading or school**, the first revelation came to a man who said, "I do not know how to read" ({hadith:bukhari:3}). Learning is open to everyone at every pace.

**If you were made to feel your questions were wrong**, the Quran praises "people of understanding" (39:9) and the Prophet (pbuh) asked for more knowledge. Asking is part of learning.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is "seeking knowledge is an obligation on every Muslim" authentic?** It is in Ibn Majah 224; its chain is graded weak by the Darussalam editors. The obligation to learn what one needs for faith is supported by the scholars.

**Does "knowledge" mean only religious knowledge?** Classical scholars counted medicine and other sciences as communal duties (Badhib); 35:27–28 links knowledge to the study of creation.

**What did Ibn Rushd argue?** That the Quran commands reasoned study of creation, so philosophy is obligatory or recommended for those able (*Decisive Treatise*).

**Why was the first word "Recite"?** The verses speak of the pen and of teaching what was not known (96:4–5); the tradition reads this as a call to knowledge.

**Why do some Muslim-majority countries lag in education?** For many historical, economic and political reasons; no data were checked for this run.

### Honest limits
Al-Ghazali's Book of Knowledge was not read. Education statistics for Muslim-majority countries were not checked. "Golden age" nostalgia can wrongly imply nothing needs to change today.

## M15 — Self-discipline and character

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 91:7–10, 79:40–41, 29:69, 12:53, 13:11.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 6464 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:6464.
- Sunan Abi Dawud 4799 (sunnah.com), https://www.sunnah.com/abudawud:4799, with al-Albani's grade.
- T. E. Moffitt et al., "A gradient of childhood self-control predicts health, wealth, and public safety", *PNAS* 108 (2011), as reported by *Contemporary Pediatrics*, https://contemporarypediatrics.com/view/children-poor-self-control-have-worse-health-and-other-outcomes-adults.
- P. Lally, C. H. M. van Jaarsveld, H. W. W. Potts and J. Wardle, "How are habits formed: Modelling habit formation in the real world", *European Journal of Social Psychology* (2010), as reported by the British Psychological Society, https://bps.org.uk/research-digest/how-form-habit.
- M. S. Hagger, N. L. D. Chatzisarantis et al., "A multilab preregistered replication of the ego-depletion effect", *Perspectives on Psychological Science* 11:4 (2016), https://www.psychologicalscience.org/journals/perspectives/1745691616652873/.

### In brief
"He has succeeded who purifies it, And he has failed who instills it [with corruption]" (91:9–10). Character is trained little by little: "the most beloved deed to Allah is the most regular and constant even if it were little" ({hadith:bukhari:6464}).

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Nafs, tazkiya, mujāhada.** *Nafs* is the self or soul, including its desires. *Tazkiya* is purification — the same root as *zakāh* — meaning both cleansing and growth. *Mujāhada* is striving against the self. *Khuluq* is character.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The soul and its purification (91:7–10).** "And [by] the soul and He who proportioned it And inspired it [with discernment of] its wickedness and its righteousness, He has succeeded who purifies it, And he has failed who instills it [with corruption]" (91:7–10), Meccan. The verse says humans are given the knowledge of both, and their success depends on what they do with it.

**Holding the self back (79:40–41).** "But as for he who feared the position of his Lord and prevented the soul from [unlawful] inclination, Then indeed, Paradise will be [his] refuge" (79:40–41), Meccan.

**Striving and guidance (29:69).** "And those who strive for Us — We will surely guide them to Our ways. And indeed, Allah is with the doers of good" (29:69). The effort comes first, and guidance follows.

**The self that urges evil (12:53).** "And I do not acquit myself. Indeed, the soul is a persistent enjoiner of evil, except those upon which my Lord has mercy" (12:53). In Sura Yusuf, this is spoken at the end of the story of the minister's wife (Ibn Kathir and others differ on whether the speaker is Joseph or the woman; that discussion was not read for this run).

**Change begins inside (13:11).** "Indeed, Allah will not change the condition of a people until they change what is in themselves" (13:11). The verse is about a *people*; applying it to an individual's habits is a common extension.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Small and steady.** ʿAʾisha (ra) reports the Prophet (pbuh) as saying: "Do good deeds properly, sincerely and moderately and know that your deeds will not make you enter Paradise, the most beloved deed to Allah is the most regular and constant even if it were little" ({hadith:bukhari:6464}). Moderation and constancy, not bursts of effort, and no pride in one's deeds.

**Good character weighs most.** "There is nothing heavier than good character put in the scale of a believer on the Day of Resurrection" ({hadith:abudawud:4799}, narrated by Abu al-Dardaʾ; al-Albani: *ṣaḥīḥ*).

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Training the soul.** The current page cites al-Ghazali's *Riyāḍat al-Nafs* (*Iḥyāʾ*, Book 22, *On Disciplining the Soul*, translated by T. J. Winter) — comparing training the soul to training an animal, by gradual habit — and Miskawayh's *Tahdhīb al-Akhlāq* and Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī's *Akhlāq-i Nāṣirī*, which follow Aristotle in treating virtue as a balanced mean formed by repetition. None of these texts was read for this run (see Gaps); the Bukhari hadith above states the same principle of steady practice.

#### research — What research shows
**Childhood self-control predicts adult life.** [Established; correlational.] The Dunedin study "followed 1,037 children from birth to age 32". Children with lower self-control, as rated by teachers, parents and themselves, were more likely as adults to have worse physical health, substance dependence, money and credit problems, and criminal convictions — "after controlling for social class and IQ score" (Moffitt et al., 2011, as reported).

**Habits take time.** [Moderate; small study.] In Lally and colleagues (2010), 96 people chose a new daily habit (82 completed). The "average time to reach maximum automaticity was 66 days", ranging from 18 days to a predicted 254. Missing one day made little difference; many missed days lowered the habit's strength (BPS summary). This fits the hadith: little and regular.

**"Willpower runs out" did not replicate.** [Established for the replication.] The "ego depletion" idea — that self-control is a fuel that runs down — was tested in 23 laboratories with 2,141 participants. The effect was d = 0.04 (95% CI −0.07 to 0.15), not reliably different from zero (Hagger et al., 2016).

#### practice — In daily life
**Start small and keep it up**: two minutes of Quran, two units of extra prayer, one act of kindness — every day ({hadith:bukhari:6464}).

**Expect about two months** before a new habit feels natural (Lally et al.), and don't give up after a missed day.

**Change the setting, not only the will**: remove temptations, set reminders, choose good company.

**Work on character, not only rituals**: patience, honesty, gentleness weigh most ({hadith:abudawud:4799}).

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you keep failing**, the Quran expects struggle: "the soul is a persistent enjoiner of evil, except those upon which my Lord has mercy" (12:53). Falling and getting up again is the path, not a sign that you are not on it.

**If you are trying to stop an addiction**, willpower alone is rarely enough; ask for help — a doctor, a counsellor, a support group. Seeking help is part of striving (29:69).

### Questions readers ask next
**Can character really change?** The Quran's call to purify the soul (91:9) assumes it can; the research on habits shows it takes time and repetition.

**Why "little but constant"?** Because steady habits last; bursts of effort fade ({hadith:bukhari:6464}; Lally et al.).

**Is willpower a limited resource?** The large replication found no reliable "ego depletion" effect (Hagger et al., 2016).

**Who is speaking in 12:53?** Commentators differ (Joseph or the minister's wife); not checked here.

**Does 13:11 apply to individuals?** It speaks of "a people"; using it for personal change is a common extension.

### Honest limits
Al-Ghazali's *On Disciplining the Soul*, Miskawayh and al-Ṭūsī were not read for this run. The Dunedin and habit studies are reported from summaries. No one can promise that discipline will feel easy or quick.
