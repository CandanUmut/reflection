## Q27 — Why believe Muhammad was a prophet?

### Sources read

- James A. - Youssef Chouhoud, "What Causes Muslims to Doubt Islam? A Quantitative Analysis", Yaqeen Institute (13 February 2018; updated 22 July 2024), https://yaqeeninstitute.org/read/paper/what-causes-muslims-to-doubt-islam-a-quantitative-analysis.

### The question, at its strongest
"Many people in history have claimed to receive messages from God. Some were frauds; many were sincere. I don't have to think Muhammad was lying to doubt him: he could have been completely sincere and still mistaken — a gifted, spiritual man who experienced his own deep thoughts as revelation. His later life also included war, political power and many marriages, which look like the career of a successful leader more than of a messenger. And the arguments I hear — the Quran is inimitable, he was illiterate, he predicted things — are either untestable or depend on Muslim sources written long after him."

### Why people ask it
The question underlies all the others: if he was a prophet, the rest of Islam follows; if not, none of it does. People meet the "liar, lunatic or prophet" argument, often in a weak form that ignores the sincere-but-mistaken option.

### Short answer
That a man named Muhammad led the Arab movement in the 630s is confirmed by non-Muslim sources written within a few years of his death; what those sources do not confirm is his prophethood. The case for it is cumulative: his reputation for honesty before he claimed anything (Khadija's appeal to his character, {hadith:bukhari:3}); his steadiness under years of persecution, which even the critical historian W. Montgomery Watt took as evidence of sincerity; a scripture that, by its own challenge, cannot be imitated (2:23), and which came, by most classical readings, through a man without formal learning; and the moral transformation of the people around him. The "sincere but mistaken" option is the serious one, and it cannot be ruled out by argument alone. What weighs against it, for Muslims, is the content of the message itself and its fruits. Some popular "prophecies" are overstated; the Byzantine prediction of 30:2–4 is the best-known, and it has real complications that should be stated.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**This is a question worth taking slowly.** Believing that someone spoke for God is a large step, and Islam does not ask you to take it on hearsay. The Quran itself says: "Then will you not reason?" (10:16).

#### quran — What the Quran says
**"A lifetime before it" (10:16).** "Say, 'If Allah had willed, I would not have recited it to you, nor would He have made it known to you, for I had remained among you a lifetime before it. Then will you not reason?'" (10:16), Meccan, addressed to the Quraysh. The argument: they had known him for forty years; nothing in his past prepared them for the Quran.

**The challenge (2:23; 17:88).** "And if you are in doubt about what We have sent down upon Our Servant [Muhammad], then produce a surah the like thereof and call upon your witnesses other than Allah, if you should be truthful" (2:23), Medinan, addressed to those who doubt. Elsewhere: "Say, 'If mankind and the jinn gathered in order to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not produce the like of it…'" (17:88). The challenge is to produce something of the same kind. In al-Baqillani's classical account of the Quran's inimitability (*iʿjāz*), it lies chiefly in its unique composition (*naẓm*), alongside its reports of the unseen and the Prophet's (pbuh) being *ummī* (Divlekci 2019; see "Are 'scientific miracles' in the Quran real?").

**The Byzantine prediction (30:2–4).** "The Byzantines have been defeated In the nearest land. But they, after their defeat, will overcome. Within three to nine years" (30:2–4), Meccan. "Three to nine years" is Saheeh's rendering of *biḍʿi sinīn*, which Saʿid ibn Jubayr explained as "less than ten" (Ibn Kathir). Ibn Kathir says the verses "were revealed about the victory of Sabur, the king of Persia, over Ash-Sham", and records a wager: Abu Bakr (ra) bet the idolaters that Rome would win; they agreed on six years; the Romans won "when the seventh year came" (Ibn Kathir's abridgement: "This is a Hasan Hadith"). On sunnah.com the report, narrated by Niyar b. Mukram al-Aslami, is {hadith:tirmidhi:3194}; al-Tirmidhi calls it *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ gharīb*, known only through ʿAbd al-Rahman b. Abi al-Zinad, and Darussalam grades it *ṣaḥīḥ*. A report from Ibn ʿAbbas in al-Tirmidhi (graded *ḥasan gharīb*) gives five years instead of six.

**What is complicated about it.** First, the dates: the Persians took Jerusalem "following a three weeks siege in 614"; Heraclius counter-attacked from 622 to 626, defeated the Persians at Nineveh on 12 December 627, and the war ended about 628 (*Wikipedia*, "Byzantine–Sasanian War of 602–628", read by the independent source check). The Ibn Kathir page read does not give dates. Second, an old variant reading vowels the verbs the other way — the Byzantines "have overcome… and will be defeated" — reported in the tafsir literature; the Ibn Kathir page read here does not mention it, so it is noted as unverified. A fair reader will say: the prediction was made in Mecca, at a time when Rome looked beaten; it came true; and the reports about it come from Muslim sources.

#### history — History: what non-Muslim sources say
**Early non-Muslim sources confirm that he existed and led the movement.** Bishop's survey (drawing on Hoyland) lists, among others:
- **Thomas the Presbyter** (c. 640) describing a battle near Gaza in 634 against "the Arabs of Muḥammad".
- **The Khuzistan Chronicle** (no later than the 660s): the Arab forces, "whose leader (*mdabbrānā*) was Muḥammad".
- **Sebeos**, an Armenian bishop (second half of the seventh century), who portrays him as a merchant who came to believe in one God, preached to his people, and gave laws — against carrion, wine, false speech and fornication — that parallel the Quran's.
- **John bar Penkaye** (688), who says the Arabs "kept to the tradition of Muḥammad".
- **The Doctrina Jacobi** (c. 634–640), in which a Jew hears that "the prophet had appeared" among the Saracens; Bishop notes that its identification is disputed.

**What they do not confirm.** These sources say little about his life in Mecca, and nothing that could prove or disprove revelation. They confirm a monotheist leader and lawgiver; the claim that God spoke to him rests on other grounds. Most detailed biographical information comes from later Muslim sources (Bishop).

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**What "ummī" means.** The Quran calls the Prophet (pbuh) *al-nabī al-ummī* (7:157, not quoted from verified data here). The word is debated. Ibn Manzur's dictionary defines *ummī* as "someone unable to read or write"; al-Tabari and Fakhr al-Din al-Razi suggest it refers to "Arabs without a holy scripture"; al-Razi and others also proposed "the people of Makkah", a reading that is "debated due to linguistic reasons, as discussed by Al-Zamakhshari and Al-Qurtubi"; al-Suyuti and al-Tabatabaʾi read 62:2 as "a lack of formal religious education rather than literal illiteracy" (IslamOnWeb). The same article reports that "the majority of commentators, including Fakhr al-Din al-Razi and Shaykh Tusi, interpret 'Ummiyy' as indicating a lack of formal education in the scriptures". So "he could not read or write" is one classical reading among several. The account of the first revelation is consistent with it: when the angel told him to read, he answered, "I do not know how to read" ({hadith:bukhari:3}).

**A critical historian on his sincerity.** W. Montgomery Watt (1909–2006), a Scottish historian of Islam, wrote in *Muhammad at Mecca*: "His readiness to undergo persecutions for his beliefs, the high moral character of the men who believed in him…" — evidence, for Watt, of fundamental integrity — and that "none of the great figures of history is so poorly appreciated in the West as Muhammad" (quoted on Goodreads; page not given). Watt held the Prophet (pbuh) to be sincere. On revelation his view does not fit a simple "accepted" or "rejected": *Wikipedia* (citing an interview) reports that he regarded the Quran as "divinely inspired", though not "infallibly" true, and WikiShia reports that in *Muhammad: Prophet and Statesman* he described revelation in terms of "creative imagination" (both pages read by the independent source check).

#### reasoning — How philosophers argue about it
**The trilemma, done properly.** If someone claims revelation, there are more than three options: he lied; he was mentally ill; he was sincere but mistaken (his own mind produced what he took to be revelation); or he was telling the truth. Watt, a non-Muslim historian, rejected the first: he took the Prophet (pbuh) to be sincere. The third is the serious alternative.

**Against "sincere but mistaken".** It has to explain several things together:
1. **The character before the claim.** Khadija's reassurance — "Allah will never disgrace you. You keep good relations with your kith and kin, help the poor and the destitute, serve your guests generously" ({hadith:bukhari:3}) — appealed to forty years of known conduct.
2. **His surprise and fear.** The first revelation left him "with his heart beating severely" saying "Cover me! Cover me!" ({hadith:bukhari:3}): not the reaction of someone seeking a role.
3. **The text.** A long, varied scripture, delivered over 23 years in public, which presents its own inimitability as evidence (2:23), from a man with — on the common reading — no formal learning.
4. **The fruit.** A movement that, in non-Muslim sources too, held to strict monotheism and law (Sebeos; John bar Penkaye).

*Limit:* each of these can be explained in other ways by someone who already doubts revelation: sincere visionaries can be honest, frightened and gifted; movements can be transformed by charismatic leaders. The "sincere but mistaken" view is not refuted; for many people it is outweighed.

**His later power.** Critics point out that in Medina he became a ruler and military leader and had several wives. Muslims reply that the Quran presents this as part of his mission — a prophet who also governs. The specific questions are treated on other pages ("The Prophet's wars and Banu Qurayza"; "Aisha's age"; "Polygyny"). *Limit:* for some readers these features remain the strongest reason to doubt.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"The sources are all late and Muslim."** *Reply:* the main source, the Quran, is very early (see "Was the Quran preserved?"), and non-Muslim sources confirm the outline. *Limit:* most of the detail about his life comes from biographies written a century or more later.

**"No one met the challenge of 2:23 because it is undefined."** *Reply:* in the classical understanding the challenge concerns above all the Quran's composition — a text of its kind, in its language — and it was issued to people who spoke that language. *Limit:* literary judgement is not measurable, and critics say the challenge cannot be tested objectively.

**"Many prophecies are stretched."** *Reply:* yes. Claims that the Quran predicted modern scientific discoveries are often overstated (see the next page). The Byzantine prediction is the clearest case, and even it depends on Muslim reports. *Limit:* prophecy alone should not carry the argument.

#### views — The range of views
- **Muslim scholars** — prophethood is known by the Quran, his character and his message (see al-Ghazali's argument, on "Why believe the Creator is involved?").
- **Watt** — sincere; the Quran "divinely inspired" but not infallible (*Wikipedia*, citing an interview).
- **Critics** — sincere but mistaken, or worse; his political career counts against him.

### Questions readers ask next
**What about someone who sincerely believed he received revelation but was mistaken?** It is the serious alternative. Muslims answer that his prior character, his fear at the first revelation, the text and its fruits together make it less likely; it cannot be disproved.

**Which predictions were fulfilled besides the Byzantine one, and which are overstated?** Only the Byzantine one was examined. Modern "scientific prophecies" are often overstated (see the next page).

**What exactly is the challenge of 2:23?** To "produce a surah the like thereof": a text of the Quran's kind. For al-Baqillani, its inimitability lay chiefly in its composition, alongside its reports of the unseen and the Prophet's (pbuh) being *ummī* (Divlekci 2019).

**Which seventh-century non-Muslim sources mention him?** Thomas the Presbyter (c. 640), the Khuzistan Chronicle (by the 660s), Sebeos (later seventh century), and the disputed Doctrina Jacobi (c. 634–640).

**Is it established that he could not read or write?** It is one classical reading of *ummī*, consistent with the first-revelation hadith ("I do not know how to read"). IslamOnWeb reports that most commentators, including al-Razi, read *ummī* as lacking formal education in the scriptures, and others as "without a scripture".

**What did his followers' transformation look like?** Non-Muslim sources describe a monotheistic community that kept his laws strictly (Sebeos; John bar Penkaye). More detailed accounts come from Muslim sources.

### Honest limits
No argument proves that God spoke to him; the case is cumulative and judged differently by different people. Hoyland's book, Watt's book and the variant reading of 30:2–4 have not yet been consulted directly; the war's dates come from *Wikipedia* only. The doubt survey (Chouhoud, 2018) is discussed on the "scientific miracles" page.

## Q4 — Was the Quran preserved?

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 15:9.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 4986 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:4986 — Abu Bakr's collection.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 4987 (sunnah.com, as read for run 2) — ʿUthman's standardisation.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 6829 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:6829 — the verse of stoning.
- "Birmingham Quran manuscript", *Wikipedia*, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Birmingham_Quran_manuscript.
- "Sanaa manuscript", *Wikipedia*, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanaa_manuscript (summarising Sadeghi & Bergmann 2010, Sadeghi & Goudarzi 2012, Hilali).
- "Codex of Ibn Masʿud", *Wikipedia*, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Codex_of_Ibn_Mas%27ud.
- "Versions Of The Qur'an?", Islamic Awareness (updated 15 January 2002), https://islamic-awareness.org/quran/text/qiraat/hafs — summarising Adrian Brockett, "The Value of Hafs and Warsh Transmissions for the Textual History of the Qur'an", in A. Rippin (ed.), *Approaches to the History of the Interpretation of the Qur'an* (Oxford, 1988), pp. 33–45.
- "Qur'an fragment Tübingen University Library Ma VI 165", *Medieval.eu* (11 November 2014) (as read for run 2).

### The question, at its strongest
"Muslims say not a letter of the Quran has changed. But the early sources themselves say that the caliph ʿUthman had the other copies burned, that the Companion Ibn Masʿud had a copy without the first and last two suras, that a verse on stoning was once recited and is not in the book, and that there are different 'readings' still recited today. The oldest manuscripts show variants. How is that 'preserved'?"

### Why people ask it
Christian and atheist critics raise it; Muslims hear the strong claim "not a letter changed" and then discover the early history.

### Short answer
The Quran's text was fixed very early: manuscripts radiocarbon-dated to within a few decades of the Prophet's (pbuh) death match the standard text closely, and the tradition itself records, openly, how it was collected under Abu Bakr and standardised under ʿUthman. Preservation in the classical Muslim sense does not mean that no other wording ever existed. The tradition records early variant copies (such as Ibn Masʿud's), verses reported as no longer recited, and several accepted ways of reading the same consonantal text (*qirāʾāt*). An early witness from outside the ʿUthmanic line, the lower text of the Sanaa palimpsest, has variants in wording and sura order; *Wikipedia* judges, without a citation, that they "rarely diverge from the fundamental meaning". So "not a letter has changed since ʿUthman" is close to the evidence; "there were never any variants" is not.

### The full answer

#### quran — What the Quran says
**"We will be its guardian" (15:9).** "Indeed, it is We who sent down the Qur'an and indeed, We will be its guardian" (15:9), from the Meccan Sura al-Hijr, answering those who mocked the Prophet (pbuh) (15:6). The promise is God's; how it was fulfilled through human transmission is a historical question.

#### history — How the text was collected
**Under Abu Bakr.** After the battle of Yamama, ʿUmar told Abu Bakr that "Casualties were heavy among the Qurra' of the Qur'an" — the memorisers — and feared more would die. Abu Bakr charged Zayd ibn Thabit (ra), the Prophet's (pbuh) scribe, to "search for (the fragmentary scripts of) the Qur'an and collect it in one book." Zayd says: "I started looking for the Qur'an and collecting it from (what was written on) palme stalks, thin white stones and also from the men who knew it by heart." He found the last verses of Sura al-Tawba only with Abu Khuzayma al-Ansari. The sheets stayed with Abu Bakr, then ʿUmar, then ʿUmar's daughter Hafsa (ra) ({hadith:bukhari:4986}).

**Under ʿUthman.** Hudhayfa urged ʿUthman to "Save this nation before they differ about the Book"; ʿUthman had copies made from Hafsa's sheets, told the copyists that where they differed they should "write it in the dialect of Quraish", sent copies to the provinces, and ordered other Quranic materials "burnt" ({hadith:bukhari:4987}).

**Why the burning?** The report gives no explicit reason for the burning itself; ʿUthman acted after Hudhayfa's warning that people would "differ about the Book". The burned materials were other written copies. Their full contents are unknown, because most were destroyed; the reports about Ibn Masʿud's copy (below) and the Sanaa lower text give partial glimpses.

#### history — What the manuscripts show
**The Birmingham leaves.** Two leaves in the University of Birmingham, in Hijazi script (the early Arabic script of Mecca and Medina, with no short-vowel marks and some consonants marked with "oblique dashes"), containing parts of suras 18–20, were radiocarbon-dated at Oxford to "1465±21 years BP (before 1950), which corresponds with 95.4% confidence to the calendar years CE 568–645 when calibrated" (*Wikipedia*). *BP* means "before present", where "present" is fixed at 1950. The range begins before the Quran's revelation began — which shows the limits of the method: radiocarbon dates the death of the animal whose skin became the parchment, not the writing. David Thomas notes the text "could have been written as late as 650–655"; François Déroche has "expressed reservations about the reliability of the radiocarbon dates" (*Wikipedia*).

**Tübingen.** A fragment in Tübingen University Library (Ma VI 165) was dated to AD 649–675 with 95.4% probability (*Medieval.eu*).

**The Sanaa palimpsest.** A *palimpsest* is a manuscript whose first text was scraped off and written over. In the Sanaa manuscript, both layers are Quran. *Wikipedia* gives the parchment's radiocarbon range as 578–669 CE, citing Sadeghi & Bergmann (2010, p. 348); its sentence "95% accuracy to before 669 CE, and 75% probability from before 646 CE" has no citation. The lower text "contains many variations from the standard text", and its sura order "corresponds to no known Quranic order". Two examples: at 2:196, where the standard text has *fa-man kāna minkum* ("whoever among you"), the lower text has *fa-in kāna aḥadun minkum* ("if one of you"); at 9:125, where the standard text has "in their hearts is *disease*" (*maraḍ*), the lower text has "in their hearts is *filth*" (*rijs*). *Wikipedia*'s own judgement, without a citation, is that the variants "rarely diverge from the fundamental meaning". It also cites Éléonore Cellard as finding "numerous similarities" with the sura orders reported for Ibn Masʿud and Ubayy. Sadeghi and Goudarzi find some overlap with Ibn Masʿud's reported variants but "the differences much outnumbering the correspondences"; Asma Hilali argues both layers may be "school exercises", a view Nicolai Sinai has criticised.

#### history — Ibn Masʿud's codex
**What is reported.** Reports say his copy "excludes the first chapter, as well as the two chapters known as Al-Muʿawwidhatayn" — suras 1, 113 and 114 (*Wikipedia*, citing Sadeghi & Bergmann 2010 and Harvey 2017). But "historical accounts are inconsistent regarding the details", the variant "has not been definitively found in any existing early Quranic manuscript", and his reading had supporters in Kufa "as late as the 9th century". Classical explanations of the omission have not yet been consulted.

#### history — Readings (*qirāʾāt*) and modes (*aḥruf*)
**The consonantal text.** Early Arabic script wrote consonants without vowels and often without the dots that distinguish letters. This written skeleton is the *rasm*; the ʿUthmanic copies fixed it.

**Readings.** A *qirāʾa* is an accepted way of reciting that skeleton, with particular vowels and pronunciation, traced back through chains of teachers. *Ḥafṣ* and *Warsh* are two transmissions of two of these readings. Brockett, comparing them closely, concluded that "the limits of their variation clearly establish that they are a single text" (Islamic Awareness, quoting Brockett, 1988). Islamic Awareness's own example of an acceptable difference is dot placement: *taʿlamūn* ("you know") against *yaʿlamūn* ("they know").

**Modes.** The *aḥruf* ("seven modes") of a well-known hadith are, in one common explanation, the dialectal forms in which the Quran was first allowed to be recited, reduced to the dialect of Quraysh under ʿUthman (Islamic Awareness). Scholars differ on what the seven *aḥruf* were; that debate was not covered here.

#### history — Verses no longer recited
**The verse of stoning.** Ibn ʿAbbas (ra) reports ʿUmar (ra) saying: "I am afraid that after a long time has passed, people may say, 'We do not find the Verses of the Rajam (stoning to death) in the Holy Book'", and so leave "an obligation that Allah has revealed" ({hadith:bukhari:6829}). The tradition has a category for this: *naskh al-tilāwa dūna al-ḥukm*, a verse whose wording was abrogated (no longer part of the recited Quran) while its ruling remained. Which classical scholars applied it to this report, and how, has not yet been consulted in a named source. Critics see it as evidence that the text lost material. Both sides read the same report.

#### reasoning — How oral transmission worked
**Memory and writing together.** Zayd's method used both written fragments and "the men who knew it by heart" ({hadith:bukhari:4986}). Recitation in prayer, every day, by many people, meant that a change in one copy would be noticed by others.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"ʿUthman burned the evidence."** *Reply:* the tradition records the burning openly, after Hudhayfa's warning ({hadith:bukhari:4987}); the copies sent out were made from Hafsa's sheets, collected under Abu Bakr; and an early witness that escaped (Sanaa) differs mainly in order and wording, not in content. *Limit:* we cannot compare the burned copies, so "nothing was lost" cannot be proved.

**"The variant readings show the text was fluid."** *Reply:* the readings vary within the fixed consonantal skeleton, and Brockett found Hafs and Warsh to be "a single text". *Limit:* the pre-ʿUthmanic variants (Sanaa, Ibn Masʿud) are real differences in wording.

**"Verses were lost."** *Reply:* the classical category of abrogated recitation explains the stoning report within the tradition. *Limit:* to someone outside the tradition, that explanation can look like a way of covering a gap.

#### views — The range of views
- **Classical Muslim view** — the Quran was preserved by God through memory and writing; the ʿUthmanic text is the revealed text; readings are revealed variants; some recitation was abrogated by God.
- **Sadeghi and others** — the standard text goes back to the Prophet's (pbuh) time or very near it; the Sanaa lower text is an independent early tradition.
- **More sceptical historians** — the text was fixed later or with more change; Joseph Lumbard (*Huffington Post*, as cited by *Wikipedia*) argues that the Birmingham dates weaken this view; *Wikipedia* also reports opposing views.

### Questions readers ask next
**What did the burned codices contain, and why were they burned?** Other written copies; their full contents are unknown. ʿUthman ordered them burned after Hudhayfa warned that people would "differ about the Book" ({hadith:bukhari:4987}).

**What does a variant reading look like?** In the Sanaa lower text at 9:125, "filth" (*rijs*) instead of "disease" (*maraḍ*). Between Hafs and Warsh, differences such as "you know" / "they know".

**What does the Sanaa palimpsest show?** An early Quran text on parchment radiocarbon-dated to 578–669 CE (Sadeghi & Bergmann, via *Wikipedia*) — the date is the parchment's, not the writing's — with variants in wording and sura order.

**What is a consonantal text, and what are *qirāʾāt*?** The written skeleton of consonants; the accepted ways of vowelling and reciting it.

**How did oral transmission work?** Daily recitation by many people, alongside written copies; Zayd used both.

**What do BP, OxA-29418 and Hijazi script mean?** BP: years "before present", i.e. before 1950. Hijazi: the early script of western Arabia.

### Honest limits
"Not a letter has changed" is true of the ʿUthmanic text across the manuscripts; it is not true of every early copy. The Sanaa and Ibn Masʿud evidence was read through *Wikipedia*'s summaries of Sadeghi, Bergmann, Goudarzi and Harvey. Research on memory and oral transmission, and the classical explanations of Ibn Masʿud's omissions, have not yet been consulted.

### The question, at its strongest
"The hadith collections were compiled two centuries after the Prophet (pbuh). Western scholars like Joseph Schacht argued that many hadith were invented later to support legal positions, with chains of narrators added to make them look old. Even Muslim scholars admit there are thousands of fabricated hadith. Some 'authentic' ones contain things that seem morally wrong. Why not follow the Quran alone, as some Muslims in Türkiye and elsewhere now argue?"

### Why people ask it
Hadith are the source of much of Islamic law and practice, and of many of the hardest texts. In Türkiye, a "Quran-centred" movement (*Kuran İslamı* or *Kur'an Müslümanlığı*) has drawn growing interest "in recent years". The Hitit University study (2024), which looks at figures from 2000 onwards, calls it *Kur'an merkezli din söylemi* and *Kur'an Müslümanlığı*, and itself assesses its view as "hadis inkarı" (rejection of hadith).

### Short answer
A *hadith* is a report of what the Prophet (pbuh) said, did or approved; the *Sunna* is his way of life, known mainly through hadith and through the community's continuous practice. Early Muslims wrote down some hadith in the Prophet's (pbuh) lifetime — ʿAbdullah ibn ʿAmr (ra) was told, "Write" ({hadith:abudawud:3646}) — and later scholars built a detailed system for testing each report's chain (*isnād*) and text (*matn*). Western critics such as Schacht argued that chains were often added later; later Western scholars such as Harald Motzki developed a method that studies chains and texts together, and argued that the "common link" theory used by Schacht's successors is flawed. The Quran itself tells believers to obey the Messenger (4:59; 59:7) and says he was sent to explain the revelation (16:44), which is why mainstream Muslims hold that the Quran cannot be followed without the Sunna. The system is not infallible: scholars disagree about particular hadith, and a morally troubling *ṣaḥīḥ* hadith deserves careful study, not blind acceptance or easy dismissal.

### The full answer

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Obey God and the Messenger (4:59).** "O you who have believed, obey Allah and obey the Messenger and those in authority among you. And if you disagree over anything, refer it to Allah and the Messenger" (4:59), Medinan. The verse distinguishes obedience to God from obedience to the Messenger and names both.

**"Whatever the Messenger has given you — take" (59:7).** "And whatever the Messenger has given you — take; and what he has forbidden you — refrain from" (59:7). The verse's setting is the distribution of *fayʾ* (property gained without fighting); Akram Nadwi, among many others, treats it as general evidence for the Prophet's (pbuh) authority. That general use is a scholarly extension of a verse about a specific distribution.

**To make clear what was sent down (16:44).** "And We revealed to you the message that you may make clear to the people what was sent down to them and that they might give thought" (16:44), Meccan. The Prophet's (pbuh) role includes explaining the Quran.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Writing hadith in his lifetime.** ʿAbdullah ibn ʿAmr (ra) said he "used to write everything" he heard from the Prophet (pbuh), "to memorise it". The Quraysh objected that the Prophet (pbuh) "is a human being: he speaks in anger and pleasure", so he stopped and told the Prophet (pbuh), who pointed to his mouth and said: "Write, by Him in Whose hand my soul lies, only right comes out from it" ({hadith:abudawud:3646}; al-Albani: *ṣaḥīḥ*). Other reports discourage writing hadith early on; scholars reconcile them in different ways (not covered here).

#### scholars — How the system works
**Terms.** An *isnād* is "the chain of narration" — who heard it from whom; the *matn* is the text. A *ṣaḥīḥ* (sound) hadith has an unbroken chain in which "every narrator is known to be upright and precise in their transmission", with a text "free from hidden defects or irregularities". A *ḥasan* (good) hadith meets most criteria, but a narrator "may have slightly less precise memory". A *ḍaʿīf* (weak) hadith fails them, "typically because of a break in the chain, an unreliable narrator, or a textual irregularity". A *mawḍūʿ* (fabricated) hadith is "invented and falsely attributed to the Prophet" (ParallelQuran's summary). Scholars judged narrators using biographical dictionaries that recorded each person's teachers, students, reliability and memory.

**Bukhari and Muslim.** Al-Bukhari and Muslim each compiled a collection limited to reports they judged sound. How later scholars ranked these collections, and where they criticised particular reports in them, was not covered here.

**A worked example of grading.** In run 6, the hadith "Tie it and rely (upon Allah)" (al-Tirmidhi 2517) was called *gharīb* by al-Tirmidhi: he knew it from Anas only by this one route, and he noted a similar report from ʿAmr b. Umayya al-Damri. Yahya al-Qattan called it *munkar* (rejected); Zubair ʿAli Zaʾi graded it *ḥasan* (good); and Ibn Hibban (no. 731) has a version graded *ḥasan li-ghayrihi* ("good because of support from other routes") by al-Arnaʾut. So a familiar saying can rest on a disputed chain. By contrast, the three-outcomes hadith on du'a (Musnad Ahmad 11133) has a chain al-Arnaʾut called *jayyid* (good), al-Hakim authenticated, and a parallel in al-Adab al-Mufrad (no. 710; *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Adab al-Mufrad* 547) graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by al-Albani. Grading is done chain by chain, and agreement between independent chains strengthens a report.

#### history — Western scholarship
**Schacht's argument (outline only).** Joseph Schacht (*The Origins of Muhammadan Jurisprudence*, 1950) is the best-known Western critic of hadith chains. Neither his book nor a summary of it was read, so his thesis is given here only as the question above states it: that many legal hadith arose later and their chains were extended back to the Prophet (pbuh). Mansur Ali's review discusses the "common link" theory of G. H. A. Juynboll, the method Little (2022) also uses: the narrator on whom many chains of one hadith converge is treated as its likely originator.

**Motzki's response.** Harald Motzki developed the *isnād-cum-matn* method — "the chain of narrators should be studied in tandem with the text of a hadith" — first in his study of the *Muṣannaf* of ʿAbd al-Razzaq al-Sanʿani. Mansur Ali's review says Motzki's critique shows that Juynboll's common-link theory "nevertheless suffers from many methodological and epistemological flaws", while noting that accepting Motzki's conclusions "will depend on how much reliance one is willing to place on the chain of narrators". Motzki's own dating results have not yet been consulted.

**A live example: Aisha's age.** Joshua Little (2022) used the common-link method to argue that the report of Aisha's age at marriage "appears to have been created and disseminated" by Hisham ibn ʿUrwa in Iraq; Cheema (2024) replied that it is corroborated by chains not through Hisham, including al-Zuhri's in Muslim 1422c (see "Aisha's age at marriage"). The case shows both methods at work on one hadith.

#### reasoning — The Quran-only argument
**What the Quran-centred view holds.** The Hitit University study (2024) describes it as the belief that the Quran is complete, clear and self-sufficient — "the sole source of Islamic teaching" — so that other sources are unnecessary.

**The mainstream reply.** Nadwi argues that revelation includes the Prophet's (pbuh) guided words and acts; that the Quran commands obedience to him (4:59; 59:7); and that the Quran states general duties — prayer, zakāh, legal penalties — without the details, which the Sunna supplies; in his words, "Without the Sunnah, Islamic practice becomes unmoored from its interpretive anchor and is rendered vulnerable to personal reinterpretation, relativism, and sectarianism." An example of this page's own (it is not in Nadwi's article): the Quran commands prayer many times but does not describe how to perform it; the number of daily prayers and their form come from the Prophet's (pbuh) practice as transmitted by the community.

**Two different arguments.** The reliability of the *practice* (how Muslims pray, fast and give zakāh) rests on continuous transmission by whole communities, not on individual chains. The reliability of the *corpus* of individual hadith reports rests on the grading system. One can be more confident in the first than in every item of the second.

*Limit:* Quran-centred Muslims reply that 4:59 and 59:7 refer to the Prophet's (pbuh) authority in his lifetime, not to reports collected later. This is a real disagreement about how the Prophet's (pbuh) authority reaches later generations.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Compiled two centuries later."** *Reply:* the great collections are from the third Islamic century, but they drew on earlier written collections and on chains of teachers; writing began in the Prophet's (pbuh) lifetime ({hadith:abudawud:3646}). *Limit:* how much early writing survived into the collections is debated.

**"Thousands of fabrications exist."** *Reply:* yes, and Muslim scholars themselves identified and catalogued them; that is what the grading system is for. *Limit:* a system can catch many fabrications and still miss some.

**"What do I do with a *ṣaḥīḥ* hadith that seems morally wrong?"** *Reply:* first, check the text, context and translation — many difficulties dissolve. Second, ask whether the report describes a particular situation rather than a rule for all times (see the discussion of al-Qarafi and Ibn ʿAshur on "Aisha's age at marriage"). Third, scholars have sometimes judged a report's text problematic despite a strong chain; that is a recognised, though cautious, part of the tradition. *Limit:* where the tradition has accepted a report and its meaning, a reader may still find it hard, and the honest answer is to say so.

#### views — The range of views
- **Mainstream Sunni scholarship** — the Sunna is binding; graded hadith are its main record; *ṣaḥīḥ* collections are highly reliable.
- **Schacht, Juynboll** — many hadith, especially legal ones, arose in the second century.
- **Motzki** — chains and texts must be studied together; the common-link theory is methodologically flawed (as reported by Mansur Ali; Motzki's own dating results have not yet been consulted).
- **Quran-centred Muslims (e.g. *Kur'an Müslümanlığı* in Türkiye)** — the Quran alone is the source; hadith are not binding.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is the Aisha marital-age hadith authentic?** By classical standards, yes; Little's thesis argues it originated with one narrator in Iraq, and Cheema replies with corroborating chains. See "Aisha's age at marriage".

**How did the testing system work?** Scholars examined every narrator's reliability and memory using biographical records, checked that the chain was connected, and compared versions of the text.

**What do I do with a *ṣaḥīḥ* hadith that seems morally wrong?** Check text and context; ask whether it is situational; study how scholars have read it; and be honest if it remains hard.

**What is a hadith, and how is it different from the Quran?** A hadith is a report about the Prophet (pbuh); the Quran is, for Muslims, God's own word. Hadith are graded; the Quran is not.

**What are Bukhari and Muslim?** Two hadith collections, each limited to reports its compiler judged sound.

**What is the Sunna?** The Prophet's (pbuh) way: his words, acts and approvals, known through hadith and community practice.

### Honest limits
Schacht's and Juynboll's books, Motzki's own results, and research on human memory and oral transmission have not yet been consulted. Classical criticism of particular hadith in Bukhari and Muslim (for example by al-Daraqutni) was not covered. Named figures of the Turkish Quran-centred movement were not identified in the source read.

## Q12 — Are "scientific miracles" in the Quran real?

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 3:190, 2:2, 21:30, 51:47, 23:12–14.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English), on 51:47, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-47-sora-51.html, and on 21:30, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-30-sora-21.html.
- "Scientists' Comments On The Qur'an", Islamic Awareness, https://islamic-awareness.org/quran/science/scientists — Keith Moore's statements (a source that promotes the claims).
- Muhammad Salim Abu ʿAsi, *Quranic Sciences According to al-Shatibi in His Book al-Muwafaqat* (Dar al-Basaʾir, 2005), via usul.ai, https://usul.ai/es/t/quranic-sciences-according-to-al-shatibi-in-his-book-al-muwafaqat/49.
- Youssef Chouhoud, "What Causes Muslims to Doubt Islam? A Quantitative Analysis", Yaqeen Institute (2018; updated 2024), https://yaqeeninstitute.org/read/paper/what-causes-muslims-to-doubt-islam-a-quantitative-analysis.
- Divlekci, "Bâkıllânî'nin İʿcâz Anlayışı", *EKEV Akademi Dergisi* 23/80 (2019), pp. 73–94 — read by the independent source check.

### The question, at its strongest
"Videos say the Quran predicted embryology, the Big Bang, the expanding universe, even the speed of light. If that were true it would be strong evidence of divine origin. But when I check, the claims depend on stretching Arabic words, ignoring what commentators said for a thousand years, and announcing the 'prediction' only after scientists made the discovery. If the Quran is from God, why would it need such arguments — and if they collapse, what is left?"

### Why people ask it
The genre (*al-iʿjāz al-ʿilmī*, "scientific miraculousness") is popular on YouTube and in some institutions. Many young people meet it first, believe it, and then feel misled when a claim collapses.

### Short answer
The Quran calls nature full of "signs" for reflection (3:190) and calls itself "guidance" (2:2); it does not present itself as a science textbook. Many modern claims that it "predicted" discoveries read new meanings into words that classical commentators understood differently: Ibn Kathir, for example, read "We are [its] expander" (51:47) as God having made the sky vast, and he reports Ibn ʿAbbas reading 21:30's "joined entity" as the sky not yet raining and the earth not yet growing plants (Ibn Kathir's own gloss: "one piece… piled up") — not as the Big Bang. Some descriptions harmonise well with what we know, and can be reflected on honestly, as this site does on its Reflect pages; but "harmony" is not "prediction". The classical case for the Quran's inimitability centred on its composition; al-Baqillani's three aspects (composition, reports of the unseen, the Prophet's being *ummī*) do not include science (Divlekci 2019). Dropping overstated claims costs little: in a 2018 survey of Muslim Americans, the issues that most often triggered doubt were moral and social, not scientific.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the one whose faith was shaken
**If a "miracle" you believed in turned out to be false, you were not foolish, and your faith was not foolish either.** You trusted people who presented a claim with confidence. The Quran never asked you to believe it because of modern science; it asks you to reflect, and it makes its own case.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Signs for reflection (3:190).** "Indeed, in the creation of the heavens and the earth and the alternation of the night and the day are signs for those of understanding" (3:190). Nature is a sign pointing to God, to be reflected on.

**A book of guidance (2:2).** "This is the Book about which there is no doubt, a guidance for those conscious of Allah" (2:2). Its stated purpose is guidance.

#### scholars — A worked example: 21:30 and 51:47
**The verses.** "Have those who disbelieved not considered that the heavens and the earth were a joined entity, and We separated them and made from water every living thing?" (21:30), Meccan. "And the heaven We constructed with strength, and indeed, We are [its] expander" (51:47), Meccan.

**The modern claim.** That 21:30 describes the Big Bang (a single original state, separated) and 51:47 the expanding universe.

**Classical tafsir.** Ibn Kathir reports Ibn ʿAbbas on 21:30: "the heavens were joined together and it did not rain, and the earth was joined together and nothing grew", until "rain came forth from the heavens and vegetation came forth from the earth"; al-Hasan and Qatada: "They were joined together, then they were separated by this air"; and his own gloss: "in the beginning they were all one piece… piled up on top of one another", then separated. On 51:47 he explains "We constructed… with strength" with Ibn ʿAbbas, Mujahid, Qatada and al-Thawri, and *la-mūsiʿūn* ("expander") as: "We made it vast and We brought its roof higher without pillars to support it."

**Assessment.** The words *ratq* (joined) and *fatq* (separated) and *mūsiʿūn* (making wide, or having the power to make wide) can be read as compatible with modern cosmology, and that compatibility can be a prompt for reflection. But Ibn Kathir, and the early authorities he reports, do not read these verses as describing an expanding universe or an initial singularity; the modern readings arose after the science. That is the pattern critics point to. So the honest statement is: these verses *can be read in harmony with* modern cosmology; they do not *predict* it.

#### scholars — Embryology
**The claim.** That 23:12–14 — "a sperm-drop in a firm lodging… a clinging clot… a lump [of flesh]… bones… covered the bones with flesh" — matches modern embryology so exactly that it must be from God. Its best-known supporter was the Canadian anatomist Keith Moore, who is quoted as saying he had worked "for the past three years" with an embryology committee at King Abdulaziz University in Jeddah. With Abdul-Majeed al-Zindani, named there as Director of the Project of Scientific Miracles at that university, he produced a special edition of his textbook, *The Developing Human*, "with Islamic Additions" (Saunders, 1982; Dar Al-Qiblah, 1983). He is quoted as saying the Quranic descriptions "cannot be based on scientific knowledge in the seventh century" (Islamic Awareness, a site that promotes the claims).

**Assessment.** The stages named in 23:14 are the same words the site's sign page on the embryo (S4) examines, with their meanings and the classical readings. The current S4 page says the historian of science Joseph Needham noted parallels with Galen's Greek medicine; that has not yet been checked in Needham's text. The fair position is the site's general one: describe accurately, note what harmonises, and flag overclaims.

**The speed of light.** A popular claim derives the speed of light from 32:5 and the Moon's orbit. No source assessing it was read, so this page makes no judgement on it.

#### scholars — Classical and modern scholars
**Al-Shatibi and the Arabs' usage.** Muhammad Salim Abu ʿAsi, in his study of the Andalusian jurist al-Shatibi's *al-Muwāfaqāt*, writes in his own words that "the whole Quran was revealed in the established usage of the Arabs, without doubt" (translated). He says this in a section on why tafsir is still needed: Arabic words carry literal, figurative and implied meanings. The passage read says nothing about reading later sciences into the Quran.

**Classical *iʿjāz*.** According to Divlekci (2019), al-Baqillani names three aspects of the Quran's inimitability: its reports of the unseen ("gâipten haberler"), the Prophet's (pbuh) being *ummī*, and the uniqueness of its composition (*naẓm*).

**Modern critics.** Guessoum's 2008 article in *Zygon* could not be opened (blocked), so his arguments are not summarised here.

#### research — What research shows
**Science is not what most often triggers doubt.** [Moderate; one US sample.] In Chouhoud's survey of 630 Muslim Americans (2017), "the top four issues all come from the Moral and Social Concerns family of doubts", led by "The way that [some] insist there is only one 'right' way to practice faith", while "each of the four items that least trigger doubt are directly related to doctrinal belief". The exact rank of the science item (evolution and creation) was not given in the text read.

#### reasoning — If the claims go, what is left?
**The Quran's own case.** The Quran points to its language and its challenge (2:23), to the character of the messenger (10:16), to the coherence of its message, and to the signs in nature as prompts for reflection (3:190) — not as hidden proofs. These are the arguments on "Why believe Muhammad was a prophet?".

**How apparent conflicts are handled.** If it describes nature as people saw it (the sun "setting", for example), that is the language of appearance, not a scientific claim. Ibn Rushd argued that demonstrated truth cannot contradict revelation, and that apparent conflicts call for interpretation (see "Knowledge and seeking it" for his *Decisive Treatise*).

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Without the miracles, there is no evidence."** *Reply:* the classical case for inimitability did not rest on them: al-Baqillani's three aspects are composition, reports of the unseen and the Prophet's (pbuh) being *ummī* (Divlekci 2019). *Limit:* for people who came to faith through these claims, losing them can still feel like losing the ground.

**"Some harmonies are striking."** *Reply:* they can be noted and reflected on, as harmonies. *Limit:* critics will still say any rich text can be made to fit many things after the fact.

**"Muslim institutions still promote *iʿjāz ʿilmī*."** *Reply:* yes; the view on this page is a scholarly position, not a consensus.

#### views — The range of views
- **Supporters** — Zaghloul El-Naggar, Keith Moore (1982/83 edition), Tantawi Jawhari.
- **Critics** — Amin al-Khuli, Mahmud Shaltut, Nidhal Guessoum.
- **This site** — verses as signs for reflection; harmony noted honestly, prediction not claimed.

### Questions readers ask next
**Which popular claims hold up?** None that was checked here holds up as a *prediction*. Some verses can be read in harmony with modern science (21:30; 51:47); classical commentators read them differently.

**If I drop these claims, what reason is left?** The Quran's own case: its language, its messenger, its message and its call to reflection.

**Which verses "harmonise with science"?** 21:30 and 51:47 can be read in harmony with cosmology, and 23:12–14 with embryology, as discussed on the site's sign pages — with the caveats above.

**What is tafsir, and why does classical tafsir matter here?** Tafsir is Quranic commentary. Classical tafsir shows how the words were understood before modern science, which is the test of whether a "prediction" is in the text or read into it.

**What does it mean that classical *iʿjāz* centred on composition?** That al-Baqillani located the Quran's inimitability chiefly in its composition (*naẓm*), alongside its reports of the unseen and the Prophet's (pbuh) being *ummī* (Divlekci 2019).

**What does al-Shatibi say about the first audience?** His own text has not yet been consulted. Abu ʿAsi, writing about him, says the Quran was revealed "in the established usage of the Arabs", in a discussion of why tafsir is still needed.

### Honest limits
Guessoum's article, al-Shatibi's own text, the classical *iʿjāz* works, critiques of the embryology claim, and any assessment of the speed-of-light claim have not yet been consulted. The doubt survey is of one American sample.
