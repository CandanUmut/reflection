## Q3 — Why believe the Creator is involved (deism)?

### The question, at its strongest
"I can follow the argument that the universe needs a cause. But a First Cause is not a God who chooses a man in seventh-century Arabia, dictates a book through him, and then waits to hear my prayers. Every religion says God spoke to *it*, and the messages contradict each other. If God wanted to be known, He could make Himself plain to everyone, yet millions of honest, open people never find Him. And the people who claim to speak for God are often the least trustworthy people I know. So the most honest position is the smallest one: a Creator, yes; a God who speaks, no."

### Why people ask it
Many people who keep belief in God but drop religion are reacting to what they saw in religious people, more than to an argument. Others reach the same place through philosophy: reason seems to reach a Creator but not a revelation. In Türkiye, a 2018 workshop of religion teachers named deism as prominent among young people with problems of belief, and it became a national debate.

### Short answer
Deism keeps a Creator but denies that He reveals Himself or acts in the world. The Muslim case against stopping there has three steps. First, the Creator we infer from an ordered, life-permitting world is not only powerful but purposeful; a purposeful Creator of beings who ask "why?" has reason to answer them. Second, whether He has answered is a question of evidence, and al-Ghazali argued that a prophet is known the way a doctor is known: from many signs in his conduct and teaching taken together, not from one wonder. Third, God's partial hiddenness and the failures of religious people are real problems, but they do not show that God is silent. None of these steps compels belief. They show that deism is not the only reasonable stopping point, and perhaps not the most stable one.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If you left practice because of what religious people did, your anger may be justified.** The Konya teachers' own report in 2018 described young people turning to deism, "the rejection of God's intervention in life", and said the question they most often asked was why God "does not intervene in evil on earth and remains silent" (*İktibas*, 2018). Hypocrisy and abuse by religious people are moral failures, and the Quran itself condemns them harshly (see the page "If religious people behave like this, why should I believe?").

**You can trust God without trusting every institution that speaks in His name.** The question this page asks is not "are religious people good?" but "has God spoken?". Those are different questions, and the second deserves to be asked on its own terms. Al-Ghazali, whose story is told below, spent years doubting the scholars of his own time before he found certainty.

**A pause is not the end of the road.** Many people stay in a "Creator but not religion" position for a while. That is a place to think, not a verdict.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The Quran argues from creation to guidance.** When Pharaoh asked Moses (pbuh) who his Lord was, he answered: "Our Lord is He who gave each thing its form and then guided [it]" (20:50). The verse is from Sura Ta-Ha, a Meccan sura; it is Moses' reply to Pharaoh (20:49). The same pairing appears in Sura al-Aʿla: "Who created and proportioned / And who destined and [then] guided" (87:2–3). The words do not, by themselves, argue that God must send prophets; they describe a Creator whose work includes guidance. Reading them as an argument against deism is a modern use of the verses.

**The Quran ties messengers to justice.** "We have already sent Our messengers with clear evidences and sent down with them the Scripture and the balance that the people may maintain [their affairs] in justice" (57:25). This Medinan verse names a purpose for revelation: justice among people.

**It describes a human nature turned toward God.** "So direct your face toward the religion, inclining to truth. [Adhere to] the fitrah of Allah upon which He has created [all] people" (30:30), from the Meccan Sura al-Rum, addressed to the Prophet (pbuh) and through him to the listener. The Prophet (pbuh) said: "Every child is born with a true faith of Islam (i.e. to worship none but Allah Alone) and his parents convert him to Judaism or Christianity or Magianism" ({hadith:bukhari:1385}).

**It offers its own text as evidence, and invites testing.** "And if you are in doubt about what We have sent down upon Our Servant [Muhammad], then produce a surah the like thereof" (2:23), from Sura al-Baqara, Medinan, addressed to those who doubt. Whether this challenge has force is debated outside the Muslim tradition; the point here is that the Quran asks to be judged, not simply accepted.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**What deism is.** *Encyclopaedia Britannica* defines deism as "natural religion": accepting religious knowledge "that is inborn in every person or that can be acquired by the use of reason", and rejecting religious knowledge acquired through "revelation or the teaching of any church". It differs from atheism (no God at all) and from Islamic theism, in which God creates, sustains every moment of existence, and speaks through prophets.

**The kalam argument from grace (luṭf).** Kalam is Islamic rational theology. The Muʿtazila (an early rationalist school) and later Shiʿi (Imami) theologians held that *luṭf* — God's act that "motivates a person to obedience and keeps him away from disobedience" — is required of God, and they counted both moral obligation (*taklīf*) and the sending of prophets (*irsāl al-rusul*) as instances of it. Their argument ran: sending prophets is an act of *luṭf*; *luṭf* is required of God; so sending prophets is required of God. Shiʿi theologians such as Shaykh al-Mufid and Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī (in *Tajrīd al-Iʿtiqād*) developed this (Meghjee).

**The Ashʿari answer: prophecy is a gift, not a debt.** The Ashʿaris, the largest Sunni school of theology, rejected the claim that anything is required *of* God, since nothing outside God governs His acts. For them God was not obliged to send prophets; He chose to. Meghjee cites al-Ghazali's *al-Iqtiṣād fī al-Iʿtiqād* for this view. Both sides agreed that prophets were in fact sent; they disagreed on whether reason can show that they *had* to be.

**Al-Ghazali: from inherited belief to certainty.** Al-Ghazali (d. 1111), the most influential Sunni theologian of his age, describes in *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal* (*Deliverance from Error*) how his search began: "I saw that Christian youths always grew up to be Christians, Jewish youths to be Jews and Muslim youths to be Muslims", and he recalled the hadith that everyone is born with "a sound nature" (Watt, section I). He went through a period of radical doubt lasting "almost two months", which ended not by argument but by "a light which God most high cast into my breast" (section II). On prophecy, he argues that you know a prophet the way you know that someone is a physician or a lawyer: from his conduct and his work. "Certainty can only be reached by acquaintance with his conduct", he writes; seek certainty "about the prophetic office, and not from the transformation of a rod into a serpent". By testing the Prophet's teaching in many instances, "you will arrive at a necessary knowledge beyond all doubt" (Watt, passage before section V). This is a cumulative argument: no single sign settles it, but many together can.

**Nursi: the alternatives to a Maker.** Said Nursi (d. 1960), the Turkish scholar whose *Risale-i Nur* is widely read, argues that when we see a living thing we have only a few explanations: it formed itself, causes produced it, nature required it, or it was made. Against self-formation he writes that "each particle or atom forming your body would have to have an eye to see your whole body". Against blind causes: "How can that which is deaf, blind, and unconscious bestow hearing, sight, and consciousness on something else?" Against "nature": "Nature cannot create because it is something supposed to exist" — for him "nature" is a name for an order, not a maker. His conclusion is that each creature points to one conscious Maker. Nursi's word *inayet* (Arabic *ʿināya*), "care" or "providence", names what he sees in that order: not only design but attention to each thing's needs. His argument reaches a caring Maker; it does not by itself reach revelation, which he defends elsewhere.

#### science — What science says
**Some features of physics look finely balanced for life.** [Established for the numbers; Debated for what they mean.] The SEP entry by Simon Friederich gives examples. Had the strong nuclear force been "stronger by more than about 50 %", nearly all hydrogen would have burned in the early universe. Had the weak force been "weaker by a factor of about 10", the early universe would have had far more neutrons. The size of the density variations in the early universe, Q, is "roughly 2·10⁻⁵". The mismatch between the observed and the theoretically expected cosmological constant is "between 10⁵⁰ and 10¹²³", depending on assumptions. Roger Penrose estimated that the universe's initial low-entropy state occupies "one part in 10^(10^123)" of the possible states.

**What the numbers do and do not show.** The design argument says such conditions are very improbable without a designer. Two main replies: the *observation-selection* reply (Elliott Sober: we could only observe a universe compatible with our existence), and the *multiverse* reply (if there are many universes, some will be life-friendly). Critics of the multiverse reply, following Ian Hacking and Roger White, call it an "inverse gambler's fallacy": other universes do not make it more likely that *this* one is life-friendly; defenders such as Epstein dispute that (Friederich, §§3–4). Fine-tuning is a reason to take a purposeful Creator seriously. It is not evidence that this Creator speaks.

#### reasoning — How philosophers argue about it
**From a purposeful Creator to a speaking one.** The step that deism refuses is this: if the Creator made beings who can understand, choose and ask about their purpose, then leaving them with no word about that purpose would be strange for a Creator who acts with wisdom. This is close to the Muʿtazili *luṭf* argument above, and to what the Maturidi and Hanbali traditions call God's wisdom (*ḥikma*). *Limit:* the Ashʿari tradition itself denies that reason can show what God must do; the step is a reasonable expectation, not a proof.

**What would count as evidence of revelation.** Following al-Ghazali, the evidence is cumulative: the messenger's character before and after his claim; what his teaching does to people who live it; whether the message holds together and corrects earlier error; the survival and transmission of the text (see "Why Islam and not another religion?"). *Limit:* each strand can be disputed, and people weigh them differently.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"If God wanted to be known, He would not hide."** J. L. Schellenberg's argument, as the SEP states it: "There are people who are capable of relating personally to God but who, through no fault of their own, fail to believe. If there is a personal God who is unsurpassably great, then there are no such people. So, there is no such God." A *nonresistant nonbeliever* is someone who could relate to God, does not believe, and is not resisting God. *Reply:* the SEP lists several replies. One is that a world where God is obvious would remove the freedom and moral growth that a test needs (John Hick, Richard Swinburne). The Quran describes life as exactly such a test: God "created death and life to test you [as to] which of you is best in deed" (67:2). Another reply denies that there are truly nonresistant nonbelievers (Green, Wainwright), which fits the Islamic idea of an inborn disposition (*fiṭra*, 30:30). A third is "skeptical theism": we are not in a position to know whether God has reasons we cannot see (Howard-Snyder; McBrayer and Swenson). And the Quran says no one is punished for a message that did not reach them: "never would We punish until We sent a messenger" (17:15). *Limit:* Schellenberg replies that a loving relationship with God could develop alongside these goods, so the argument is not defeated, only contested. The "nonresistant nonbelievers do not exist" reply is the hardest to defend, because it says something about many sincere people that we cannot check.

**"Religions contradict each other, so none of them is from God."** *Reply:* disagreement shows that not all can be right, not that none is. Islam's own account expects this: it presents earlier revelations as genuine but changed in transmission, and itself as their confirmation (5:48). Whether that account is true is the question of the page "Why Islam and not another religion?". *Limit:* every tradition can tell a similar story about the others; the comparison has to be made on evidence.

**"Religious people are hypocrites, so their God is not real."** *Reply:* the behaviour of believers is evidence about believers. It is weak evidence about whether God has spoken, in the same way that a corrupt doctor is weak evidence against medicine. *Limit:* a religion that claims to transform people should show it in many of them; if it rarely does, that counts against it.

#### views — The range of views
- **Muʿtazila and Imami theologians** (e.g. Shaykh al-Mufid; Naṣīr al-Dīn al-Ṭūsī, *Tajrīd al-Iʿtiqād*) — sending prophets is required of God as *luṭf* (Meghjee).
- **Ashʿaris** (e.g. al-Ghazali, *al-Iqtiṣād*) — prophecy is God's free gift; reason cannot oblige God (Meghjee).
- **Al-Ghazali** (*al-Munqidh*) — prophecy is known by cumulative acquaintance with the prophet's conduct and teaching, not by a single miracle.
- **Said Nursi** (*Risale-i Nur*) — the order of creation points to one caring Maker (*inayet*).
- **Schellenberg** — divine hiddenness counts against a perfectly loving God (SEP).
- **Hick, Swinburne, Howard-Snyder, Green** — various replies to hiddenness (SEP).

### Questions readers ask next
**What exactly is deism?** The belief that a Creator exists but does not reveal Himself or intervene; religious truth comes from reason alone (*Britannica*). Islam agrees that reason can reach a Creator, but holds that God also speaks through prophets.

**Is the "deism wave" in Türkiye real?** Partly. In Zübeyir Nişancı's survey (1,942 adults, 35 provinces), 94.3% said they believe in God, but among 18–24-year-olds 11% said they do not, against 2% of over-65s; and 11% of 18–24-year-olds felt close to the label "deist". Nişancı himself said the debate about deism spreading is "in some ways artificial" (VOA Türkçe). Feeling close to a label is not the same as holding deist beliefs.

**Is this only a Turkish question?** No. In a Pew survey of US adults (December 2017), 56% believed in God "as described in the Bible", and 33% in "another type of higher power or spiritual force" — a large group that believes in something without a revealed religion.

**What did al-Ghazali say convinced him of prophecy?** Not a single miracle but long acquaintance with the Prophet's conduct and teaching, tested in practice, until he reached "a necessary knowledge beyond all doubt" (*al-Munqidh*).

**Can I believe in God and still be angry at religious people?** Yes. The question of God and the question of religious institutions are different. The page on religious hypocrisy takes up the second.


### Honest limits
Arguments for a Creator are strong for many people but not decisive; the SEP entries record serious replies to each. The step from "a Creator" to "a Creator who speaks" depends on an expectation about God's wisdom that the Ashʿari tradition itself says reason cannot establish. The hiddenness argument remains open. Whether a particular revelation is genuine has to be weighed on evidence that people judge differently.

## Q32 — Is deism a stable position?

- Pew Research Center, "Key findings about Americans' belief in God" (2018).

### The question, at its strongest
"I am not confused and I am not angry. I simply think deism is the most honest position. A Creator explains why there is something rather than nothing. But every claimed revelation contains things a good God would not say, the revelations contradict each other, and God has left the question so unclear that sincere people disagree everywhere. Calling deism 'unstable' is just a way of pressuring me to choose a religion. Why can't 'a Creator, and nothing more' be a place to live?"

### Why people ask it
Religious families and teachers often treat deism as a phase that ends in atheism unless someone intervenes, and deists hear that as pressure. In 2018 a workshop of Turkish religion teachers reported deism as prominent among young people with problems of belief, and the claim became a national debate. The same question exists wherever people believe in "a higher power" but not in a religion.

### Short answer
Deism can be held for a lifetime, and some people do. It is "unstable" in a narrower sense: it keeps a Creator who is wise and good enough to make an ordered world, but then assumes this Creator has nothing to say to the beings who ask about Him. Muslim theologians argued in different ways that a wise God would guide those He created, and the Quran says that denying revelation is a failure to "appraise Allah with true appraisal" (6:91). Historically, English deism had its high point between about 1689 and 1742 and then declined as a movement, though the reasons are debated. In Türkiye, survey data suggest that many who feel close to the "deist" label are protesting religious people more than holding a worked-out philosophy. Patience and honest conversation help more than pressure.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If you are a deist, you are not in "no relationship" with God.** You already believe that He made you. That belief carries questions with it — gratitude, purpose, what He might want — and you can keep asking them.

**If someone you love has become a deist, do not treat them as lost.** Listen to why. In the Konya teachers' report, the most-asked question was why God "does not intervene in evil on earth and remains silent" (*İktibas*, 2018). That is a real question; a lecture will not answer it. The pages on suffering and on religious hypocrisy may help you understand what they are carrying. Keep the relationship warm; the Quran tells even the Prophet (pbuh) that compelling belief is not his task (10:99, on the page "Is claiming one religion is true arrogant?").

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The Quran calls denial of revelation a failure to value God rightly.** "And they did not appraise Allah with true appraisal when they said, 'Allah did not reveal to a human being anything.' Say, 'Who revealed the Scripture that Moses brought as light and guidance to the people?'" (6:91). The verse is in Sura al-Anʿam, a Meccan sura. Ibn Kathir reports that Ibn ʿAbbas, Mujahid and ʿAbdullah ibn Kathir said it was revealed about the Quraysh (the Prophet's Meccan opponents), and that "it was also said" to be about some Jews. He reads the phrase as meaning they "did not estimate Allah with an estimation due to Him" when they denied that He sends anything down; he gives no explicit preference between the two occasions. The verse's own argument is that revelation has already happened (the Torah of Moses), so a blanket denial ignores evidence.

**The Quran addresses people who already accept a Creator.** "If you asked them, 'Who created the heavens and earth and subjected the sun and the moon?' they would surely say, 'Allah.' Then how are they deluded?" (29:61); "…but most of them do not reason" (29:63). These Meccan verses address the Quraysh, who believed in God as Creator but rejected His messenger. The Quran's complaint is not that they lack a Creator but that they stop there. Reading this as a description of modern deists is a modern application.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**The classical argument from grace and wisdom.** The Muʿtazila and later Shiʿi theologians held that God's grace (*luṭf*) — what moves people toward obedience without forcing them — is required of Him, and that sending prophets is an instance of it; Shaykh al-Mufid also grounded this in God's wisdom, since "the objective of creation is predicated on it" (Meghjee). The Ashʿaris denied that anything is required of God; al-Ghazali's *al-Iqtiṣād* argues that God sends prophets freely, not by obligation (Meghjee). The Maturidi position, which stresses God's wisdom (*ḥikma*), could not be checked for this page (see Gaps).

**What the argument shows.** All schools agree that a wise God *did* send prophets. Only the Muʿtazili and Shiʿi line says reason shows He *had to*. For a deist, the useful point is the shared one: a Creator who acts with wisdom is not a Creator who is indifferent to the creatures He gave reason.

#### history — History
**English deism.** Lord Herbert of Cherbury's *De Veritate* (1624) is usually called the first major statement of deism in English (Wikipedia). *Britannica* gives his five religious ideas "recognized as God-given and innate": a supreme being, the duty to worship him, a virtuous life as the best worship, repentance for sins, and rewards and punishments after death. John Toland's *Christianity Not Mysterious* (1696) stressed "the rational element in natural religion", and Matthew Tindal's *Christianity as Old as the Creation* (1730) became the centre of the controversy (*Britannica*; Wikipedia). *Britannica* puts "the high point of Deist thought" in England "from about 1689 through 1742"; by 1754–56 the controversy "had passed its peak". It notes that deism then "took deep root in 18th-century Germany". In France, Voltaire, who lived in England in 1726–1728, became the best-known deist (Wikipedia).

**Why it declined is debated.** Wikipedia, citing the historian Peter Gay, describes deism as entering "slow decline as a recognizable movement in the 1730s", and lists reasons attributed to Mossner: the growing influence of naturalism and materialism; Hume and Kant questioning reason's ability to answer metaphysical questions; the violence of the French Revolution; and Christian revival movements such as Pietism and Methodism. It also says deist ideas fed into liberal theology and Unitarianism. *Limit:* these are reasons given by historians, from a secondary summary; they show that deism lost ground, not that it was refuted. Some reasons push toward atheism, others toward revealed religion.

**Türkiye as one example.** On 4 March 2018, about 50 religion teachers met in Konya, organised by the İKDAM Education Association and the International Pioneer Educators Association. Their closing statement said that among young people with problems of belief, deism — "the rejection of God's intervention in life" — "is especially prominent" (*İktibas*). Later survey data gave a more careful picture. In Zübeyir Nişancı's *Sayılarla Türkiye'de İnanç ve Dindarlık* (1,942 respondents, 35 provinces), 94.3% said they believe in God; 11% of 18–24-year-olds said they do not (2% of over-65s); and 11% of 18–24-year-olds felt close to the label "deist" (4% of over-65s). Nişancı called the debate about deism spreading "in some ways artificial" (VOA Türkçe). KONDA's survey (6,137 people; reported 31 May 2025) found "believer but not religious" rising from 31% in 2008 to 34%, "religious" falling from 55% to 46%, and "atheist or non-believer" rising from 2% to 8% (*Paraanaliz*).

**Elsewhere.** In a Pew survey of US adults (December 2017), 56% believed in God "as described in the Bible", 33% in "another type of higher power or spiritual force", and about one in ten in neither; 72% of the religiously unaffiliated believed in "a higher power of some kind".

#### reasoning — How philosophers argue about it
**Why "a Creator, and nothing more" is under pressure from both sides.** From the atheist side: if God never acts or speaks, what work does belief in Him do? From the theist side: the reasons that support a Creator — order, fine-tuning, the existence of minds — support a Creator who is purposeful, and purpose makes communication likely. The deist must say why the Creator cared enough to make minds, but not enough to address them. *Limit:* a deist can answer that a Creator's purposes may simply be beyond us — but that is close to the "skeptical theism" that believers use, and it weakens the deist's own objection to revelation.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Calling deism unstable is just pressure."** *Reply:* the claim here is not that deists are confused, but that the position faces questions from both directions, as above. *Limit:* the history of deism's decline shows social change as much as argument; it does not show that any individual deist is wrong.

**"Revelations contain things a good God would not say."** *Reply:* this is the strongest deist argument, and it has to be met case by case, as the site's pages on hard questions try to do (slavery, war, women, apostasy, Hell). *Limit:* some of those answers are partial, and the pages say so.

**"God's hiddenness shows He does not communicate."** *Reply:* see the replies to Schellenberg on "Why believe the Creator is involved?". The Quran presents revelation as a test that can be refused, not as overwhelming proof. *Limit:* the hiddenness argument remains a live debate in philosophy (SEP).

#### views — The range of views
- **Muʿtazila, Imami theologians (Shaykh al-Mufid, al-Ṭūsī)** — reason shows God sends prophets (*luṭf*) (Meghjee).
- **Ashʿaris (al-Ghazali, *al-Iqtiṣād*)** — God sends prophets freely; reason cannot oblige Him (Meghjee).
- **Historians (Gay; Mossner, as summarised)** — deism declined as a movement from the 1730s for several reasons, intellectual and social.
- **Nişancı** — much Turkish "deism" is closeness to a label rather than a settled belief.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is deism the same as atheism?** No. Deists believe in a Creator; atheists do not. Deists and Muslims share belief in a Creator; they differ on revelation.

**Do deists usually become atheists?** No reliable data was found on what happens to individual deists over time. The surveys measure numbers at one moment, not paths.

**What did the Konya teachers actually say?** That among young people with problems of belief, deism — rejecting God's intervention in life — was especially prominent, and that the most-asked question was why God stays silent before evil (*İktibas*, 2018).

**How should I talk to a family member who is a deist?** Ask what brought them there and listen; separate questions about God from complaints about people; do not threaten. The Quran reminds the Prophet (pbuh) that compulsion is not his task (10:99).

**Am I still in a relationship with God as a deist?** You believe He made you; that is already a relationship of creature to Creator. Gratitude and honest questions are ways of continuing it.

**Why did English deism fade?** Historians give several reasons — naturalism, Hume's and Kant's critiques, the French Revolution, religious revival. They disagree on the weight of each.

### Honest limits
Some people remain deists for life, and nothing here shows they are irrational. The "instability" argument is philosophical and depends on what one thinks God's wisdom implies; the Ashʿari tradition itself denies that reason can establish what God must do. The history of deism's decline was taken from secondary summaries. The Turkish figures come from one survey whose method has been criticised (see the earlier research, Batch 2, A5), and no data were found on individual paths over time.

## Q28 — Why Islam and not another religion?

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 2:285, 29:46, 5:48, 3:85, 2:62, 15:9, 2:23, 112:1.
- Sahih Muslim 153 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/muslim:153.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 4987 (sunnah.com, Book 66, Hadith 9) — the Uthmanic copies.
- "Sanaa manuscript", *Wikipedia*, https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sanaa_manuscript — summary of Sadeghi & Bergmann (2010), Sadeghi & Goudarzi (2012), Hilali, Puin (secondary; see Gaps).
- "Qur'an fragment Tübingen University Library Ma VI 165", *Medieval.eu* (11 November 2014), https://www.medieval.eu/sensational-fragment-early-quran-identified/ — the Tübingen radiocarbon date.
- Green and Howard-Snyder, "Hiddenness of God", SEP; Basinger, "Religious Diversity (Pluralism)", SEP (substantively revised 21 February 2025), https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/religious-pluralism/.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir*, on 10:99 and 109:6 (quran.com) — for the respect owed to others.
- The site's earlier research, Batch A (`docs/research/07-ask-deep-A.md`), for Mohammad Hassan Khalil's *Islam and the Fate of Others* (OUP, 2012) as summarised by E. Bazzano's review (*AJIS* 30:1) — used only as a pointer.

### The question, at its strongest
"Every religion has holy people, sacred texts, miracles reported by followers, and believers who are sure. Christians say their scripture is preserved and their founder is historical too. Muslims say the Bible was changed — but early Qurans also show variants, and Uthman burned the other copies. 'Islam is the continuation of all the prophets' is what Islam says about itself; every newer religion says that. If I apply the same standards to all of them, why would Islam come out on top — and if I had been born in Chennai, wouldn't I be saying the same about Hinduism?"

### Why people ask it
People meet friends of other faiths, read critical material online, or notice that their own belief came with their family. Students want the actual arguments, not slogans. Outsiders want to know what Islam claims about other religions, concretely.

### Short answer
Islam does not claim to be a new religion. It claims to be the same message — the worship of one God and right action — that all earlier prophets brought, now given in a final, preserved form (2:285; 5:48). Concretely, it honours Moses and Jesus (pbuh) as prophets, but denies that Jesus is divine and holds that earlier scriptures were changed in transmission. The case for Islam over other faiths rests on criteria that can be stated: the coherence of its idea of God; the character and impact of its messenger; the transmission of its text; and its fit with human nature. Each criterion has strong points and real difficulties — including variants in the earliest manuscripts and the burning of non-Uthmanic copies. Applying the criteria fairly leaves a strong case, not a proof. Conviction and respect for people of other faiths go together.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If your closest friend follows another faith, you do not have to choose between your conviction and your friendship.** The Quran tells Muslims how to speak with Jews and Christians: "do not argue with the People of the Scripture except in a way that is best… and say, 'We believe in that which has been revealed to us and revealed to you. And our God and your God is one'" (29:46). Begin there: with what you share, said with courtesy. For the question of whether your friend will be saved, see the page "Are good people of other faiths saved?"

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Islam presents itself as the confirmation of earlier revelation.** "And We have revealed to you, [O Muhammad], the Book in truth, confirming that which preceded it of the Scripture and as a criterion over it" (5:48). This is Medinan, in Sura al-Maʾida, addressed to the Prophet (pbuh) about judging between communities. The same verse adds: "To each of you We prescribed a law and a method. Had Allah willed, He would have made you one nation [united in religion], but [He intended] to test you in what He has given you; so race to [all that is] good." So the Quran both claims authority over earlier scripture ("criterion over it") and accepts that communities have had different laws.

**It asks belief in all the prophets.** "The Messenger has believed in what was revealed to him from his Lord, and [so have] the believers. All of them have believed in Allah and His angels and His books and His messengers, [saying], 'We make no distinction between any of His messengers'" (2:285). In practice this means a Muslim affirms Abraham, Moses, David and Jesus (pbuh) as prophets of the same God.

**It differs sharply on who God is.** "Say, 'He is Allah, [who is] One'" (112:1). Islam rejects the Christian doctrines that Jesus (pbuh) is God or God's son; this is the main point of difference with Christianity, not a detail.

**It makes an exclusive claim, and a broader one.** "And whoever desires other than Islam as religion – never will it be accepted from him" (3:85). Yet an earlier verse promises reward to "those who believed and those who were Jews or Christians or Sabeans [before Prophet Muhammad] – those [among them] who believed in Allah and the Last Day and did righteousness" (2:62). How the two fit is discussed on the salvation page; Ibn Kathir reports Ibn ʿAbbas's view that 3:85 came later and governs after the Prophet's (pbuh) mission. The Prophet (pbuh) said: "he who amongst the community of Jews or Christians hears about me, but does not affirm his belief in that with which I have been sent and dies in this state (of disbelief), he shall be but one of the denizens of Hell-Fire" ({hadith:muslim:153}). This is the hard side of Islam's claim, and it should be stated plainly.

**It invites a test of its text.** "And if you are in doubt about what We have sent down upon Our Servant [Muhammad], then produce a surah the like thereof" (2:23). And it promises preservation: "Indeed, it is We who sent down the Qur'an and indeed, We will be its guardian" (15:9), from Sura al-Hijr, Meccan.

#### history — History: is the Quran well preserved?
**The text was standardised early, and the tradition says so openly.** Anas ibn Malik (ra) reports that Hudhayfa urged the caliph ʿUthman: "Save this nation before they differ about the Book (Qur'an) as Jews and the Christians did before." ʿUthman obtained the sheets kept by Hafsa (ra); Zayd ibn Thabit and three others made copies; where they differed, ʿUthman told them to "write it in the dialect of Quraish, the Qur'an was revealed in their tongue"; copies were sent to each province, and other Quranic materials were ordered "burnt" ({hadith:bukhari:4987}). This shows both a strong concern for one text and that other written forms existed.

**Early manuscripts are close to the Prophet's lifetime.** [Established for the dates.] A Quran fragment in Tübingen University Library, Ma VI 165, was dated by radiocarbon analysis at a Zürich laboratory to "AD 649–675" with "95.4% statistical probability" (*Medieval.eu*, 2014). The Sanaa palimpsest — a manuscript whose first ("lower") text was erased and overwritten — has a parchment that Sadeghi and Bergmann (2010) dated to before 669 CE with 95% probability, and "75% probability from before 646 CE" (*Wikipedia*, summarising them). The Birmingham folios, discussed on the page "Was the Quran preserved?", are dated to 568–645 CE (from the site's earlier research; not re-read here).

**The earliest text shows variants.** [Established that variants exist; Debated what they mean.] The Sanaa lower text has a sura order that "corresponds to no known Quranic order", and many variant wordings, which, the summary says, "rarely diverge from the fundamental meaning". Within suras the verse order mostly matches the standard text, with exceptions (in sura 20 two verses are swapped; in sura 9 a verse is missing, which Sadeghi and Goudarzi attribute to a copyist's eye-skip). Sadeghi and Goudarzi (2012) report some overlap with variants attributed to the Companion Ibn Masʿud but more differences; Elisabeth Puin and Asma Hilali report little or no correspondence; Hilali argues the texts may be schoolroom writing exercises, which Nicolai Sinai has criticised (*Wikipedia*).

**What this does and does not show.** The manuscripts show that the Quran's text was fixed very early and that the standard text was not a late invention. They also show that, before ʿUthman's standardisation, copies differed in order and wording. A skeptic will say this is ordinary textual history; a Muslim will say the variants are minor and the standardisation preserved the revealed text. Both are describing the same evidence.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Philosophers of religion describe the options.** David Basinger's SEP entry defines exclusivism as believing that one's perspective "is the truth or at least closer to the truth than any other competing religious perspective", and pluralism as denying "the primacy of any one religion". It summarises John Hick's view that each major religion "constitutes a valid context of salvation/liberation; but none constitutes the one and only such context". Islam in its mainstream forms is exclusivist about truth while, in many scholars' views, generous about who may be excused (see the salvation page).

**Muslim thinkers who compared religions.** Readers asked for Ibn Taymiyya's *al-Jawāb al-Ṣaḥīḥ*, Ibn Ḥazm's *al-Fiṣal*, Seyyed Hossein Nasr, Tim Winter (Abdal Hakim Murad), Abdulaziz Sachedina and Mohammad Hassan Khalil. Their works could not be read for this page, so their arguments are not summarised here (see Gaps). Khalil's study of how al-Ghazali, Ibn ʿArabi, Ibn Taymiyya and Rashid Rida thought about the fate of non-Muslims is summarised on the salvation page.

#### reasoning — How philosophers argue about it
**Criteria that can count as evidence for one religion over another.** Stated so they can be applied to every faith, including Islam:
1. **Coherence of its idea of God** — is the account of God consistent with itself and with the existence of a single Creator?
2. **The messenger** — what is known of his life, character and claims, from early sources, including hostile ones?
3. **The text and its transmission** — how early, how stable, how openly its history is recorded?
4. **Moral and spiritual fruit** — what does the teaching do to people who live by it, over time?
5. **Fit with human nature and reason** — does it make sense of what we know about ourselves?

**Applying them to Islam fairly.** *Strong points:* a strict monotheism (112:1) that many find coherent; a messenger whose life is recorded in detail; a text with very early manuscripts. *Weak points a skeptic will press:* the earliest manuscripts show variants and a different sura order; the non-Uthmanic copies were destroyed, so we cannot compare them fully; many detailed accounts of the Prophet's (pbuh) life were written down a century or more after him; and the moral record of Muslim societies, like every society's, is mixed. A fair reader will weigh both.

**The "born elsewhere" objection.** Al-Ghazali raised it himself: "Christian youths always grew up to be Christians, Jewish youths to be Jews and Muslim youths to be Muslims" (*al-Munqidh*, Watt, section I) — and it drove him to test his inherited belief, not to abandon belief. Philosophers call this the problem of "religious luck"; the philosopher Guy Axtell argues that exclusivists' beliefs suffer from "a malignant strand of epistemic luck", and critics reply that the same luck affects atheism and moral beliefs (Liz Jackson's review of Axtell, *Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews*, 2019). See "Is claiming one religion is true arrogant?".

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Every religion calls itself the fulfilment of the ones before."** *Reply:* that is true; the claim alone proves nothing. It has to be tested by the criteria above. *Limit:* the criteria themselves are weighed differently by different people.

**"Christians also have early manuscripts and a historical founder."** *Reply:* yes. The disagreement is not about whether Jesus (pbuh) existed, but about who he was and whether the doctrines about him are original. Islam's case is that the earliest message of all prophets was the oneness of God. *Limit:* historians of Christianity and Islam disagree on how to read the earliest sources of both, and this page cannot settle that.

**"The Uthmanic standardisation hid a messier text."** *Reply:* the tradition itself records the standardisation openly (Bukhari 4987), and the Sanaa lower text, which escaped it, differs mainly in order and in wordings that rarely change the meaning. *Limit:* we cannot compare every pre-Uthmanic copy, because most were destroyed.

#### views — The range of views
- **Mainstream Sunni and Shiʿi** — Islam is the final, true religion; earlier scriptures were genuine but changed; individuals the message did not truly reach may be excused (salvation page).
- **Hick's pluralism** — all major religions are valid paths; criticised in turn as "inclusivists in disguise" (Mark Heim) and as arbitrary in deciding which religions count (Basinger, SEP).
- **Critical historians** — the Quran's text was fixed early; the pre-Uthmanic text had variants (Sadeghi & Goudarzi; Hilali; Puin).

### Questions readers ask next
**What does Islam say about Jesus?** He is a prophet and messenger of God, born of Mary; Muslims honour him. Islam denies that he is God or the son of God (112:1).

**What does "continuation of the earlier prophets" mean in practice?** A Muslim believes in all of them "We make no distinction between any of His messengers" (2:285) and sees Islam as their shared message in its final form (5:48).

**Didn't ʿUthman burn other Qurans?** Yes. Bukhari 4987 records it, with the aim of preventing disputes over the text. That is why the earliest independent witness, the Sanaa lower text, matters so much to historians.

**How old are the oldest Qurans?** Several fragments are radiocarbon-dated to within a few decades of the Prophet's (pbuh) death: Tübingen Ma VI 165, AD 649–675 (95.4% probability); the Sanaa parchment, before 669 CE (95%).

**If I'd been born elsewhere, wouldn't I believe something else?** Probably, at first. Al-Ghazali noticed exactly that and tested his belief. Where a belief comes from does not settle whether it is true; the reasons for it do.

**Is my Christian friend wrong?** On the points where Islam and Christianity disagree, a Muslim believes so. That is compatible with deep respect and friendship (29:46).

### Honest limits
The comparison cannot be finished on one page, and the criteria are weighed differently by different people. The early manuscripts support an early fixed text but also show pre-Uthmanic variants whose meaning scholars dispute. The classical Muslim arguments against other religions (Ibn Taymiyya, Ibn Ḥazm) and the modern ones (Nasr, Winter, Sachedina) could not be read for this page. The case for Islam is strong for many people; it does not compel.

## Q36 — Is claiming one religion is true arrogant?

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 109:6, 10:99, 49:13, 2:256, 29:46, 17:15.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, quran.com) on 109:6 (https://quran.com/109:6/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir), 10:99 and 49:13.
- Alvin Plantinga, *Warranted Christian Belief* (Oxford University Press, 2000), Part IV, ch. "Postmodernism and Pluralism", section B, "The Charge of Moral Arbitrariness", pp. 443–447, via CCEL, https://ccel.org/ccel/plantinga/warrant3.vii.iii.ii.ii.html.
- Lecture notes summarising Plantinga's "A Defense of Religious Exclusivism", https://hettingern.people.charleston.edu/Intro_to_Philosophy_Sp_06/Plantinga_Religious_Exclusivism.htm (used only as a pointer; not his words).
- David Basinger, "Religious Diversity (Pluralism)", SEP (2025).
- Liz Jackson, review of Guy Axtell, *Problems of Religious Luck* (Lexington, 2019), *Notre Dame Philosophical Reviews*, 2019.09.23, https://ndpr.nd.edu/news/problems-of-religious-luck-assessing-the-limits-of-reasonable-religious-disagreement/.
- Duke University press release on Leary et al. (2017), *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*, via EurekAlert, https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/908906 (the paper itself was not read; see Gaps).
- Pew Research Center, "10 key findings about the religious lives of U.S. teens and their parents" (10 September 2020), https://www.pewresearch.org/short-reads/2020/09/10/10-key-findings-about-the-religious-lives-of-u-s-teens-and-their-parents/.
- W. Montgomery Watt (trans.), al-Ghazali, *al-Munqidh*, ghazali.org.
- Sahih Muslim 153 (sunnah.com).

### The question, at its strongest
"Most people end up with the religion of their parents. If you were born in another country, you would be just as sure of another faith. So your certainty comes from the accident of where you were born — and you then claim that billions of sincere people are wrong, and that many of them will be punished forever. Believing that your group alone has the truth, and that everyone else is lost, looks like the very definition of arrogance."

### Why people ask it
In mixed societies, people live alongside friends of other faiths and none. Many feel that any claim to religious truth is a claim to superiority. The question becomes sharper when it is tied to eternal punishment.

### Short answer
Holding a belief that others reject is not, in itself, arrogant; every position — including "all religions are equally valid" — rejects someone's view. The philosopher Alvin Plantinga put it this way: "if contradicting others is arrogant and egoistical, so is dissenting." Arrogance is about *how* a belief is held: with contempt, without listening, or with force. The Quran forbids compulsion in religion (2:256), tells the Prophet (pbuh) that forcing belief is not his task (10:99), and grounds human worth in righteousness, not in group membership (49:13). The "accident of birth" problem is real; it calls for testing one's beliefs, as al-Ghazali did, rather than dropping all of them. And on eternal punishment, mainstream scholars hold that no one is held to account for a message that did not truly reach them (17:15).

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If you love someone of another faith, conviction does not require coldness.** The Quran tells Muslims to speak to Jews and Christians "in a way that is best" and to begin with "our God and your God is one" (29:46). Whether a particular person will be saved is, in the words of the tradition, God's decision, not ours; the page "Are my non-Muslim loved ones lost?" takes this up with care.

**If you feel guilty for believing at all, remember that dissent is also a belief.** You can hold your faith humbly — open to questions, kind to those who differ — without pretending you have no view.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**"For you is your religion, and for me is my religion"** (109:6). This is the last verse of Sura al-Kafirun, a Meccan sura. Ibn Kathir reports ("it has been said") that the Quraysh had invited the Prophet (pbuh) to worship their idols for a year and they would worship his God for a year; the sura was the answer. He calls it "the Surah of disavowal from the deeds of the idolators". So the verse is not a statement that all religions are equally true: it is a refusal to mix worship, combined with leaving the others to their own. The verse does not command anything about how Muslims treat others; that comes from other verses.

**No compulsion, and no forcing belief.** "There shall be no compulsion in [acceptance of] the religion. The right course has become clear from the wrong" (2:256). "And had your Lord willed, those on earth would have believed – all of them entirely. Then, [O Muhammad], would you compel the people in order that they become believers?" (10:99), from Sura Yunus, Meccan, addressed to the Prophet (pbuh). Ibn Kathir explains that the Prophet (pbuh) is "not commanded to do that", that guidance belongs to God, who guides from His "knowledge, wisdom, and justice", and he cites 2:272: "It is not up to you to guide them".

**Worth is not group membership.** "O mankind, indeed We have created you from male and female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you" (49:13), from Sura al-Hujurat, Medinan, addressed to all people. Ibn Kathir reads "that you may know one another" (with Mujahid) as the knowledge of lineage — so-and-so son of so-and-so of such a tribe — and says that "you earn honor with Allah the Exalted on account of Taqwa, not family lineage". He cites the hadith in Muslim that God "does not look at your shapes or wealth, but he looks at your hearts and actions".

**The hard side stated plainly.** The Quran also says that a religion other than Islam will not be accepted (3:85), and the Prophet (pbuh) said that a Jew or Christian who "hears about me" and dies without believing is among the people of the Fire ({hadith:muslim:153}). How scholars limit this to those whom the message truly reached is set out on the salvation page; it is not hidden here, because the question of arrogance cannot be answered honestly without it.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Plantinga: the charge applies to the critic too.** Alvin Plantinga (b. 1932), an American philosopher, discussed the charge that exclusivism involves "a sort of egoism, perhaps pride or hubris, in accepting beliefs" others reject. He calls such charges "a philosophical tar baby: get close enough to them to use them against the Christian believer, and you are likely to find them stuck fast to yourself", and concludes: "if *contradicting* others is arrogant and egoistical, so is *dissenting*" (*Warranted Christian Belief*, pp. 443–445). The pluralist who says "all religions are equally valid" also rejects what most believers in every religion hold.

**Hick: all great religions as paths.** John Hick held that each major religion "constitutes a valid context of salvation/liberation; but none constitutes the one and only such context" (Basinger, SEP). His critics argue that this is itself a disputed position: Mark Heim calls such pluralists "inclusivists in disguise", and others say Hick's ethical test for which religions count is "as arbitrary as the standard for acceptable paths to salvation set forth by exclusivists or inclusivists" (Basinger, citing Meeker 2003).

**Religious luck.** Guy Axtell's *Problems of Religious Luck* (2019) argues that because believers would probably have held opposite beliefs if raised elsewhere, exclusivist belief suffers from "a malignant strand of epistemic luck". Liz Jackson's review lists replies: some beliefs (that discrimination is wrong, for example) we rightly keep despite their cultural origin; atheism and theism are equally affected by upbringing; and the same problem hits moral, political and philosophical beliefs (*NDPR*, 2019). The same worry appears much earlier in al-Ghazali: "Christian youths always grew up to be Christians, Jewish youths to be Jews and Muslim youths to be Muslims" (*al-Munqidh*, Watt) — and his answer was to test what he had inherited.

#### science — What research says
**Belief is strongly passed on in families.** [Established, for the US sample.] In a Pew survey of 1,811 pairs of US teens (13–17) and their parents (March–April 2019), "Most teens share the religion of their parents"; eight in ten evangelical Protestant parents had a teen who also identified as evangelical; 48% of teens said they have "all the same" religious beliefs as their parent; and 88% of parents who regularly attend services had a teen who reported attending that often.

**Intellectual humility can be measured, and it matters for how we treat people who disagree.** [Moderate; from a university press release, not the paper.] Leary and colleagues (2017, four studies, *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin*) found that intellectually humble people were less likely to judge the character of someone they disagreed with, and better at telling strong arguments from weak ones. They found essentially no difference in intellectual humility between religious and non-religious people. This supports the claim that arrogance is about how a belief is held. Whether humble confidence improves relations *between groups* was not shown in the material read for this page.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Every view excludes others" is a dodge.** *The objection:* it does not answer the accident-of-birth problem; it just says everyone has it. *Reply:* it answers the *arrogance* charge, not the luck problem. The luck problem is answered differently: by testing one's beliefs on their reasons, as al-Ghazali did, rather than abandoning all of them. *Limit:* most people never test their inherited beliefs, and for them the luck problem stands.

**"Exclusivism plus eternal punishment is arrogance."** *Reply:* mainstream Muslim scholars hold that no one is punished for a message that did not truly reach them — "never would We punish until We sent a messenger" (17:15) — and they forbid declaring any particular person damned (salvation page). *Limit:* for someone who heard the message clearly and rejected it, the strict mainstream view gives no comfort, and that should be said honestly.

**"Calling others to Islam is disrespectful."** *Reply:* sharing what one believes is true is a form of taking others seriously, as long as it is without force (2:256), with courtesy (29:46), and leaving the outcome to God (10:99). *Limit:* the history of every religion, Islam included, contains coercion; principles do not erase that record.

#### views — The range of views
- **Exclusivism (Plantinga; mainstream Muslim theology)** — one's own religion is true where it conflicts with others; this is not in itself arrogant.
- **Pluralism (Hick)** — all major religions are valid paths; criticised as itself exclusive or arbitrary (Heim; Meeker, in Basinger).
- **Religious-luck critique (Axtell)** — exclusivist belief is undermined by its dependence on upbringing; replies in Jackson's review.
- **Classical Muslim view of the excused** — those the message did not truly reach are not held to account (salvation page).

### Questions readers ask next
**What do "exclusivism" and "pluralism" mean?** Exclusivism: one's own religion is true, or closer to the truth, where religions disagree. Pluralism: no single religion has primacy (Basinger, SEP).

**How can 109:6 fit with calling people to Islam?** Ibn Kathir reads 109:6 as a refusal to mix worship with idolatry, not as saying all religions are equal. Calling others is allowed; compulsion is not (2:256; 10:99).

**Does Islam teach that all non-Muslims go to Hell?** No. Mainstream scholars excuse those the message did not truly reach; the strict view applies to those who understood and rejected it. Only God knows who that is.

**What does intellectual humility look like in practice?** Listening to the strongest form of the other view; not judging the person's character for disagreeing; admitting what you cannot answer.

**If I'm Muslim because my parents were, is my faith worth anything?** It is where you start, not where you must end. Testing it — as al-Ghazali did — can make it your own.

**How do I love friends of other faiths without feeling I betray mine?** Begin with what you share (29:46), be honest about what you don't, and leave judgment to God.

### Honest limits
The "accident of birth" problem is not fully answered by pointing out that everyone faces it; it is answered only by testing beliefs, which most people do not do. Exclusivism combined with eternal punishment remains hard for many readers even with the classical limits on who is held to account. The intellectual-humility finding is from a press release, not the paper, and no evidence was read on effects between groups.
