## S17 — Clouds and winds

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 24:43 and 15:22.

### Classical tafsir
**Driven, gathered, piled up.** On 24:43, Ibn Kathir explains that God "drives the clouds from the beginning, when they are formed and are still weak", then "brings them together after they have been scattered" and "piles them up on top of one another"; the rain comes "from the gaps between them", as Ibn 'Abbas and al-Dahhak understood it. Al-Qurtubi describes the same steps, all under God's direction. On the "mountains" in the sky, Ibn Kathir reports one view that there are "mountains of hail in the sky", and another that "mountains" is a metaphor for clouds — "And Allah knows best."

**Winds that fertilise.** On "the fertilizing winds" (15:22), Ibn Kathir quotes Ibn Mas'ud: "The wind is sent bearing water from the sky, then it fertilizes the clouds until rain begins to generously fall", and says Ibn 'Abbas, al-Nakha'i and Qatada held the same view. Early commentators also read *lawaqih* as winds that pollinate trees.

### Questions readers ask next
**Did the commentators know how rain forms?** They described what anyone can watch — clouds driven, joined and piled up before rain — and read it as God's direction. The physics of clouds is in "How it works".

**Are the "mountains" in 24:43 hail clouds?** Ibn Kathir gives both readings, literal hail-mountains and clouds described as mountains, and leaves it open.

## S18 — Fire from the green tree

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 36:80 and 56:71–72.

### Classical tafsir
**The opposite from the opposite.** Ibn Kathir says these last verses of Ya Sin answer a man who came to the Prophet (pbuh) crumbling a dry bone and asking whether God would bring it back. On 36:80 he explains: the One who made the tree "from water, when it has become green and beautiful, bearing fruit, then He changes it until it becomes dry wood with which fires are lit". On 56:71–72 he names the two Arabian trees: "The Arabs had two kinds of trees called Al-Markh and Al-'Afar"; rubbing a green branch of each against the other made sparks. Al-Qurtubi gives the same account. The argument: fire comes out of something wet and green, so bringing life out of dead bones is no harder.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is this about photosynthesis or stored solar energy?** The commentators read it as the markh and 'afar fire-sticks and as a sign of resurrection. Modern chemistry is kept in "How it works", not read back into the verse.

**What has fire to do with the resurrection?** The verse comes right after the denial "Who will give life to bones while they are disintegrated?" (36:78); the answer points to opposites coming from opposites.

## S19 — Iron "sent down"

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 57:25.

### Classical tafsir
**Power next to justice.** In 57:25, iron comes right after the messengers, the Book and "the balance", which Ibn Kathir explains as "justice, according to Mujahid, Qatadah and others". On iron's "great military might", he explains: God made iron "a deterrent for those who refuse the truth and oppose it after the proof has been established against them". Its benefits are everyday tools: "coins, hammers, axes, saws chisels, shovels and various tools" for farming, cooking and crafts. *Anzalna* ("We sent down") is the same verb used for livestock (39:6) and clothing (7:26), so classical scholars often read it as "We created and granted": a gift from above in rank, not necessarily from the sky.

### Questions readers ask next
**Does "sent down" mean iron came from meteorites or stars?** Classical scholars read the verb as "created and granted", as with livestock and clothing. The modern astrophysics of iron is in "How it works", and the honest notes explain why the verse is not a prediction of it.

**Why is iron in a verse about justice?** Ibn Kathir reads it as strength that should serve the balance: power to defend justice, and tools for daily life.

## S20 — The ant

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 27:18–19.

### Classical tafsir
**An ant's warning, a prophet's gratitude.** Ibn Kathir places the story among "the great blessings and favors" given to David and Solomon (peace be upon them both). He says Solomon (pbuh) "understood what the ant said", and al-Qurtubi and al-Razi likewise treat the ant's speech as real communication that God let Solomon (pbuh) understand. The commentators notice that the ant warned the whole community and even excused the army: "while they perceive not". Solomon's (pbuh) answer is gratitude, not pride; Ibn Kathir explains his prayer as "inspire me to give thanks for the blessings that You have bestowed upon me".

### Questions readers ask next
**Can ants really "speak"?** The commentators read it as real communication that Solomon (pbuh) was given to understand. Ant communication by chemical signals is described in "How it works"; the page does not claim the verse describes it.

**What is the lesson of the story?** Fairness even in danger (the ant excuses the army) and gratitude in power (Solomon's prayer).

## S21 — Night and day; the sun as a lamp, the moon as a light

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 10:5 and 78:13.

### Classical tafsir
**Two lights, two jobs.** On 10:5, Ibn Kathir explains that God "made the rays that come forth from the bright sun as the source of light, and made the beams that come forth from the moon as light. He made them of two different natures so they would not be confused with one another." He ordained phases for the moon: "it starts small then its light increases until it completes a full moon. Then it begins to decrease". On "a burning lamp" (78:13) he says: "the radiant sun that gives light to all of the world". Al-Razi and al-Zamakhshari discuss *diya'* as light with heat and strength, and *nur* as calmer light. Both are tied to timekeeping: the moon for months and years, the sun for days and prayer times.

### Questions readers ask next
**Does "derived light" mean the Quran says the moon reflects sunlight?** Classical commentators read the two words as two kinds of light, strong and gentle; they did not draw out reflection. The honest notes discuss the modern claim.

**Why does the moon matter for time?** Its phases give the months of the Islamic calendar, as Ibn Kathir describes.

## S22 — Fingertips and resurrection

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 75:3–4.

### Classical tafsir
**Power over the smallest parts.** Ibn Kathir explains the verse as God's answer to doubt about the resurrection: "does man think that We will not gather his bones Surely, We will gather them and We are quite able to put together his fingertips." This is the most common reading (al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir, al-Qurtubi): if God can restore even the tiny bones of the fingertips, rebuilding the whole body is easy. A second reading, reported from Ibn 'Abbas: "We could have made his fingers flat and joined, like a camel's hoof", so he could not do fine work; Ibn Kathir has a similar reading, that God could make the fingertips "all equal (in length)". Neither classical reading mentions fingerprints.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is this verse about fingerprints?** No classical commentator read it that way; the honest notes explain why the modern claim is weak.

**Why fingertips?** They are among the smallest and finest parts of the body; restoring them shows power over every detail.

## S23 — Expansion of the heavens

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 51:47.

### Classical tafsir
**Built with strength, made wide.** Ibn Kathir explains "with strength" as "with strength, according to 'Abdullah bin 'Abbas, Mujahid, Qatadah, Ath-Thawri and several others" — *ayd* means power, not hands. On "We are [its] expander" he explains: "We made it vast and We brought its roof higher without pillars to support it." *Musi'un* was read in several ways: "We are able, with vast capacity" (reported by al-Tabari); "We widen provision for creatures"; or "We made it wide". The classical focus is God's power and generosity, not a change in the size of the cosmos over time.

### Questions readers ask next
**Did classical scholars read 51:47 as the expanding universe?** No; their readings were about power, vastness and provision. The modern cosmology and the debate about reading it into the verse are on this page, kept separate.

**Is "expander" a good translation?** It is Saheeh International's; the Arabic allows the classical meanings above as well.

## S24 — A smoke-like origin; heavens and earth joined then split

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 21:30 and 41:11.

### Classical tafsir
**Joined, then parted.** On 21:30, Ibn Kathir reports Ibn 'Abbas's answer to a man who asked about it: "Yes, the heavens were joined together and" did not send rain, and the earth did not grow plants; then God split the sky with rain and the earth with plants. Another reading is that they were one mass that God separated. Ibn 'Abbas also argued that "night came before the day", since when they were joined there was nothing between them "except darkness".

**"While it was smoke".** On 41:11, Ibn Kathir explains the smoke as "steam which arose from it when the earth was created"; reports from Ibn Mas'ud and others, cited by al-Tabari, describe it as vapour rising from water. "We have come willingly" is read as all creation obeying God's command: "everything that You want to create in us… will all be obedient to You."

### Questions readers ask next
**Is this the Big Bang?** Classical scholars read it as rain and plants, or as one mass separated, and the smoke as vapour. The page shows the modern comparison and its problems separately.

**Which came first, the earth or the heavens?** Ibn Kathir notes that 41:9–12 discusses the creation of the earth and of the heaven separately; the page does not settle the order.

## S25 — The protected roof

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 21:32.

### Classical tafsir
**High and guarded.** On "a protected ceiling" (21:32), Ibn Kathir explains: "high and protected from anything reaching it"; Mujahid said "Raised up." Other commentators read "protected" as protected from falling onto the earth (22:65), and from devils listening to the heavens (15:17–18, 37:6–10). On "but they, from its signs, are turning away", Ibn Kathir says: "They do not think about how Allah has created it, so vast and high", adorned with the sun, moon and stars. The emphasis is God's care and order: a roof built and kept for us.

### Questions readers ask next
**Does "protected" mean the atmosphere shields us?** Classical commentators read it as protected from falling, from reaching, and from devils. The atmosphere's protection is described in "How it works", not read into the verse.

**What is the reader asked to do?** Not to "turn away": to look at the sky and think about how it was made.

## S26 — Darkness in the deep sea

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 24:40.

### Classical tafsir
**A parable of the heart.** The verse is a parable; it follows the parable of the mirage (24:39). Ibn Kathir: "This is the parable of the heart of the disbeliever whose ignorance is simple, who merely follows and does not know the true nature of the one whom he follows or where he is going." Al-Tabari, Ibn Kathir and al-Qurtubi map the layers of darkness onto layers of the heart: darkness of belief, of words, of deeds, one on top of another. "He can hardly see it" means, in Ibn Kathir's words, "because it is so intensely dark." *Lujji* means a deep, vast sea.

### Questions readers ask next
**Does the verse describe internal waves in the ocean?** The commentators read it as a parable of the heart; the modern claim and its limits are in the honest notes.

**Why two parables (24:39–40)?** Both describe the one without guidance: the mirage, then the darkness. Ibn Kathir reads the darkness as the heart that "merely follows" without knowing.

## S27 — Gardens and fruits watered by one water

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 13:4 and 35:27.

### Classical tafsir
**One water, many tastes.** On "watered with one water; but We make some of them exceed others" (13:4), Ibn Kathir notes "differences between plants and fruits with regards to shape, color, taste, scent, blossoms" and form. *Sinwan* ("from one root") describes palms growing from a shared root, a feature of date palms. Al-Razi uses this verse as a key argument for a choosing Creator: the same soil, the same water and the same sun produce different tastes, colours and smells, so the difference cannot come from these causes alone.

### Questions readers ask next
**Doesn't genetics explain the differences?** The science is in "How it works". Al-Razi's argument is that the same outer causes give different results; genetics describes how, and the reflection asks why there is such variety at all.

**What does "from one root" mean?** Date palms that grow several trunks from one root (*sinwan*).

## S28 — Livestock and their benefits

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 16:5–7 and 36:71–73.

### Classical tafsir
**Warmth, food, riding — and beauty.** On "in them is warmth" (16:5), Ibn Kathir quotes Ibn 'Abbas: it "refers to clothing", and "numerous benefits" to food and drink. On 36:72 he explains "some of them they ride on their journeys, and use them to carry their burdens", and lists their wool, fur, hair and milk. Commentators highlight *dhallalnaha*: large, strong animals made manageable, so even a child can lead a camel — a blessing people did not create. On "beauty when you bring them in" (16:6), Ibn Kathir says "Their beauty is a kind of adornment", when they come back in the evening, full and well fed: a rare mention of pleasure in looking at animals.

### Questions readers ask next
**Why does the Quran mention the beauty of cattle?** The verse joins usefulness and beauty: animals are for use, and also a pleasure to see (16:6).

**Does "tamed" mean they were domesticated?** Classical scholars read it as God making them manageable; the history of domestication is described separately.

## S29 — Human consciousness and the soul

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 17:85 and 51:21.

### Classical tafsir
**A question left open on purpose.** Ibn Kathir reports from 'Abdullah ibn Mas'ud (ra) that some Jews asked the Prophet (pbuh) about the *ruh*, and the verse came down: "The soul is of the affair of my Lord. And mankind have not been given of knowledge except a little" (17:85). Most commentators read this as a deliberate limit: the nature of the *ruh* is not explained, and human knowledge of it is small. Scholars debated the relation of *ruh* (spirit) and *nafs* (self). Al-Ghazali treats the true self as a spiritual reality that uses the body as a tool; Ibn al-Qayyim (*Kitab al-Ruh*) collects many views; some theologians held the *ruh* to be a subtle body. On "And in yourselves. Then will you not see?" (51:21), Ibn Kathir quotes Qatada: "He who thinks about his own creation will realize that he was created with flexible joints so that it is easy for him to perform acts of worship."

### Questions readers ask next
**Is the soul the same as consciousness?** Classical scholars did not settle what the *ruh* is; 17:85 itself says little is known. The modern "hard problem" is in "How it works".

**Does neuroscience disprove the soul?** The page shows what neuroscience explains and what it has not, without claiming either side is proven.

## S30 — DNA and the information in life

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 20:50 and 87:2–3.

### Classical tafsir
**Form, then guidance.** On "Our Lord is He who gave each thing its form and then guided [it]" (20:50), Ibn Kathir quotes Ibn 'Abbas: "He made the man a man, and the donkey a donkey and the sheep a sheep", and Mujahid: "He gave everything its form." On "And who destined and [then] guided" (87:3), he quotes Mujahid: "he guided the cattle to their pastures", and sums up: "He decreed a set measure and guided the creation to it." Commentators read the two verses as two steps — form, then guidance to what each creature needs, such as a newborn to its mother's milk. They saw guidance (*hidaya*) built into every creature's nature.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is "guided" a reference to DNA?** No classical scholar read it that way. The page describes DNA in "How it works" and treats the link as a reflection, not a prediction.

**What does "each thing its form" mean?** In Ibn 'Abbas's words, each kind made what it is: a man a man, a sheep a sheep.

## S31 — Ecological balance and measure

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 55:7–9 and 15:19.

### Classical tafsir
**From the balance of the sky to fair trade.** On "And the heaven He raised and imposed the balance" (55:7), Ibn Kathir explains the balance as "the justice" and adds: "He created the heavens and earth in justice and truth so that everything is founded on, and observing, justice and truth." On 55:9 he draws the practical lesson: "do not cheat in the weights and measures, but rather observe justice and fairness". Commentators read *mizan* as both the cosmic order and justice; *mawzun* in 15:19 is read as "in right proportion", each thing according to wisdom and need. The move is from cosmos to ethics: the balance in creation is a model for balance in human dealings.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is the verse about ecology?** Classically it is about justice and measure. Applying it to the environment is a modern reflection, and the page labels it as one.

**What does "do not make deficient the balance" mean in practice?** Ibn Kathir: honest weights and measures, and fairness in all dealings.

## S32 — The human being taught to speak

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 55:3–4, 90:8–10 and 2:31.

### Classical tafsir
**Speech, choice and names.** On "[And] taught him eloquence" (55:4), Ibn Kathir quotes al-Hasan: "Eloquent speech", and explains God teaching people "to speak and pronounce letters with the various parts of the mouth, such as the alveolar bridge, the tongue and the lips". *Al-bayan* is read as the ability to express what is in the mind, and more broadly to explain and distinguish (al-Razi, Ibn Kathir). 90:8–10 lists the tools of seeing and speaking, then moral choice; on "the two ways", Ibn Kathir quotes Ibn Mas'ud: "The good and the evil." On 2:31, he says God taught Adam (pbuh) "the names of everything", which showed "Adam's superiority over them in knowledge" — the capacity to name things and form concepts.

### Questions readers ask next
**Why is speech counted as a blessing?** The verses join speech with moral choice: being able to explain is tied to being responsible.

**Did Adam (pbuh) learn a language?** Commentators differ on what "the names" were; Ibn Kathir reads it as the names of everything. The science of language is in "How it works".

## S33 — The diversity of colours in nature

### Sources read
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English, Quran.com), on 35:27–28.

### Classical tafsir
**Colours, and those who know.** On 35:27, Ibn Kathir describes fruits "of various colors, yellow, red, green, white", and mountains: "white and red mountains, and in some of them there are streaks which are also of varying colors". *Judad* are streaks or paths: "Ibn 'Abbas said Al-Judad means pathways." Commentators stress the variety arising from one water and one earth. The verse ends: "Only those fear Allah, from among His servants, who have knowledge" (35:28). Ibn Kathir explains: "the more they know about the Almighty… the more they will fear Him." Al-Razi and others link this variety to knowledge: those who know see the signs and respond with reverence (*khashya*).

### Questions readers ask next
**Who are "those who have knowledge"?** Classically, those who know God; many today read it to include anyone whose study of nature leads to reverence.

**Why mention colours of people and animals?** 35:28 continues the list: "among people and moving creatures and grazing livestock are various colors similarly" — variety as a sign.
