# Questions of the Age, Batch 4: Deep Answers Q17–Q38

These 22 questions are best answered by being honest about moral and historical difficulty, not by defending certainty. The only quantitative study of Muslim doubt (Yaqeen, n=630) concluded that doubt "appears to be the actions of Muslims rather than the doctrines of Islam." So Q18 (hypocrisy), Q19 (sectarianism) and the sensitive questions Q20–Q25 are the core of this batch.

**Labels:** [Established] scientific/historical consensus · [Scholarly] mainstream interpretation · [Debated] contested or minority view. Questions marked **(HIGH)** are high-sensitivity and need the gentle help card and extra care on the site.

## TL;DR
- **Philosophy (Q17, Q26, Q29–Q30, Q32–Q34):** classical arguments answer the popular versions of these doubts well. Ibn Sīnā's contingency proof does not need the universe to have a beginning, and "who made God?" does not apply to a necessary being. But none of these arguments is a knock-down proof, and near-death research and the hard problem of consciousness remain open.
- **Sensitive topics (Q20–Q25):** each answer shows the full range of views.
  - Aisha's age: the traditional report, Little's 2022 thesis, and his critics.
  - Banu Qurayza: Arafat (1976) against Kister (1986).
  - Slavery: the Quran pushed toward freeing slaves but did not abolish slavery; abolition came in the 19th–20th centuries.
  - Hijab and sexuality: majority and minority positions.

  The site rejects child marriage, coercion and contempt.
- **Pastoral questions (Q18, Q31, Q35–Q38):** in Türkiye, "believer but not religious" rose from 31% to 34% and "religious" fell from 55% to 46% (KONDA, 2008→2025). Most people drifting away are hurt believers, not convinced deists. They need practice, community and mental-health care more than more apologetics.

## Key Findings
1. **Doubt is mainly moral and social.** In Yaqeen's Muslim American Attitudes Survey (2017, n=630), 55% reported at least one current "hard doubt." Those who had any averaged 3.5. Critics such as *Muslim Skeptic* say the framing suited Yaqeen's agenda, but there is no competing dataset.
2. **Türkiye is changing gradually, not collapsing.** In KONDA 2025 (n=6,137), atheists and non-believers rose from 2% to 8%. The most devout group ("sofu") held steady at 12%. Batch 2 found deism under 2%.
   - The 2018 "deism panic" began at one event: the "Gençlik ve İnanç" (Youth and Faith) workshop of 50 religion teachers in Konya, on 4 March 2018.
   - It was organised by İKDAM and Uluslararası Öncü Eğitimciler Derneği, with support from Konya's provincial education directorate.
   - Its closing statement said deism "ön plana çıkmakta" ("is coming to the fore") among young people with problems of belief.
3. **Several popular slogans rest on weak hadith.** "My community's disagreement is a mercy" has no known chain of narrators. The "Hell" clause of the "73 sects" hadith is debated. According to Ibn Taymiyya, "I was a hidden treasure" has no chain.
4. **The science gives limited support to either side.** Ramadan weight loss averages 1.24 kg, and most of it comes back. In AWARE-II, no patient identified a hidden visual target. The finding that "analytic thinking reduces belief" failed a large replication.

## Q17. Who created God?
**1. Steelmanned.** If everything needs a cause, so does God. If God doesn't, why not say the universe is the uncaused thing? Dawkins adds that a designer would be more complex than what it designs.

**2. Why asked.** It is a childhood question that comes back as a test of whether faith is afraid of thinking. The Prophet foresaw the whisper "who created your Lord?" and advised seeking refuge (Bukhārī 3276; Muslim 134). That is guidance for vesvese (intrusive, obsessive doubts), not a ban on philosophy.

**3. Short answer.** The argument does not say everything has a cause. It says that whatever *begins*, or *could fail to exist*, needs one. A necessary, beginningless being cannot be created. Asking who made it is like asking who drew a circle's corners. The real debate is whether such a being exists.

**4. Religious explanation.** God is *al-Ṣamad*, the one on whom all depend (112). 52:35 sets out the logical options.
- **The kalām origination argument (*ḥudūth*):** whatever begins has a cause (al-Ashʿarī, al-Māturīdī, al-Ghazālī).
- **Ibn Sīnā's *burhān al-ṣiddīqīn*** (*Ishārāt*, namaṭ 4): even an infinite chain of contingent things needs a necessary ground (*wājib al-wujūd*).
- **Later responses:** al-Ghazālī and Ibn Rushd criticised parts of it; Mullā Ṣadrā and Ṭabāṭabāʾī reworked it.

**5. Philosophy.** The main objections and replies:
- *Why not a necessary universe?* Its physical constants look contingent, and a necessary being would have to be simple.
- *Hume and Kant:* the argument starts from the fact of existence, not from a definition of God.
- *Composition fallacy:* the claim is about dependence, not about parts and wholes.
- *Dawkins:* classical theism holds that God is simple, not complex.

Among philosophers, 18.93% lean toward theism and 66.95% toward atheism (PhilPapers 2020, N=1,770). The arguments are respectable, not decisive.

**6. Science.** The Big Bang and the Borde–Guth–Vilenkin theorem fit a universe with a beginning. Quantum-gravity models leave this open [Debated]. Cosmology is not a "proof."

**7. Psychology.** Taking the question seriously builds trust. If the thought keeps intruding compulsively, treat it as vesvese.

**8. Honest note.** The regress objection fails. But getting from a necessary being to a God who is personal, one, and the God of the Quran takes further steps. Ibn Sīnā's derivation of the divine attributes is the weakest link.

**9. Visuals.** A chain of lamps lit only by a self-luminous source. A sorter for impossible, contingent and necessary things. A "corners of a circle" card.

**10. Sources.** Ibn Sīnā, *al-Ishārāt* (Ar.); al-Ghazālī, *al-Iqtiṣād* (Ar.); P. Adamson, *Philosophy in the Islamic World* (2016); TDV, "Vâcibü'l-vücûd" (Tr.); Ṭabāṭabāʾī, *Uṣūl-e falsafe* (Per.); Bourget & Chalmers, *Philosophers' Imprint* (2023); [PhilPapers Survey 2020](https://survey2020.philpeople.org/survey/results/all).

## Q18. "If religious people act like this, why believe?" (HIGH)
**1. Steelmanned.** If a faith truly transforms people, its public face should look better. Lying preachers, abuse cover-ups and religion used to win votes suggest it is a tool of power.

**2. Why asked.** This is the top trigger in Yaqeen's data. In Türkiye, young people have seen:
- religion used by rival political and community actors;
- abuse cases in religious dormitories;
- the 2016 coup attempt, which the state attributes to a religious network.

Diyanet's own monthly magazine (November 2018) asked how to protect youth from *din istismarı* (exploitation of religion). This is doubt born of betrayal, not of logic.

**3. Short answer.** The Quran expects hypocrisy among believers and condemns it more harshly than any critic does. Misuse of religion confirms its account of human nature; a doctor who smokes does not disprove medicine. Your anger is legitimate. Judge the message by its source and its best examples, and don't let corrupt people own your faith.

**4. Religious explanation.**
- "Do you command righteousness and forget yourselves?" (2:44); see also 61:2–3, Sūra 107, and Sūra 63 on hypocrites.
- The signs of a hypocrite are lying, breaking promises and betraying trust (Bukhārī 33; Muslim 59).
- "Religion is sincere counsel," including counsel to rulers (Muslim 55).
- Al-Ghazālī attacked the "scholars of this world." Nursi sought refuge "from Satan and politics."

**5. Philosophy.** The objection is *ad hominem*: it judges the message by its messengers. The data are mixed. Religious practice predicts more giving, but a religious label alone predicts little. That matches the Quran's distinction between *islām* (outward submission) and *īmān/iḥsān* (faith and excellence). A fair counterpoint is the "no true Scotsman" problem: it is too easy to say bad actors were never real believers. Communities must own their failures.

**6. Science.** Norris & Inglehart link faster disaffiliation among the young to the fusion of religion and state. KONDA's Turkish trend fits this pattern but cannot prove the cause.

**7. Psychology.** Hurt caused by religious people shows up as "interpersonal" spiritual struggle (Exline et al. 2014). For abuse survivors, safety and trauma therapy come first. Leaving an abusive group is not leaving God.

**8. Honest note.** Turkish communities, like others, have sometimes shielded abusers and used faith politically. "That's not real Islam" is not enough. The site should:
- name abuse as both sin and crime;
- point to legal and clinical help;
- avoid party politics;
- criticise misuse by *any* side.

**9. Visuals.** A "mirror vs window" card. A slider of verses on hypocrisy. A sorter that separates message, messenger and misuse.

**10. Sources.** [Chouhoud (Yaqeen 2018)](https://yaqeeninstitute.org/read/paper/what-causes-muslims-to-doubt-islam-a-quantitative-analysis); Diyanet Aylık Dergi, Kasım 2018 (Tr.); al-Ghazālī, *Iḥyāʾ* (Ar.); Nursi, *Mektubat* (Tr.); Exline et al. (2014); H.H. Behr, "Keine Angst vor Kritik" (Ger., 2014).

## Q19. Why one "right" Islam? Why so many sects?
**1. Steelmanned.** If God's message is clear, why the splits, each group claiming to be the "saved sect"? Either the message is unclear or the claim to certainty is arrogant.

**2. Why asked.** "Only one right way" rigidity is a top Yaqeen trigger. Young Turks meet rival religious communities (cemaats), online Salafi preachers, Alevi neighbours and secular relatives. The "73 sects" hadith is often used as a weapon.

**3. Short answer.** All Muslims share a core: God, the Prophet, the Quran, prayer and the afterlife. Disagreement over interpretation was accepted from the Companions onward. On 4–6 July 2005, 200 leading scholars from 50 countries met in Amman. They recognised the validity of all eight schools of Sunni, Shiʿi and Ibāḍī Islam and forbade declaring other Muslims unbelievers (*takfīr*). More than 500 scholars later endorsed this.

**4. Religious explanation.** The Quran treats diversity as part of God's plan (5:48; 11:118) but condemns hostile splitting (3:103, 105; 6:159).
- **The 73-sects hadith.** Abū Dāwūd 4596 is graded *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ* by al-Albānī. Abū Dāwūd 4597 adds "72 in the Fire, one in Paradise: the *jamāʿa*" and is graded *ḥasan*. Ibn Taymiyya held that the 72 are *not* unbelievers (Abdallah, *AFKAR* 2006). Al-Baghdādī's *al-Farq* forced known sects into the number 73 (Gömbeyaz, *ULUM* 2018).
- **"Disagreement is a mercy."** "*Ikhtilāf ummatī raḥma*" has no known chain (al-Subkī, al-Suyūṭī, al-Albānī), and Ibn Ḥazm condemned it. What is attested is al-Qāsim b. Muḥammad's saying that the Companions' differences are a mercy (Ibn Saʿd).
- **Sunni–Shiʿi recognition.** Shaltūt of al-Azhar ruled the Jaʿfarī (Shiʿi) school valid in 1959. Sistani and the Shaykh al-Azhar backed the Amman Message.

**5. Philosophy.** Classical theory separates the definitive (*qaṭʿī*) from the probable (*ẓannī*): certainty for the core, humility elsewhere. A scholar who reasons carefully and errs is still rewarded (Bukhārī 7352).

**6. History.** Of dozens of early legal schools, the four Sunni schools plus the Jaʿfarī, Zaydī and Ibāḍī survived. The Sunni–Shiʿi split began over leadership after 632. Türkiye has Hanafi, Shāfiʿī, Alevi-Bektashi and Jaʿfarī communities.

**7. Psychology.** Black-and-white religious thinking correlates with anxiety and intolerance. Intellectual humility predicts better relations between groups.

**8. Honest note.** Not every difference is harmless, and the Amman Message excluded some groups (e.g., Ahmadis). Both slogans overstate their case.

**9. Visuals.** A tree with the shared core as trunk, schools as branches and local customs as leaves. A *qaṭʿī/ẓannī* sorting game. A hadith grading card.

**10. Sources.** Abū Dāwūd 4596–97 (sunnah.com); al-Baghdādī, *al-Farq* (Ar.); Gömbeyaz, *ULUM* 1/2 (Tr.); Abdallah, *AFKAR* 7 (2006); [ammanmessage.com](https://ammanmessage.com/); [73 Sects (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/73_Sects_(Hadith)); TDV, "İhtilâf" (Tr.).

## Q20. Aisha's age at marriage (HIGH)
**1. Steelmanned.** Bukhārī and Muslim report marriage at six and consummation at nine. If the Prophet is a model for all time, did he endorse what we now call child abuse? Some legal systems cite the report against minimum-age laws.

**2. Why asked.** It is a leading online polemic, and many young people first meet it in hostile videos.

**3. Short answer.** The traditional report is in the most respected hadith collections. Some scholars argue Aisha was older, and a 2022 Oxford thesis argues the report itself arose later; others rebut both. Whatever the history, this site does not endorse child marriage today. It is contrary to Turkish law and to the *maqāṣid* (the Sharia's purposes, including preventing harm).

**4. Religious explanation.** Bukhārī 5133–5134 and Muslim 1422 carry the report, mostly through Hishām b. ʿUrwa ← ʿUrwa ← Aisha. Classical jurists tied consummation to physical readiness (*ṭāqa*). Later legal codes let the state set minimum ages under *siyāsa sharʿiyya* (governance within the Sharia). Revisionist chronologies (Kāndhlawī in Urdu, and some Turkish theologians) use Asmāʾ's age and Aisha's presence at Badr and Uḥud to argue she was in her late teens. These rely on uncertain reports [Debated].

**5. Philosophy.** Joshua Little (Oxford DPhil, 2022) traces every version to Hishām in Kufa between 754 and 765 CE. He says it arose "probably as a response to proto-Šīʿī polemics" and lacks "genuine historical memory." ICRAA ("The Age of Aisha," 2024) disagrees, noting that at least three others corroborate Hishām from ʿUrwa, and a dozen narrators besides ʿUrwa relate the same from Aisha.

**6. History.** Marriage at puberty was common in late antiquity and medieval Europe. Article 124 of the Turkish Civil Code sets the minimum age at 17 (16 with court permission). The medical risks of early marriage give a *maqāṣid* reason to prohibit it.

**7. Psychology.** Moral shock at this report is *right*, and it should be acknowledged. If the topic touches personal trauma, gently suggest support services.

**8. Honest note.** Aisha's exact age cannot be known. The report is strong by classical standards. Little's thesis is serious but contested. Revisionist chronologies are often overstated. Avoid number games; the firm statement is the ethical one.

**9. Visuals.** A transmission map from Medina to Kufa. A timeline of marriage-age norms. A "three positions, one ethic" card.

**10. Sources.** Bukhārī 5133–34; Muslim 1422; J.J. Little (2022); ICRAA appraisal; D. Spellberg (1994); Kāndhlawī, *Umr-e ʿĀʾisha* (Urdu); TMK md. 124 (Tr.); TDV, "Âişe" (Tr.); [Aisha (Wikipedia)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aisha).

## Q21. Slavery (HIGH)
**1. Steelmanned.** The Quran regulates slavery and concubinage (*mā malakat aymānukum*, "those your right hands possess") but never abolishes either. Abolition came under European pressure.

**2. Why asked.** ISIS revived enslavement in 2014, and modern moral certainty makes these texts jarring.

**3. Short answer.** The Quran did not abolish slavery. It made freeing slaves a repeated act of worship and of atonement for sins, and it opened legal paths to freedom. Abolition came much later and did not grow out of classical law. Today Muslim scholars overwhelmingly hold slavery to be prohibited.

**4. Religious explanation.**
- Freeing a slave is the "steep path" (90:13) and an atonement (4:92; 5:89; 58:3).
- Zakat can fund freeing captives (9:60).
- Slaves can buy their freedom by contract (*mukātaba*) (24:33).
- "Your slaves are your brothers" (Bukhārī 30).
- A concubine's child was born free, and the mother (*umm walad*) was freed at her master's death.

Kecia Ali (2010) shows that classical marriage law drew on an analogy with ownership. Jonathan Brown (2019) writes that "abolition is not indigenous to Islam," but argues that scholarly consensus and the *maqāṣid* now support it.

**5. Philosophy.** Reformists (ʿAbduh, Riḍā, Ibn ʿĀshūr) say the Sharia aims at freedom. The objection is that this gradual path stalled for 1,200 years. Concubinage without consent remains the hardest point, and no fully satisfying answer exists.

**6. History.** Ottoman steps:
- 1847: the Istanbul slave market closed.
- 1854–55: the Circassian trade was banned, which sparked a revolt in the Hijaz; the Hijaz was then exempted.
- 1857: the Black slave trade was banned.

Saudi Arabia abolished slavery in 1962 and Mauritania in 1981. Pérouse de Montclos (2002): unlike Christendom, Islam produced no abolitionist movement.

**7. Psychology.** Descendants of enslaved people, such as Afro-Turks, may feel this personally. Honesty respects them.

**8. Honest note.** "Islam abolished slavery" is false. "Islamic slavery was gentle" is cherry-picking. Mainstream scholars condemned ISIS (Open Letter to al-Baghdadi, 2014).

**9. Visuals.** A "doors to freedom" diagram. An abolition timeline. An emancipation-vs-abolition card.

**10. Sources.** J.A.C. Brown, *Slavery and Islam* (2019); K. Ali (2010); E. Toledano (1998); [Pérouse de Montclos, *Études* (2002, Fr.)](https://www.cairn.info/revue-etudes-2002-6-page-751.htm); [Gilli-Elewy, *Der Islam* 77 (2000, Ger.)](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/islm.2000.77.1.116/html?lang=en); TDV, "Köle" (Tr.).

## Q22. The Prophet's wars and Banu Qurayza (HIGH)
**1. Steelmanned.** A "mercy to the worlds" (21:107) led many campaigns. After the Battle of the Trench (627), the sources report that all adult males of the Qurayẓa tribe (400–900) were executed and the women and children enslaved.

**2. Why asked.** Polemicists treat it as decisive, and Muslims who care about Jewish–Muslim relations feel the discomfort.

**3. Short answer.** According to the sources, Qurayẓa broke a defence treaty during a siege that threatened Medina's survival. They surrendered to the judgement of their former ally Saʿd b. Muʿādh, whose verdict matched the war norms of the time (cf. Deuteronomy 20). The numbers are disputed. Even if the account is accepted, its moral weight is heavy and should be acknowledged.

**4. Religious explanation.** Fighting is permitted in self-defence (22:39–40), without transgression (2:190). Killing women and children is forbidden (Bukhārī 3015; Muslim 1744). The Quran alludes to the episode in 33:26. Saʿd's verdict is in Bukhārī 4121; the numbers come from Ibn Isḥāq. Jurists treated it as arbitration over a broken treaty (*naqḍ al-ʿahd*).

**5. Philosophy.** W.N. Arafat (*JRAS* 1976) argued that the figure of 600–900 cannot be true. He cited the late sources and a possible echo of Masada. M.J. Kister (*JSAI* 1986) answered him point by point, and most historians follow Kister on the core event. Collective execution violates modern humanitarian law. Defenders cite treason during a siege that threatened the city's existence. Critics reply that context explains but does not justify.

**6. History.** Reported numbers range from about 40 to 900 [Debated].

**7. Psychology.** Love for the Prophet and moral unease can coexist. The episode must be taught with no collective guilt and no antisemitism.

**8. Honest note.** No answer removes the moral weight. The core event is probably historical; the numbers are uncertain. It must never be used against Jews today.

**9. Visuals.** A map of Medina, 622–627. A "sources lab." The Prophet's rules of war shown beside the Geneva Conventions.

**10. Sources.** [Arafat, *JRAS* (1976)](https://www.academia.edu/34137671/Some_Reflections_on_the_Story_of_Banu_Quraiza_Vol_1_No2_Article); Kister, *JSAI* 8 (1986); B. Ahmad (1979); Bukhārī 4121; Ibn Hishām, *al-Sīra* (Ar.); TDV, "Kurayza, Benî" (Tr.); Hamidullah (Fr.); [WikiShia (Fr.)](https://fr.wikishia.net/view/Bataille_de_Ban%C3%AE_Qurayza).

## Q23. Polygyny (HIGH)
**1. Steelmanned.** Men may marry four wives; women may not. Equal treatment is near-impossible, and harm to co-wives is documented.

**2. Why asked.** Women feel threatened. In Türkiye, unofficial religious marriages (*imam nikahı*) leave women without legal protection.

**3. Short answer.** The Quran allows up to four wives on a strict condition of justice. It adds "if you fear injustice, then one," and later says full justice is impossible. Many scholars read this as an exception, not an ideal. Tunisia and Türkiye ban polygyny.

**4. Religious explanation.** The key verses are 4:3 (revealed in the context of orphans' rights) and 4:129. A man who favours one wife will come on Judgement Day "with one side drooping" (Abū Dāwūd 2133). The Prophet objected to ʿAlī taking a second wife (Bukhārī 5230). Interpretations:
- **Majority:** 4:129 is about love; 4:3 is about maintenance.
- **ʿAbduh and Riḍā:** read together, the verses are a restriction the state may enforce.
- **Tunisia (1956):** banned polygyny on Quranic grounds.
- **Mawdūdī (Urdu):** the verse makes polygyny conditional on justice.
- **Indonesia:** monogamous in principle but allows polygamy with court permission.

**5. Philosophy.** Defenders say it protects widows. Critics point to the asymmetry. A partial reply: in the Ḥanbalī school (Ibn Taymiyya), a wife can make monogamy a condition of her marriage contract.

**6. Data.** Only about 2% of the world's population lives in polygamous households, and in most countries the share is under 0.5% (Pew Research Center, 7 December 2020).

**7. Psychology.** Women should know their rights: divorce for harm (*ḍarar*) and access to counselling.

**8. Honest note.** "The Quran bans it" and "the Quran encourages it" both distort the text. Restricting it is legitimate *ijtihād* (independent legal reasoning).

**9. Visuals.** A justice overlay on the verses. A map of polygyny laws. A contract-conditions card.

**10. Sources.** Abū Dāwūd 2133; *Tafsīr al-Manār* (Ar.); Mawdūdī, "Masʾala-yi taʿaddud-i azwāj" (Urdu); Musawah brief; [Melia Rosa et al., *Jurnal Konstitusi* 21(4) (2024, Indon.)](https://jurnalkonstitusi.mkri.id/index.php/jk/article/download/2347/696/9038); TDV, "Taaddüd-i Zevcât" (Tr.).

## Q24. Hijab and coercion (HIGH)
**1. Steelmanned.** Some states force women to cover (Iran). Others banned it (Türkiye until 2010–13, France in schools). Some families pressure daughters. Where is the woman's own conscience in all this?

**2. Why asked.** Türkiye's bans and family pressure have hurt women on both sides.

**3. Short answer.** "No compulsion in religion" (2:256). Forced covering loses its value as worship, and bans violate conscience. The great majority of Sunni and Shiʿi scholars hold that covering the hair is obligatory; a modern minority disagrees. Women deserve to hear both views and to choose freely.

**4. Religious explanation.** The verses are 24:31 and 33:59. The four Sunni schools and the Jaʿfarī school all require covering. H. Yunus Apaydın (TDV, "Tesettür") notes that this was not doubted before the modern era. Hayrettin Karaman says no jurist ever permitted uncovering the head. A minority (Hüseyin Atay, Shaḥrūr) argues that *khimār* simply means "a cover" [Debated].

**5. Philosophy.** Three separate questions:
- What does God require? The majority view is strong.
- Who may enforce it? 2:256 applies.
- What does not covering mean? A sin, not unbelief.

**6. History.** Veiling predates Islam. Iran required hijab in workplaces by Khomeini's decree of 7 March 1979 and made it law for all women in April 1983. Türkiye lifted its bans in 2010–2013.

**7. Psychology.** Coercion in either direction breeds resentment and loss of faith.

**8. Honest note.** Presenting the minority view as equally weighted is misleading. The site's firm line is against coercion.

**9. Visuals.** A Venn diagram of ruling, enforcement and faith. A timeline of bans. A "two women, two pressures" card.

**10. Sources.** Apaydın, TDV "Tesettür" (Tr.); Karaman, Yeni Şafak (Tr.); al-Qurṭubī on 24:31 (Ar.); Mawdūdī, *Purdah* (Urdu); L. Ahmed (2011).

## Q25. Homosexuality (HIGH)
**1. Steelmanned.** Sexual orientation is not chosen. Requiring lifelong celibacy seems unjust, and social cruelty pushes some people toward suicide.

**2. Why asked.** People ask because of their own identity, because of friends, or out of modern ethical concern.

**3. Short answer.** Islam separates attraction from acts, and feelings are not sins. Mainstream Islamic law prohibits same-sex acts; a small revisionist minority disagrees. Every person has God-given dignity. Mockery, violence and family rejection are wrong.

**4. Religious explanation.** The story of Lot (Lūṭ) (7:80–84) is classically read as condemning male same-sex acts. Abū Ḥanīfa held that there is no fixed *ḥadd* penalty. Scott Kugle (2010) reads the story as being about rape and inhospitality. Mobeen Vaid (*AJISS* 34(3), 2017) argues that Kugle misreads both the Quran and Ibn Ḥazm [Debated].

**5. Philosophy.** The objection that lifelong celibacy is a different kind of burden is serious. It has no fully satisfying answer. The theological reply is that God tests people differently (2:286).

**6. Science.** There is no single "gay gene" (Ganna et al., *Science* 2019, ~500,000 people). Conversion therapy is harmful.

**7. Psychology.** Safety comes first: 112 in Türkiye, or a crisis line.

**8. Honest note.** The mainstream position is clear, and the revisionist view is a disputed minority. The story of Lot must never be used to dehumanise anyone.

**9. Visuals.** An attraction → act → dignity diagram. A card on the debate over penalties. A map of support services.

**10. Sources.** Kugle (2010); [Vaid, *AJISS* (2017)](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/340911894_Can_Islam_Accommodate_Homosexual_Acts_Qur'anic_Revisionism_and_the_Case_of_Scott_Kugle); K. Ali (2016); Ibn Ḥazm, *Ṭawq al-Ḥamāma* (Ar.); El-Rouayheb (2005); Ganna et al. (2019).

## Q26. Isn't belief just upbringing?
**1. Steelmanned.** If I had been born in Tamil Nadu, I would be Hindu. Belief tracks birthplace.

**2. Why asked.** It is common among university students.

**3. Short answer.** How a belief arose does not settle whether it is true; assuming it does is the genetic fallacy. The Quran itself rebukes inherited faith (2:170; 43:22–23). A natural openness to God fits *fiṭra*, the innate disposition. The real question is whether you have reasons now.

**4. Religious explanation.** On *fiṭra*, see 30:30 and Bukhārī 1385. Al-Ghazālī's own doubt began with exactly this question (*Munqidh*).

**5. Philosophy.** Debunking arguments prove too much. Barrett (2012) finds children are inclined to believe, though not in any specific religious or theological tradition.

**6. Science.** The claim that analytic thinking reduces belief did not hold up in a large replication (Sanchez et al., *PLOS ONE* 2017).

**7. Psychology.** Owning one's faith is a normal stage of development.

**8. Honest note.** Upbringing does shape belief. *Fiṭra* fits the data but is not proven by it.

**9. Visuals.** "Two roads." A quiz. A seed, soil and gardener card.

**10. Sources.** Barrett (2012); Sanchez et al., *PLOS ONE* (2017); Ibn Taymiyya, *Darʾ* (Ar.); al-Ghazālī, *al-Munqidh* (Ar./Tr.); [Aimmah et al., *Taʾdibuna* (2025, Indon.)](https://ejournal.uika-bogor.ac.id/index.php/TADIBUNA/article/view/17864); TDV, "Fıtrat" (Tr.).

## Q27. Why believe Muhammad was a prophet?
**1. Steelmanned.** Many people have claimed to be prophets, and his own followers wrote the sources.

**2. Why asked.** It is the next step for people who believe in God but doubt revelation.

**3. Short answer.** The case is cumulative; together the strands make a strong rope:
- his honesty before the mission (*al-Amīn*, "the trustworthy");
- a Quran from a man who did not write;
- a simple life;
- transformed followers;
- some fulfilled predictions.

**4. Religious explanation.** See 10:16, the Quran's challenge to produce a chapter like it (2:23), and the prediction about Byzantium (30:2–4). Abū Sufyān, then an enemy, admitted to Heraclius that Muhammad never lied (Bukhārī 7). See also Nursi's *19. Mektup* and Muṭahharī's *Nubuwwat*.

**5. Philosophy.** Was he a liar, deluded, or truthful? Lying fits poorly with his poverty and persecution.

**6. History.** Non-Muslim sources from the 630s–660s mention him. The Birmingham manuscript is dated 568–645 CE.

**7. Psychology.** Stories from his life persuade more than syllogisms do.

**8. Honest note.** Some prophecies are overstated, and "scientific miracles" are weak.

**9. Visuals.** An evidence braid. A timeline of his 40 years before revelation. A Q&A with Heraclius.

**10. Sources.** Bukhārī 7; Ibn Taymiyya, *al-Jawāb* (Ar.); Nursi (Tr.); Donner (2010); Hamidullah (Fr.); Muṭahharī (Per.).

## Q28. Why Islam and not another religion?
**1. Steelmanned.** Every faith has saints, and each claims to be true.

**2. Why asked.** Friendships with people of other faiths.

**3. Short answer.** Islam presents itself as the continuation of all the earlier prophets. Its case rests on strict monotheism, a well-preserved scripture and a historically traceable prophet. Conviction can coexist with respect.

**4. Religious explanation.** 2:285; 5:48; 2:62 (read differently by different scholars); 29:46; 3:85. Nasr and Winter describe Islam as a return to *dīn al-fiṭra*, the religion of human nature.

**5. Philosophy.** Apply the same criteria to Islam's weaknesses as to other faiths.

**6. History.** The Quran's transmission is well documented.

**7. Psychology.** Humility is compatible with conviction.

**8. Honest note.** The case is strong, but it does not compel.

**9. Visuals.** A chain of prophets. A criteria compass. A verses card.

**10. Sources.** Nasr (2002); Khalil (2012); Sachedina (2001); Winter (2008); TDV, "Din" (Tr.).

## Q29. Why would a self-sufficient God create?
**1. Steelmanned.** If God needs nothing, creating seems arbitrary, and "to worship Me" (51:56) sounds like a need for praise.

**2. Why asked.** Crises of meaning.

**3. Short answer.** God creates from generosity and wisdom, not need. *ʿIbāda* (worship) in 51:56 is read as coming to know God, which is our flourishing.

**4. Religious explanation.** Mujāhid read 51:56 as "to know Me." According to Ibn Taymiyya, "I was a hidden treasure" has no chain [Debated]. Ashʿarīs deny that God acts for purposes; Māturīdīs and Ibn al-Qayyim affirm that God acts with wisdom (*ḥikma*).

**5. Philosophy.** Goodness naturally shares itself.

**6. Science.** A sense of meaning predicts well-being (Steger; Frankl).

**7. Psychology.** If questions of meaning come with hopelessness, seek professional care.

**8. Honest note.** Some mystery remains.

**9. Visuals.** A radiating light. A 51:56 card. A reflection exercise.

**10. Sources.** Ibn al-Qayyim, *Shifāʾ al-ʿalīl* (Ar.); Ṭabāṭabāʾī, *al-Mīzān* (Per.); Elmalılı (Tr.); Steger (2006).

## Q30. Evidence for an afterlife
**1. Steelmanned.** The mind depends on the brain, and near-death experiences (NDEs) may be produced by a dying brain.

**2. Why asked.** Grief and injustice.

**3. Short answer.** The Quran argues from the first creation and from justice. NDE research is intriguing but not proof.

**4. Religious explanation.** 36:78–79; 38:28. Al-Ghazālī defended bodily resurrection. Muṭahharī and Nursi argue from justice.

**5. Philosophy.** The moral argument (Kant): justice seems to require more than this life.

**6. Science.**
- **AWARE I (2014):** 2,060 cardiac arrests; 9 of 101 interviewed survivors had NDEs.
- **AWARE II (*Resuscitation* 2023):** 567 cardiac arrests and 53 survivors. About 40% reported awareness and about 20% a "recalled experience of death."

The authors say this neither proved nor disproved that the experiences are real.

**7. Psychology.** Complicated grief needs a clinician.

**8. Honest note.** NDEs are not proof. No patient identified a hidden visual target.

**9. Visuals.** A seed growing into a tree. An AWARE chart. A justice scale.

**10. Sources.** Parnia et al., *Resuscitation* 191 (2023); Parnia (2014); al-Ghazālī, *Tahāfut* (Ar.); Nursi (Tr.); Muṭahharī (Per.).

## Q31. Are my non-Muslim loved ones lost? (HIGH)
**1. Steelmanned.** Must I believe my kind grandmother is condemned?

**2. Why asked.** Grief and mixed-faith families.

**3. Short answer.** Only God knows hearts. Many classical scholars excused people who never heard the message clearly. You may grieve, hope and remember them.

**4. Religious explanation.** "We do not punish until We send a messenger" (17:15). Al-Ghazālī (*Fayṣal*) excuses those who received a distorted picture of Islam. 9:113 restricts asking forgiveness for some of the dead. The Prophet wept at his mother's grave (Muslim 976). See also Q14 (Batch 2).

**5. Philosophy.** God's knowledge of each person is complete; ours is not.

**6. Data.** Mixed-faith families are increasing.

**7. Psychology.** Grief counselling helps.

**8. Honest note.** Scholars differ on how these texts apply, and no one knows anyone's final state.

**9. Visuals.** A letters card. A mercy slider. A "what I know vs what God knows" diagram.

**10. Sources.** al-Ghazālī, *Fayṣal* (tr. S. Jackson 2002); Muslim 976; Khalil (2012); Ibn al-Qayyim, *Ḥādī al-arwāḥ* (Ar.).

## Q32. Is deism a stable position?
**1. Steelmanned.** I believe in a Creator but reject religion.

**2. Why asked.** Türkiye's 2018 debate began at the "Gençlik ve İnanç" workshop of 50 religion teachers in Konya (4 March 2018). Its closing statement said deism was coming to the fore among young people with problems of belief. Batch 2 found actual deism under 2%, though 11% of 18–24-year-olds feel close to the label.

**3. Short answer.** Deism is often a resting place, not a destination. A good Creator would hardly stay silent. Much Turkish "deism" is a protest against religious people.

**4. Religious explanation.** 6:91; 29:61–63; the classical ideas of God's grace (*luṭf*) and wisdom (*ḥikma*). See also Q3 (Batch 1).

**5. Philosophy.** If God is personal, communication is likely.

**6. History.** English and French deism faded within a few generations.

**7. Psychology.** A deist pause deserves patience.

**8. Honest note.** Some people remain deists for life.

**9. Visuals.** A watchmaker and a letter. A KONDA chart. A path map.

**10. Sources.** [KONDA 2025 (Tr.)](https://paraanaliz.com/haberler/konda-arastirmasi-turkiyede-dindarlik-geriliyor-inancsizlik-artiyor); Bianet (2018, Tr.); Diyanet Aylık Dergi 2018 (Tr.); Ekici (Tr.); Aimmah et al. (2025, Indon.).

## Q33. Miracles vs natural laws
**1. Steelmanned.** Hume argued that testimony to a miracle is always less probable than error.

**2. Why asked.** Common among STEM students.

**3. Short answer.** For al-Ghazālī, natural regularity is God's habit (*ʿāda*), and miracles are meaningful signs. Hume assumes what he needs to prove.

**4. Religious explanation.** In the 17th discussion of the *Tahāfut*, al-Ghazālī argues that observation shows only that two events occur together, not that one necessarily causes the other. Griffel (SEP) argues that he allowed both occasionalism and secondary causality. Ibn Rushd replied that denying causes destroys knowledge.

**5. Philosophy.** Laws describe regularities; they do not compel.

**6. Science.** Cartwright and Mumford treat laws as idealisations.

**7. Psychology.** Faith that rests only on miracles is fragile.

**8. Honest note.** Accepting miracles depends on prior belief in God.

**9. Visuals.** Fire and cotton. "Habit vs law." Three thinkers compared.

**10. Sources.** al-Ghazālī, *Tahāfut*; Ibn Rushd, *Tahāfut al-tahāfut*; Griffel, SEP; SEP "Occasionalism"; Hume, *Enquiry* §10; [Causality in the debate between al-Ghazālī and Ibn Rushd](https://islamic-philosophy.com/causality-in-the-debate-between-al-ghazali-and-ibn-rushd/).

## Q34. Consciousness: can matter explain the mind?
**1. Steelmanned.** Neuroscience explains more every year, and "soul" may just be a placeholder.

**2. Why asked.** Neuroscience and AI.

**3. Short answer.** Why there is any experience at all is the unsolved "hard problem" (Chalmers 1995). The Quran says the spirit is "of my Lord's command" (17:85). See also sign S29.

**4. Religious explanation.** Ṭabāṭabāʾī (*al-Mīzān* vol. 13) reads *amr* as God's direct creative command.

**5. Philosophy.** PhilPapers 2020: 51.93% physicalism, 32.08% non-physicalism.

**6. Science.** Several theories exist, but none is accepted as a solution.

**7. Psychology.** Believers and sceptics can share the mystery.

**8. Honest note.** The hard problem does not prove that a soul exists.

**9. Visuals.** "Who sees red?" A bar chart. Calligraphy.

**10. Sources.** Chalmers (1995); Bourget & Chalmers (2023); *al-Mīzān* 13 (WikiFeqh); Ibn al-Qayyim, *Kitāb al-Rūḥ*; Nagel (1974).

## Q35. Why fast? Why so many rules?
**1. Steelmanned.** Why would God care when I eat?

**2. Why asked.** The rules can feel purposeless.

**3. Short answer.** Some rules have clear purposes; others are acts of pure devotion (*taʿabbudī*). Fasting builds self-mastery and empathy. Its health effects are modest.

**4. Religious explanation.** "So that you may attain *taqwā*" (2:183). A fast that does not stop false speech is worthless (Bukhārī 1903). Al-Shāṭibī: in acts of worship, devotion is the default.

**5. Philosophy.** Practice shapes character.

**6. Science.** An umbrella review of 11 meta-analyses ([*Nutrition Reviews* 2025](https://academic.oup.com/nutritionreviews/article-abstract/83/2/e711/7604369)) found benefits. Weight drops 1.24 kg by the end of Ramadan, but the loss shrinks to 0.27 kg within 2–6 weeks (*Nutrients* 2019).

**7. Psychology.** People with eating disorders or diabetes should take medical advice; the exemptions are a mercy.

**8. Honest note.** Health is not the "reason" for fasting.

**9. Visuals.** A map of rules and their purposes. An infographic. Three levels of fasting.

**10. Sources.** Jahrami et al. (2025); Fernando et al. (2019); Glazier et al. (2018); al-Shāṭibī (Ar.); Diyanet, "Oruç" (Tr.).

## Q36. Is claiming one religion is true arrogant?
**1. Steelmanned.** Billions of people disagree.

**2. Why asked.** A pluralist culture.

**3. Short answer.** Every view excludes others, including "all religions are equal." Arrogance concerns *how* a belief is held, not *whether* it is held.

**4. Religious explanation.** 109:6; 10:99; 49:13. See also M11 (humility).

**5. Philosophy.** Plantinga (1995) defends exclusivism; Hick's pluralism is itself contested.

**6. Science.** Research on intellectual humility (Leary et al.).

**7. Psychology.** Humble confidence improves relations between groups.

**8. Honest note.** Exclusivism has sometimes been arrogant.

**9. Visuals.** "Every map excludes." How vs whether. Verses.

**10. Sources.** Plantinga (1995); Hick (1989); Khalil (2012); Nasr (1981).

## Q37. Can I be "spiritual but not religious"?
**1. Steelmanned.** Why not keep faith personal?

**2. Why asked.** KONDA 2025: "believer but not religious" rose from 31% to 34%.

**3. Short answer.** Faith in the heart is the foundation, and practice sustains it, as body and soul go together. Start small.

**4. Religious explanation.** 103:3; the hadith of Jibrīl (Muslim 8); al-Qushayrī.

**5. Philosophy.** Individualised religion lacks community (Heelas & Woodhead 2005).

**6. Science.** 27% of US adults call themselves spiritual but not religious, up 8 points in five years (Pew Research Center, survey of 5,002 adults, 2017).

**7. Psychology.** An in-between stage can be healthy.

**8. Honest note.** KONDA's "inançlı" (believer) category is broad.

**9. Visuals.** Body and soul. A chart. A "start small" card.

**10. Sources.** KONDA 2025 (Tr.); Heelas & Woodhead (2005); Pew (2017); Muslim 8.

## Q38. How do I rebuild faith after a crisis of doubt? (HIGH)
**1. Steelmanned.** My certainty is gone. How do I come back without pretending?

**2. Why asked.** In one US sample, 45.4% of adults reported doubt-related struggles (Exline et al.).

**3. Short answer.** You don't need your old certainty back all at once. Al-Ghazālī went through two crises and came back deeper. Be honest, find a guide, return to small practices, keep good company, and get therapy if needed.

**4. Religious explanation.** Al-Ghazālī's first crisis ended through "a light God cast into the breast" (*Munqidh*). Abraham asked "so that my heart may be at rest" (2:260). Distress at doubt is "clear faith" (Muslim 132).

**5. Philosophy.** Faith is trust under uncertainty.

**6. Science.** On the RSS (Religious and Spiritual Struggles scale), the doubt subscale predicted slightly *less* depression (Exline et al. 2014). A meta-analysis (Bockrath et al. 2022) links spiritual struggles to later distress.

**7. Psychology.** Seek help for hopelessness or religious OCD: 112 or a community mental health centre (TRSM).

**8. Honest note.** Not everyone returns, and that should be respected.

**9. Visuals.** A map of al-Ghazālī's journey. A steps tracker. A help card.

**10. Sources.** al-Ghazālī, *al-Munqidh*; Exline et al. (2014); Bockrath et al. (2022); Tedeschi & Calhoun (1996); H.H. Behr (2018); [APA, spiritual struggles](https://www.apa.org/topics/belief-systems-religion/spiritual-struggles); [Templeton spiritual struggle white paper](https://www.templeton.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/10/Spiritual-Struggle-Whitepaper_FINAL-10.5.pdf).
