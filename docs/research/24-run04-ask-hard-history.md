### The question, at its strongest
"The two most trusted hadith collections say the Prophet married Aisha at six and consummated the marriage at nine. Muslims call him the best example for all time. If that is true, then either a nine-year-old's marriage was right — which we now rightly call abuse — or the example is not for all time. Some governments and preachers still cite this report against minimum-age laws. Saying 'it was normal then' does not help: if God's final messenger only did what was normal, what makes him a moral model at all?"

### Why people ask it
It is one of the most common attacks on Islam online, and many young Muslims first meet it in a hostile video. It also matters in law: in some countries the report is still used to resist raising the legal age of marriage.

### Short answer
The report is in the most respected hadith collections, and it cannot honestly be called weak by classical standards. A 2022 Oxford thesis by Joshua Little argues that the earliest form of the report "appears to have been created and disseminated" by one narrator, Hisham ibn ʿUrwa, after he moved to Iraq late in his life; other scholars reply that it is corroborated by several independent chains. Revisionist calculations that make Aisha a teenager rely on reports that are weaker than the ones they try to overturn. The more important question is moral: classical scholars never treated everything the Prophet (pbuh) did as a rule for all times — they distinguished his acts as a messenger from his acts as a man of his society, and the Quran says some of his marriages were "only for you excluding the [other] believers" (33:50). Classical law tied consummation to physical readiness, and modern Muslim states use the Sharia's own tools to set minimum ages. Today, given what we know about the harm of early marriage, child marriage is wrong, and this site says so plainly.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If this subject touches your own life — if you were married, promised or abused as a child — what happened to you was not your fault, and it was not God's will for you.** You deserve safety and care. If you are in danger now, call your local emergency number. If you want to talk, a doctor, a counsellor or a trusted person can help; findahelpline.com lists free, confidential helplines by country, including for survivors of abuse. Nothing on this page asks you to accept what was done to you.

**If you are troubled by the report itself, the discomfort is a moral response, not a lack of faith.** You can hold your love for the Prophet (pbuh) and your conviction that children must be protected together. Many scholars do.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The verse most used in this debate: 4:6.** "And test the orphans [in their abilities] until they reach marriageable age. Then if you perceive in them sound judgement, release their property to them" (4:6). It is in Sura al-Nisaʾ, Medinan, addressed to guardians of orphans. The verse is about property: a guardian must test an orphan and hand over their wealth once they reach "marriageable age" (*balaghū al-nikāḥ*) and show "sound judgement" (*rushd*). It does not itself set an age for marriage.

**Why it matters.** It treats "marriageable age" as a stage of life that comes with maturity of judgement. The classical minority who forbade marriage before puberty read it as making maturity a condition of marriage; the majority answered that it concerns property only (Baderin, 2018). Modern reformers use it in the same way. This is a legal argument from the verse, not something the verse states directly.

**The verse cited on the other side: 65:4.** "And those who no longer expect menstruation among your women - if you doubt, then their period is three months, and [also for] those who have not menstruated" (65:4), from Sura al-Talaq, Medinan, setting the waiting period after divorce. The majority of classical jurists read "those who have not menstruated" as including girls before puberty, and therefore as implying that such marriages could exist (Sedick; Baderin). Some contemporary commentators read it as referring to adult women whose periods have not come (Baderin). The verse does not command or encourage marrying children; it regulates waiting periods.

**What the Quran says marriage is for.** "And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy" (30:21). Tranquillity, affection and mercy describe a relationship between people able to give them.

#### sunnah — What the hadith reports say
**The texts.** Aisha (ra) is reported as saying that the Prophet (pbuh) "married her when she was six years old and he consummated his marriage when she was nine years old, and then she remained with him for nine years (i.e., till his death)" ({hadith:bukhari:5133}); a parallel version is {hadith:bukhari:5134}. In Sahih Muslim, another version says he "married her when she was seven years old", that "he was taken to his house as a bride when she was nine", that "her dolls were with her", and that "when he (the Holy Prophet) died she was eighteen years old" ({hadith:muslim:1422c}). The difference between six and seven is in the reports themselves.

**What the terms mean.** A *hadith* is a report of what the Prophet (pbuh) said or did, or — as here — what a Companion said about him. Bukhari (d. 870) and Muslim (d. 875) compiled the two collections Sunni Muslims regard as most reliable. Each report comes with an *isnād*, a chain of narrators: "A told me that B told him that C said…". In Bukhari 5133 the chain is Muhammad ibn Yusuf ← Sufyan ← Hisham ← his father (ʿUrwa) ← Aisha; in 5134 it is Muʿalla ibn Asad ← Wuhayb ← Hisham ibn ʿUrwa ← his father ← Aisha (sunnah.com, Arabic chains). In Muslim 1422c it is ʿAbd ibn Humayd ← ʿAbd al-Razzaq ← Maʿmar ← al-Zuhri ← ʿUrwa ← Aisha.

**Why one narrator matters.** Hisham ibn ʿUrwa (d. 763–765), Aisha's great-nephew, appears in most chains. If almost every version passes through one person, historians ask whether the report began with him. This is the point Little's thesis turns on.

#### history — What historians argue
**Little's thesis.** Joshua Little's Oxford doctoral thesis (2022) uses *isnād-cum-matn* analysis — comparing the chains and the wording of every surviving version to reconstruct the earliest form of a report. He traces the earliest form of the report to the Medinan narrator Hisham ibn ʿUrwa, who, according to the abstract, created and spread it "after he moved to Iraq towards the end of his life", and places the report's origin in "the sectarian milieu of mid-8th-Century Iraq". He proposes that it was probably a reaction to "local proto-Šīʿī polemics against his great-aunt, ʿĀʾišah" (abstract). "Proto-Shiʿi" means early partisans of ʿAli, before Shiʿism became a defined school, some of whom attacked Aisha because she had fought against ʿAli. The abstract does not explain how the age report answered them; the full thesis has not yet been consulted.

**The reply.** Waqar Akbar Cheema (ICRAA, 2024) argues that at least three others besides Hisham relayed the report from ʿUrwa — al-Zuhri (via Maʿmar), ʿAbdullah ibn ʿUrwa, and Abu al-Zinad — and that about a dozen narrators besides ʿUrwa relayed it from Aisha, including al-Aswad ibn Yazid, Abu Salama ibn ʿAbd al-Rahman and ʿAmra bint ʿAbd al-Rahman. The al-Zuhri chain is in Sahih Muslim (1422c, above). Cheema criticises the common-link method for overlooking how much of the early hadith literature is lost, and says Little offers no convincing motive for Hisham to invent the report.

**The revisionist chronologies.** An anonymous contributor to the Yaqeen Institute (2018) replies that… [as now], and that at Uhud Aisha was carrying water, "acting as a nurse, not as a combatant". Kandhlawi's book itself has not yet been consulted.

**Where the evidence points.** By the standards of classical hadith criticism, the report is strong. Little's thesis is a serious scholarly challenge from a different method; it is disputed. The teenage-age calculations are the weakest of the three positions, because they set aside explicit reports for indirect inferences. So the honest answer to "nine or late teens?" is: the sources say nine; some historians doubt the sources; the late-teens view is not well supported. None of this decides the moral question.

#### scholars — How scholars separate the Prophet's acts from binding rules
**Not everything the Prophet (pbuh) did was legislation.** The Tunisian jurist Muhammad al-Tahir ibn ʿAshur (d. 1973), in his *Treatise on Maqāṣid al-Sharīʿah*, lists the capacities in which the Prophet (pbuh) acted: "legislation, issuing edicts (fatwa), adjudication, political leadership of the state, guidance, conciliation, advice to those seeking his opinion, counseling, spiritual inspiration, teaching high and lofty truths, disciplining, and non-instructive ordinary statements". Only some of these bind every Muslim in every age. Earlier, the Maliki jurist al-Qarafi (d. 1285) distinguished the Prophet's acts as messenger and mufti from his acts as judge and as head of state (Jackson, 1993; Fadel's translation of al-Qarafi).

**His marriages were explicitly special.** The Quran says of one kind of marriage permitted to him: "[this is] only for you excluding the [other] believers" (33:50). Classical law built a whole category of rulings "specific to the Prophet" (*khuṣūṣiyyāt*). The minority jurist Ibn Shubruma called the Aisha report itself "a special case (*khāṣṣ*)" (Baderin, 2018).

**So what does "a model for all time" mean?** In the classical sense, the Prophet (pbuh) is the model in his faith, his character and the law he taught as a messenger. Acts that belonged to the customs of his society — what he ate, how he dressed, the age at which marriages were arranged in seventh-century Arabia — were not, by themselves, commands. The argument is not "it was normal, so it was fine". It is: a marriage at that age was within his society's norms, was not presented by him as a rule for others, and does not oblige or permit Muslims to do the same when the conditions that made it acceptable to that society — and the knowledge of harm we now have — are different. *Limit:* critics answer that a moral model should have risen above his society's norms here, as he did on other matters; this page cannot remove that objection, only explain why the tradition does not treat the act as a command.

#### fiqh — What the four schools hold
**The majority allowed a guardian to contract a minor's marriage.** According to Baderin (2018), the Hanafi, Maliki, Shafiʿi, Hanbali and Ithna ʿAshari (Twelver Shiʿi) schools held that the marriage of a minor "may be contracted by the father or guardian acting in the minor's best interest", that consummation must wait until maturity, and that some jurists gave the child the "option of puberty" (*khiyār al-bulūgh*) to annul the marriage before consummation. Ibn Qudama (d. 1223), the leading Hanbali jurist, claimed scholarly consensus that a father may compel a prepubescent daughter into a suitable match (CIS article, abstract).

**Who could compel.** According to Sedick (SeekersGuidance), the Maliki and Hanbali schools limited this power (*wilāyat al-ijbār*) to the father or his appointed executor; the Shafiʿis to the father or grandfather; the Hanafis extended it to other close relatives under conditions. He cites al-Zuhayli's *al-Fiqh al-Islami wa-Adillatuh* and al-Shirbini's *Mughni al-Muhtaj*.

**Consummation only when she can bear it.** Classical law separated the contract from consummation, which was tied to physical readiness (*ṭāqa*). Sedick states that "consummation is only permissible when the individual is physically and emotionally ready", grounding it in the rule "no harm and no reciprocal harm". The CIS article finds that there was in fact no consensus on when a girl could bear it.

**The minority who forbade it.** Ibn Shubruma (Kufa, 8th century) and Abu Bakr al-Asamm (Basra, 9th century) held that a minor may not be married until puberty, because marriage requires consent and serves needs a child does not have (Baderin, citing Ibn Hazm's *al-Muhalla*).

**On what basis is it wrong today?** Three steps, each from within Islamic law: (1) the classical permission was a permission, not a command, and was always limited by harm; (2) the state may set conditions on permitted acts for the public good — the principle of governance within the Sharia (*siyāsa sharʿiyya*) — as many Muslim states have done; (3) medical evidence now shows serious harm, so the harm limit the jurists themselves set is reached. The classical minority view of Ibn Shubruma gives a further ground. *Limit:* this means the prohibition rests on law and evidence, not on an explicit text; a group that rejects the state's authority could try to revive the permission, which is why clear law and public argument matter.

#### science — What the evidence on harm shows
**Early pregnancy is dangerous.** [Established.] The WHO's fact sheet (10 April 2024) states that adolescent mothers aged 10–19 "face higher risks of eclampsia, puerperal endometritis and systemic infections than women aged 20–24 years", and that their babies "face higher risks of low birth weight, preterm birth and severe neonatal condition". In 2019, girls aged 15–19 in low- and middle-income countries had an estimated 21 million pregnancies each year. "As of 2021, the estimated global number of child brides was 650 million", and "child marriage places girls at increased risk of pregnancy". WHO's fact sheet "Adolescents: health risks and solutions" (26 November 2024) states that "complications from pregnancy and childbirth are among the leading causes of death for girls aged 15–19 years globally" (checked by the source-checker; a 2017 WHO graphic called them "the leading cause").

**What Muslim-majority states do now.** According to Musawah, Egypt and Kuwait set 18 for both sexes and bar registering younger marriages; Pakistan's Sindh province criminalises marriage under 18; Jordan sets 18 with a judicial exception down to 15 (Musawah's page also lists Iraq this way. In 2025 Iraq amended its personal status law so that couples may choose to be governed by a Shiʿi (Jaʿfari) code instead of the 1959 law; human rights groups fear it will allow lower marriage ages, but when Human Rights Watch reported in March 2025 the code had not yet been written, and the 1959 law sets 18, or 15 with a judge's permission); Morocco, Tunisia, Oman and the UAE set 18 with judicial exceptions; Algeria sets 19; Indonesia sets 19 but allows court exceptions with no floor. So the law varies, and exceptions remain a real problem.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"If he is the model for all time, he endorsed it."** *Reply:* classical scholars distinguished his acts as messenger from his acts as a man of his society (Ibn ʿAshur; al-Qarafi), and the Quran itself marks his marriages as special (33:50). *Limit:* this explains why the act is not binding; it does not make it comfortable, and critics will say a moral exemplar should have acted differently.

**"It was normal then" is relativism.** *Reply:* the claim is not that whatever was normal was right, but that the act was not presented as a rule, that classical law limited it by harm, and that our knowledge of harm has changed what the same principles require. *Limit:* the classical jurists did permit contracts with children, and that history should not be hidden.

**"The report is fabricated."** *Reply:* this is Little's view, and it is serious, but others show corroborating chains (al-Zuhri in Muslim 1422c). *Limit:* resting one's faith on a single disputed thesis is fragile either way; the ethical answer does not depend on the historical one.

#### views — The range of views
- **Traditional view** (ICRAA; Yaqeen 2018) — the report is authentic; six or seven at marriage, nine at consummation.
- **Little (2022)** — the report originated with Hisham in mid-eighth-century Iraq, probably in response to proto-Shiʿi polemics.
- **Revisionist chronologies** (Kandhlawi and others) — Aisha was in her late teens; replies say these rest on weaker reports.
- **Classical majority law** — guardian may contract a minor's marriage; consummation only when she can bear it.
- **Classical minority** (Ibn Shubruma, al-Asamm) — no marriage before puberty.
- **Modern Muslim states** — minimum ages, mostly 18, with exceptions.

### Questions readers ask next
**Which is more likely: nine, or late teens?** The sources say nine. Little doubts the sources. The late-teens view relies on weaker, indirect reports.

**What do Bukhari, Muslim, hadith and *isnād* mean?** See "What the hadith reports say" above: two major collections, reports about the Prophet (pbuh), and the chain of people who passed each report on.

**What were the proto-Shiʿi polemics?** Early partisans of ʿAli attacked Aisha after she fought against him. Little proposes the age report arose in that setting; how exactly is argued in his thesis, which has not yet been consulted in full.

**How do mainstream Muslims handle this report today?** Most accept it as authentic; most Muslim-majority states nonetheless set legal minimum ages. Scholars such as those cited here treat the marriage as specific to its time and to the Prophet (pbuh), not as a model to copy.

**Could child marriage become allowed again?** Only by ignoring the harm principle the classical jurists themselves set, and the state's authority to regulate marriage. That is why legal protection matters.

**Was Aisha harmed?** The reports describe her as a scholar, a teacher of the Companions and a woman of strong voice for decades after. That does not make early marriage right today; it is a description of her life as the sources give it.

### Honest limits
Aisha's exact age cannot be known with certainty. The report is strong by classical standards; Little's thesis is serious but contested; the revisionist chronologies are weak. Classical law did allow guardians to contract marriages for children, and that cannot be explained away. The ethical position of this site — that child marriage is wrong today — rests on the harm principle, the state's authority under the Sharia, and modern evidence, not on proving the report false.

### The question, at its strongest
"The Quran speaks of 'those your right hands possess' and permits men to have sex with women they own. It regulates slavery but never abolishes it. The Prophet himself owned slaves and had a concubine. Muslim societies held slaves for twelve centuries, and abolition came under European pressure, with some Muslim scholars resisting it. In 2014, ISIS enslaved Yazidi women and quoted the classical texts. How can a just God's final law have allowed this, and what stops someone from bringing it back?"

### Why people ask it
ISIS's enslavement of Yazidi women in 2014 made the question urgent. Modern readers rightly see slavery as one of history's great wrongs, and find the texts jarring. Descendants of enslaved people, in Africa, the Middle East and elsewhere, ask it about their own history.

### Short answer
The Quran did not abolish slavery. It made freeing slaves an act of high virtue and a required atonement for several sins, gave slaves a right to buy their freedom, and limited the ways people could be enslaved. Classical law allowed a master to have sex with his female slave without her consent, and the Prophet (pbuh) himself had slaves and at least one concubine. Muslim-led abolition came late, in the nineteenth and twentieth centuries, largely under outside pressure. Today, Muslim scholars overwhelmingly hold slavery to be forbidden, on the grounds of scholarly consensus, binding treaties, and the Sharia's aim of freedom. That modern position is real and widely shared, but it is not the classical one, and honesty requires saying both.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If you are descended from enslaved people, your history is not a footnote.** Your ancestors were people "honored" by God (17:70), whatever their legal status, and no defence of a legal system should make their suffering smaller. You are right to want the truth told without softening. You may also find meaning in the Quran's insistence that "the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you" (49:13), and in the many freed slaves the tradition honours, such as Zayd ibn Haritha and Bilal.

**If you are a Muslim and this topic shakes you,** that is a sign of a working conscience. You do not have to defend everything in history to keep your faith.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Freeing slaves as virtue and atonement.** "And what can make you know what is [breaking through] the difficult pass? It is the freeing of a slave" (90:12–13), from Sura al-Balad, Meccan. Righteousness includes giving wealth "for freeing slaves" (2:177). Other verses (4:92; 5:89; 58:3; not quoted here) make freeing a slave an atonement for manslaughter, broken oaths and a pre-Islamic form of divorce.

**The right to buy freedom.** "And those who seek a contract [for eventual emancipation] from among whom your right hands possess - then make a contract with them if you know there is within them goodness and give them from the wealth of Allah which He has given you. And do not compel your slave girls to prostitution, if they desire chastity" (24:33), from Sura al-Nur, Medinan. This is the *mukātaba*, a contract by which a slave pays for freedom.

**Captives.** "So when you meet those who disbelieve [in battle], strike [their] necks until, when you have inflicted slaughter upon them, then secure their bonds, and either [confer] favor afterwards or ransom [them] until the war lays down its burdens" (47:4), Medinan. The verse names release ("favour") or ransom for prisoners of war; it does not mention enslavement. Classical jurists nonetheless allowed the ruler to enslave captives as well (Wikipedia, "Islamic views on slavery", citing classical law).

**Sex with female slaves.** The Quran praises believers "who guard their private parts / Except from their wives or those their right hands possess, for indeed, they will not be blamed - / But whoever seeks beyond that, then those are the transgressors" (23:5–7), Meccan. It forbids "married women except those your right hands possess" (4:24), Medinan — classical scholars read this as allowing a captive woman whose husband remained on the other side. To the Prophet (pbuh) it says: "We have made lawful to you your wives… and those your right hand possesses from what Allah has returned to you [of captives]" (33:50). And: "marry those that please you of [other] women… But if you fear that you will not be just, then [marry only] one or those your right hand possesses" (4:3). These verses permit sexual relations with women one owns. That is what they say, and the page should not soften it.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught and did
**"Your slaves are your brothers."** Al-Maʿrur reports that he saw Abu Dharr (ra) wearing a cloak like his slave's. Abu Dharr explained that he had insulted a man's mother, and the Prophet (pbuh) told him: "You still have some characteristics of ignorance. Your slaves are your brothers and Allah has put them under your command. So whoever has a brother under his command should feed him of what he eats and dress him of what he wears. Do not ask them (slaves) to do things beyond their capacity (power) and if you do so, then help them" ({hadith:bukhari:30}). The hadith demands equal food and clothing and limits work; it does not abolish ownership.

**The Prophet's household.** According to the sources summarised by *Wikipedia* ("Islamic views on slavery"; "Maria al-Qibtiyya"), the Prophet (pbuh): freed Zayd ibn Haritha and adopted him as a son; married Safiyya bint Huyayy, a captive of Khaybar; had Mariya al-Qibtiyya, sent to him as a gift in 628 by al-Muqawqis, the Christian governor of Alexandria; and freed Mariya's sister Sirin and married her to the poet Hassan ibn Thabit. Mariya bore him a son, Ibrahim, who died in infancy. Ibn Ishaq, Ibn Saʿd and al-Tabari list Mariya among his wives, while Abu ʿUbayda listed her among his concubines; there is a scholarly debate on which she was. Ibn ʿAbbas (ra) is reported as saying, "Her son has set her free" — in classical law, a slave woman who bore her master's child (*umm walad*) could not be sold and became free at his death. A few historians (John Morrow, 2023, as cited by *Wikipedia*) doubt that Mariya existed; that is a minority view and has not yet been checked. The Shafiʿi jurist al-Mawardi (*al-Hawi al-Kabir*) is cited by Sedick as treating her as a lawful concubine.

#### scholars — How the system worked
**How people became slaves.** In classical law, enslavement was allowed through "capture in war (on the condition that the prisoner is non-Muslim)"; children of a slave mother (not fathered by her owner) were born slaves; slaves could be bought, and received as tribute. Non-Muslims living under Muslim rule (*dhimmīs*) "could not be enslaved", and "a free person could not sell himself or his children into slavery" (Wikipedia, "Islamic views on slavery", citing classical sources and Sikainga). In practice, many slaves were bought from outside the Muslim world, which the law's restrictions did not stop.

**Legal rights.** Owners were bound to provide "sustenance… shelter, food, clothing, and medical attention", and slaves could not inherit property (Wikipedia). Brown's book, as summarised by Azzuhri, treats slavery (*riqq*) as a legal status rather than a single moral category, notes the presumption that every person is free unless proven otherwise, and says a master could not refuse a *mukātaba* contract (pp. 110–111). A child born to a master by his slave was free, and the mother became an *umm walad*.

**Consent in concubinage.** Kecia Ali (2017) concludes that premodern Sunni jurists did not require a master to obtain his slave's consent before sex. She writes that she recalls "no instance in any Maliki, Hanafi, Shafiʿi, or Hanbali text" of the eighth to tenth centuries requiring it, adds that "the mere absence of discussion proves nothing, of course", and argues that the silence is nonetheless telling because the same jurists did discuss consent to marriage. Jurists agreed that an owner could marry off his female slave without her consent; al-Shafiʿi (d. 820) says he may do so "whether she is a virgin or non-virgin" (p. 149). Forced sex within these relationships fell outside the legal category of *zinā*; quoting Hina Azam, Ali writes that "concerns about consent in sex acts were secondary" to the legal status of the act (p. 150). Ali herself states that "meaningful consent is a prerequisite for ethical sexual relationships" (p. 148).

**Scale.** [Moderate; estimates are uncertain.] Rönnbäck summarises current estimates: about 12.5 million people were carried across the Atlantic from the sixteenth to the nineteenth centuries (Transatlantic Slave Trade Database); "around 5 million people" were taken from sub-Saharan Africa through the trans-Saharan, Red Sea and Indian Ocean trades in the same centuries, about two-thirds across the Sahara; and before 1500, numbers "can only be guessed at", with scholars suggesting "a couple of million people in total" for 650–1400. Earlier claims of tens or hundreds of millions "are now generally recognized to have been vastly inflated". The Atlantic trade took mostly men and boys for plantation labour; the Red Sea and Indian Ocean trades took comparatively more young women, as domestic workers or concubines. Enslaved people also came to Muslim lands from Europe, Central Asia and elsewhere; those numbers are not covered here.

#### history — History of abolition
**The Ottoman steps.** The public slave market in Istanbul was closed in 1846–47, but the trade went on in private, reportedly even among the highest officials (Karamursel, *IJMES*, 2025). A ban on the Circassian and Georgian slave trade in 1854 contributed to a rebellion in the Hijaz, which led to the Hijaz being exempted (Miers, cited by *Wikipedia*). The firman of 1857 "formally prohibited the import of African slaves" but "did not prohibit slavery as such", excluded the Hijaz, and "was never enforced in practice". The Kanunname of 1889 formally banned the trade.

**The twentieth century.** Saudi Arabia formally abolished slavery in 1962: a June 1962 royal decree banned the sale and purchase of people, and in November 1962 King Faisal prohibited owning slaves (Miers, cited by *Wikipedia*). Mauritania abolished slavery in 1981 but made it a crime only in 2007; at that time Anti-Slavery International reported that "Thousands of people in Mauritania are enslaved as a result of being born into an established slave class."

**Did Islam produce an abolitionist movement?** Kecia Ali (cited by *Wikipedia*) writes that "there has not been a strong internally developed critique of past or present slaveholding practices". Brown's book contrasts the Euro-American tradition, where abolitionism arose, with others, including Islam, which "did not consensually abolish slavery" (Azzuhri). But there were Muslim abolitionist arguments: Tunisian reformers argued for abolition "on the basis of Islamic law", and Ameer Ali argued that "the Qur'an disapproved of slavery, but Muhammad could not abolish the institution overnight" (Wikipedia). In the late Ottoman Empire, the writer Ahmed Midhat "saw slavery as an incorrigible wrong that was best abolished altogether", and Sezai's novel *Sergüzeşt* attacked the trade (Karamursel, *IJMES*, 2025). These were writers, not jurists, and they did not end slavery.

#### reasoning — The argument for prohibition today
**Step 1: the Sharia's aim of freedom.** Ibn ʿAshur names freedom (*ḥurriyya*), in its first meaning "the opposite of slavery", as an aim of the Sharia. He argues that the Sharia was "prevented from abolishing the factors of slavery all at once", so "it combated slavery's causes by reducing its numbers" and "abolished many factors leading to slavery" (*Treatise*, "Universal Principles"). On this reading, the direction of the law points to abolition.

**Step 2: consensus.** The 2014 Open Letter to al-Baghdadi, signed by Muslim scholars from many countries, states: "The re-introduction of slavery is forbidden in Islam. It was abolished by universal consensus", referring to "a century of Muslim consensus on the prohibition of slavery". The letter's argument is that a scholarly consensus (*ijmāʿ*) binds later generations. *Objection:* the classical consensus was that slavery was permitted, and whether a modern consensus of about a century can count as binding *ijmāʿ* in the classical sense is exactly what is disputed.

**Step 3: treaties.** The same letter notes that "all the Muslim countries in the world are signatories of anti-slavery conventions". Classical law requires Muslims to keep their agreements.

**Step 4: the permission was never a command.** Owning slaves was permitted, not required; the ruler could always choose release or ransom for captives (47:4). A permitted act can be restricted for the public good.

**Brown's view.** As Aammari summarises it, Brown holds that "God wishes freedom" (p. 264), so abolition is the best way to realise that wish, and that modern norms of consent make concubinage a "dead letter" in Islamic law and ethics. The Iranian scholar Mohsen Kadivar argues that slavery is now "forbidden and illegitimate", because "the reasonable people consider it to be detested and oppressive". He treats its earlier permission as a temporary ruling "whose era has passed", and says explicitly that this is not "technical abrogation" ("The Issue of Slavery in Contemporary Islam", 2018).

**Could ISIS bring it back legitimately?** On the Open Letter's argument, no: it holds that a later group cannot overturn the modern consensus or the treaties Muslim states have signed, and cannot claim a permission that the community, through its scholars and states, has closed. *Limit:* ISIS rejected exactly these premises — the authority of modern consensus and of treaties — and quoted the classical texts. The strongest defence against a revival is that Muslims say clearly why the classical permission no longer holds, not that they pretend it never existed.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"If God allowed slavery and sex with captives, was it ever morally right?"** This is the hardest question. Three positions are held by Muslims. Some, like Brown, argue that slavery was not always "a gross, intrinsic moral evil at all points in history" (Aammari, quoting p. 269), and that its moral weight depended on what it meant in a given society. Others, like Kecia Ali, hold that sex without meaningful consent is wrong, and that the tradition must face this. A third view, Ibn ʿAshur's, is that the law accepted an institution it could not end at once while moving toward freedom. *Limit:* none of these answers fully settles the moral question, and readers should know that serious Muslim scholars disagree. The concubinage rules, in particular, have no fully satisfying answer.

**"Gradual abolition is a myth: it stalled for twelve centuries."** *Reply:* the critic is largely right that the movement did not come from within classical law. Critics such as Gordon (cited by *Wikipedia*) argue that the Prophet's example gave slavery Islamic legitimacy, and Clarence-Smith criticises Mawdudi's "dogged refusal" to give up on slavery. *Limit:* the gradualist reading is a modern interpretation of the law's direction, not a description of what happened.

**"Islamic slavery was gentle."** *Reply:* there were real protections — *mukātaba*, the *umm walad*, equal food and clothing in the hadith — and some slaves rose to great power. *Limit:* many enslaved people died in raids and on the way; women had no right to refuse sex; and the law was often ignored. "Gentle" is not an honest summary.

#### views — The range of views
- **Classical law** — slavery and concubinage permitted, with rights and paths to freedom; consent to sex not required (Kecia Ali, 2017).
- **Ibn ʿAshur** — the Sharia aims at freedom and reduced slavery's causes.
- **Brown (2019)** — abolition not indigenous to Islam, but consistent with God's wish for freedom; concubinage a "dead letter" today.
- **Kecia Ali** — the tradition must face the absence of consent.
- **The Open Letter (2014)** — reintroducing slavery is forbidden by consensus.
- **Kadivar** — slavery is now "forbidden and illegitimate"; its earlier permission was temporary, "whose era has passed", not abrogated (2018).
- **Mawdudi** (as criticised by Clarence-Smith) — refused to give up slavery in principle.

### Questions readers ask next
**Did the Prophet (pbuh) own slaves or concubines?** Yes, according to the sources. He freed several, including Zayd and Sirin; he had Mariya, whose status as wife or concubine is debated.

**How did people become slaves?** Through capture in war (non-Muslims only), birth to a slave mother, or purchase from outside. Free Muslims and *dhimmīs* could not be enslaved.

**What does *maqāṣid* mean?** The aims of the Sharia — the goods the law exists to protect. Ibn ʿAshur counts freedom among them.

**How does it compare with the Atlantic trade?** Current estimates: about 12.5 million across the Atlantic (16th–19th centuries) and about 5 million out of Africa through the Muslim world's trade routes in the same centuries, plus earlier and non-African enslavement. The patterns differed: plantation labour in one, more domestic and military slavery in the other.

**Was slavery in Muslim lands always racial?** The law did not make it racial, but the trades out of Africa created strong links between blackness and slavery in many Muslim societies — a legacy that descendants still face.

**What do Muslims think now?** Muslim scholars and states overwhelmingly hold slavery to be forbidden (Open Letter, 2014).

### Honest limits
"Islam abolished slavery" is false. "Islamic slavery was gentle" is not honest. The classical permission of sex without consent has no fully satisfying answer, and Muslim scholars disagree on how to judge it. The modern prohibition is real and strongly grounded, but it is a modern development. Brown's book was read only through two reviews; numbers for the trades remain uncertain.

## Q22 — The Prophet's wars and Banu Qurayza

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 22:39–40, 2:190, 33:26, 21:107, 5:32.
- Saheeh International via legacy.quran.com — 9:5, 9:6, 9:7, 9:29, 47:4.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 4121 (sunnah.com), 3015 (sunnah.com); Sunan Abi Dawud 4404 (sunnah.com, graded by al-Albani).
- Michael Lecker, "Qurayẓa, Banū", *Encyclopaedia Judaica*, 2nd ed., via Encyclopedia.com.
- Uri Rubin, "The 'Constitution of Medina': Some Notes", pp. 6–22, https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/Library/Rubin,%20U%20-%20The%20‘Constitution%20of%20Medina’.pdf.
- Sadik Kirazli, "Re-Examining the Story of Banū Qurayẓah Jews in Medina with a Reference to the Account of Ibn Isḥāq", *Australian Journal of Islamic Studies* 4:1 (2019), pp. 1–17 — abstract.
- *Wikipedia*: "Siege of Banu Qurayza"; "Military career of Muhammad"; "Ibn Ishaq"; "Sword Verse"; "Jizya" — used for figures and for the scholars they cite (Watt, Stillman, Peterson, Abdel Haleem, Crone, Kister, Rida), which were not read directly.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English), on 9:5 and 9:29, surahquran.com.
- Open Letter to al-Baghdadi (2014).

### The question, at its strongest
"The Prophet is called 'a mercy to the worlds' (21:107), yet he led many military campaigns. After the Battle of the Trench in 627, the Jewish tribe of Qurayza surrendered, and according to the sources all their adult men — hundreds of them — were beheaded, and their women and children enslaved. The Quran itself mentions it: 'a party you killed, and you took captive a party' (33:26). And the Quran commands: 'kill the polytheists wherever you find them' (9:5) and 'fight those who do not believe… until they give the jizyah… while they are humbled' (9:29). How is this a model of mercy?"

### Why people ask it
It is cited as decisive by critics of Islam, and Muslims who care about Jewish–Muslim relations feel its weight. Jewish readers ask about it as part of their own history.

### Short answer
The sources report that Qurayza, during a siege that threatened Medina with destruction, entered negotiations with the attacking army; that after the siege they surrendered and accepted the judgment of Saʿd ibn Muʿadh, leader of their former allies; and that he sentenced the fighting men to death and the women and children to captivity, a verdict the Prophet (pbuh) approved ({hadith:bukhari:4121}). The numbers in the later historical sources — 600 to 900 in Ibn Ishaq and al-Tabari, 700 to 800 in Ibn Kathir — are uncertain, and some scholars think they are inflated. M. J. Kister (1986) defended the core account as historical. It was a collective punishment that modern humanitarian law forbids. Muslims explain it as a wartime judgment for treason, not a rule against Jews; the Quran's general rules on war are defensive and forbid killing women and children. Verses 9:5 and 9:29 have specific settings, and their classical readings were sometimes broader than modern ones. No reading removes the moral weight of Qurayza, and nothing in it supports hatred of Jews.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If you are Jewish and reading this,** this is part of your people's history, told here as the sources tell it, with its weight acknowledged. It gives no one any right to hostility toward Jews today. The Quran itself defends synagogues by name among the places of worship God protects (22:40).

**If you are Muslim and feel uneasy,** love for the Prophet (pbuh) and moral unease can live together. Trying to make the account painless by changing the facts would cost more trust than it gains.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The permission to fight was defensive.** "Permission [to fight] has been given to those who are being fought, because they were wronged" (22:39); they were "evicted from their homes without right - only because they say, 'Our Lord is Allah.' And were it not that Allah checks the people, some by means of others, there would have been demolished monasteries, churches, synagogues, and mosques" (22:40). These are the first verses permitting fighting, revealed around the emigration to Medina. "Fight in the way of Allah those who fight you but do not transgress" (2:190), Medinan.

**The Qurayza verse.** "And He brought down those who supported them among the People of the Scripture from their fortresses and cast terror into their hearts [so that] a party you killed, and you took captive a party" (33:26), in Sura al-Ahzab, Medinan, after the Battle of the Trench. "Those who supported them" means those who supported the besieging coalition. The verse confirms the outcome but gives no numbers and no details.

**9:5 in its setting.** "And when the sacred months have passed, then kill the polytheists wherever you find them and capture them and besiege them and sit in wait for them at every place of ambush. But if they should repent, establish prayer, and give zakah, let them [go] on their way" (9:5). The next verses say: "And if any one of the polytheists seeks your protection, then grant him protection so that he may hear the words of Allah. Then deliver him to his place of safety" (9:6), and "So as long as they are upright toward you, be upright toward them" (9:7). The passage, in Sura al-Tawba (one of the last Medinan suras), concerns Arab polytheists who had broken treaties; 9:4 and 9:7 exempt those who kept them.

**9:29 in its setting.** "Fight those who do not believe in Allah or in the Last Day and who do not consider unlawful what Allah and His Messenger have made unlawful and who do not adopt the religion of truth from those who were given the Scripture - [fight] until they give the jizyah willingly while they are humbled" (9:29). Ibn Kathir connects it with the campaign of Tabuk, when the Prophet (pbuh) marched "towards Ash-Sham to fight the Romans". Al-Shafiʿi, as quoted by al-Qaradawi, explained "while they are humbled" as meaning "they should submit to the Islamic judgment regarding them" (*Fiqh al-Jihad* 2/797).

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Women and children are not to be killed.** Ibn ʿUmar (ra) reports that in one campaign "a woman was found killed", and the Prophet (pbuh) "forbade the killing of women and children" ({hadith:bukhari:3015}).

**Saʿd's verdict.** Abu Saʿid al-Khudri (ra) reports: "The people of (Banu) Quraiza agreed to accept the verdict of Saʿd bin Muʿadh." The Prophet (pbuh) sent for him and told the Ansar, "Get up for your chief or for the best among you." Saʿd said: "Kill their (men) warriors and take their offspring as captives", and the Prophet (pbuh) said: "You have judged according to Allah's Judgment," or "according to the King's judgment" ({hadith:bukhari:4121}).

**How "adults" were identified.** ʿAtiyya al-Qurazi said: "I was among the captives of Banu Qurayzah", and "those who had begun to grow hair (pubes) were killed"; he was spared because he had not ({hadith:abudawud:4404}; al-Albani: *ṣaḥīḥ*). So yes, this report exists and is graded authentic. It shows that puberty was used as the line between "fighting man" and "child".

#### history — History
**The setting.** Medina had three main Jewish tribes: Qaynuqaʿ, al-Nadir and Qurayza. Qaynuqaʿ were besieged and expelled in 624 after a dispute that escalated into killings; al-Nadir were besieged and expelled in 625 after the Prophet (pbuh) was told they were plotting to kill him; both left (Qaynuqaʿ toward Syria, al-Nadir to Khaybar) (Wikipedia, "Military career of Muhammad"). Leaders of al-Nadir then helped assemble the coalition that besieged Medina in 626–627 — about 10,000 men — at the Battle of the Trench.

**What treaty?** The so-called Constitution of Medina, an early agreement among Medina's groups, does not name Qaynuqaʿ, al-Nadir or Qurayza: "the three great Jewish tribes of Medina are not included in the document" (Rubin, p. 6). Lecker writes that Qurayza had a separate "non-belligerency agreement with Muhammad" (*Encyclopaedia Judaica*). The historians Watt and Stillman doubted that a specific treaty existed; the historian al-Waqidi (d. 823) reports one that Qurayza tore up (Wikipedia, "Siege").

**What Qurayza did.** Lecker writes that Qurayza "probably remained neutral": they lent the Muslims tools for digging the trench but also sold provisions to the besiegers. Ibn Kathir reports that Huyayy ibn Akhtab, leader of al-Nadir, persuaded Qurayza's leader Kaʿb ibn Asad to break their covenant, promising to join them in their fortress if the coalition left. So the "breach" was, at the least, negotiating with an army besieging the city they were bound not to fight against; how far it went is disputed.

**The siege and the verdict.** After the coalition withdrew, the Muslims besieged Qurayza for twenty-five days (Ibn Kathir on 33:26). They surrendered unconditionally, and their fate was "reportedly decided not by Muhammad" but by Saʿd ibn Muʿadh, the gravely wounded leader of the Aws, Qurayza's former allies; some of the Aws opposed his decision (Lecker). One woman was also executed. ʿAʾisha (ra), who saw her before her death, says only that she was killed for "a new act" she had committed ({hadith:abudawud:2671}; al-Albani: *ḥasan*). Ibn Hisham (d. 833) adds that she was the one who threw a millstone on Khallad ibn Suwayd and killed him; the commentator al-Khattabi, as cited in *ʿAwn al-Maʿbūd*, says instead that she had insulted the Prophet (pbuh).

**How late are the sources?** Ibn Ishaq, the main source for the details, died in Baghdad in 767 — about 140 years after the event. His biography of the Prophet (pbuh) survives mostly through Ibn Hisham (d. 833) and extracts in al-Tabari (Wikipedia, "Ibn Ishaq"). His contemporary Malik ibn Anas (d. 795) rejected his stories about the Jews of Medina because, he said, "they were taken solely based on accounts by sons of Jewish converts" (as reported by *Wikipedia*, citing Arafat).

**The numbers.** The hadith collections give no number. Ibn Ishaq, in Ibn Hisham's edition, says: "They were six hundred or seven hundred; the one who gives the highest number says between eight hundred and nine hundred" — with no chain of transmitters for the figure. Ibn Kathir, on 33:26, gives "between seven hundred and eight hundred". The hadith collections give no number. Kister estimated the women and children at about 1,000. Kirazli (2019) argues that "the number of Jews punished in this incident were significantly less than what is reported by Ibn Isḥāq".

**Arafat and Kister.** W. N. M. J. Kister (*Jerusalem Studies in Arabic and Islam* 8, 1986) re-examined the traditions and concluded that the core account — the execution of the adult men — is historical. Whether most historians follow Kister has not yet been checked; the numbers remain uncertain.

**The other campaigns.** The sources count 27 campaigns led by the Prophet (pbuh) (al-Waqidi and Ibn Saʿd; Ibn Ishaq counts 19) and 35–48 smaller expeditions (Wikipedia). They end with the conquest of Mecca (630), which was "largely bloodless" with a general amnesty, and at Khaybar (628) the Jews were allowed to stay in return for half their produce.

The early sources disagree on the number executed: al-Tabari reports "600 or 700", with the largest estimate "between 800 and 900"; Ibn Kathir, on 33:26, "between seven hundred and eight hundred"; the hadith collections give no number (as summarised in *Wikipedia*, "Siege of Banu Qurayza"; the primary texts have not yet been consulted).

#### scholars — How scholars have read 9:5 and 9:29
**Classical readings were often broad.** Ibn Kathir writes that 9:5 "was called the Ayah of the Sword", and cites al-Dahhak ibn Muzahim: it "abrogated every agreement of peace between the Prophet and any idolator, every treaty, and every term". He says these verses "allowed fighting people unless, and until, they embrace Islam". On 9:29, he reads "while they are humbled" as "disgraced, humiliated and belittled", and cites conditions attributed to ʿUmar that restricted Christians' public worship.

**Other classical voices.** Al-Hasan al-Basri held 9:5 to be unabrogated but specific; al-Shafiʿi reports scholars explaining "while they are humbled" as meaning that "Islamic rulings are enforced on them"; Ibn al-Qayyim read it as requiring all subjects to obey the law (Wikipedia, "Sword Verse"; "Jizya"). "*An yadin*" ("willingly", literally "from a hand") was read by al-Tabari as paying directly, by Rashid Rida as according to means (Wikipedia, "Jizya").

**Modern readings.** Muhammad Abdel Haleem says the targeted polytheists "continuously broke their agreements and aided others against the Muslims", that the passage exempts those who kept their treaties (9:4, 9:7), and that 9:6 "orders that those enemies seeking safe-conduct should be protected"; he rejects the humiliation reading of 9:29. Patricia Crone, a non-Muslim historian, says 9:5 "is directed against a particular group accused of oath-breaking and aggression" (Wikipedia, "Sword Verse"). The Ottoman Empire abolished the jizya with the reform decree of 1856 (*Hatt-ı Hümâyun*); a tax for exemption from military service took its place (Thompson, *Encyclopedia of the Modern Middle East*). The 2014 Open Letter to al-Baghdadi states: "Jihad in Islam is defensive war."

#### reasoning — How to think about it morally
**What the defence says.** Qurayza were judged for treason during a siege that threatened the city's survival, by an arbiter they themselves accepted, under the war norms of their time; the Prophet (pbuh) forbade killing women and children (Bukhari 3015); the verdict was a judgment on one group's conduct, not a rule about Jews; and the Quran's rules on war are defensive (22:39–40; 2:190).

**What the critic says.** Collective execution of all adult males, decided by physical signs of puberty, violates basic modern principles of individual guilt. "The norms of the time" explain; they do not justify. And if the Prophet (pbuh) is a model beyond his time, his approval of the verdict needs more than context.

**How the tradition can answer the "model" question.** As on the page about Aisha's age, Muslim jurists distinguished the Prophet's acts as messenger from his acts as judge and head of state (al-Qarafi; Ibn ʿAshur). Saʿd's verdict was a judicial and political act in a specific war, not a general law; the Quran's general law of war is restraint and defence. *Limit:* the Prophet (pbuh) called the verdict "Allah's Judgment" ({hadith:bukhari:4121}), which makes it harder to treat as merely political. The moral weight remains.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"The numbers prove a massacre."** *Reply:* the numbers come from sources written 140 years later and vary widely; Arafat and Kirazli argue they are inflated. *Limit:* even the lowest numbers describe a mass execution; the debate is about scale, not about whether it was severe.

**"9:5 commands killing all non-Muslims."** *Reply:* the passage names treaty-breaking polytheists, exempts those who kept treaties (9:4, 9:7), and orders protection for those who seek it (9:6). *Limit:* classical scholars such as al-Dahhak (via Ibn Kathir) read it far more broadly, and that reading had real effects; the modern contextual reading is strong but not the only one in the tradition.

**"9:29 commands humiliating Christians and Jews."** *Reply:* scholars disagreed on what "humbled" means — from humiliation (Ibn Kathir) to submission to the law (al-Shafiʿi's report; Ibn al-Qayyim; Abdel Haleem) — and the jizya was abolished in 1856. *Limit:* the humiliation reading was common in classical tafsir and law.

#### views — The range of views
- **Traditional account** — treason in wartime, arbitration, verdict approved by the Prophet (pbuh).
- **Arafat (1976)** — the story as told by Ibn Ishaq is not credible.
- **Kister (1986)** — the core account is historical.
- **Kirazli (2019)** — far fewer were executed than Ibn Ishaq says.
- **On 9:5 and 9:29** — broad classical readings (al-Dahhak; Ibn Kathir) and contextual readings (al-Hasan al-Basri; Abdel Haleem; Crone; al-Qaradawi).

### Questions readers ask next
**Who were Saʿd ibn Muʿadh and Ibn Ishaq?** Saʿd was leader of the Aws, a Medinan Arab tribe allied to Qurayza before Islam; he died of wounds from the Trench soon after. Ibn Ishaq (d. 767) wrote the earliest surviving biography of the Prophet (pbuh), about 140 years after the events.

**Were boys judged adult by checking for pubic hair?** Yes, according to ʿAtiyya al-Qurazi's report in Abu Dawud 4404, graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by al-Albani.

**What happened to the other Jewish tribes?** Qaynuqaʿ (624) and al-Nadir (625) were besieged and expelled; Khaybar's Jews (628) stayed on terms.

**Was there a treaty?** Not the "Constitution of Medina", which does not name Qurayza (Rubin). Lecker speaks of a non-belligerency agreement; some historians doubt any specific treaty.

**How is the Prophet (pbuh) a moral model on this point?** In his general rules of war — defence, restraint, protection of women, children and those who seek safety — rather than in one wartime verdict whose moral weight Muslims should acknowledge.

**Does this justify hostility to Jews?** No. The Quran protects synagogues (22:40), and a judgment about one group in one war is not a judgment about a people.

### Honest limits
No answer removes the moral weight of Qurayza. The core event is probably historical; the numbers are uncertain. Classical readings of 9:5 and 9:29 were sometimes broader than modern contextual ones. Arafat's and Kister's articles have not yet been consulted directly, and the reasons for their conclusions are given second-hand.

### The question, at its strongest
"I did not choose to be attracted to people of my own sex; most gay people say the same, and the science agrees that orientation is not a simple choice. Islam tells me I may never marry the person I love, never have a partner, and must live alone for life — a burden that straight Muslims never carry. In some countries the law punishes people like me with prison or death, and in many families, being found out means being thrown out. Some young gay Muslims take their own lives. How can a just and merciful God create someone this way and then forbid them love?"

### Why people ask it
Some ask because it is their own life. Others ask for a friend, a child or a sibling, or because modern ethics treats this as a matter of equality and they cannot see how Islam's position fits.

### Short answer
Mainstream Islamic law, in all the Sunni and Shiʿi schools, prohibits same-sex sexual acts, and the Quran's story of Lot is read that way by classical commentators. But Islamic law judges acts, not feelings: attraction one did not choose is not a sin, and a Muslim who feels it and does not act on it "carries no blame" (Yaqeen). The legal penalties the schools describe were tied to strict proof — four witnesses or a confession — and to a functioning legal system, not to private discovery or vigilante action. A small revisionist minority, led by Scott Kugle, argues the Quran does not forbid loving same-sex relationships; their critics argue that this misreads the texts. Science shows that same-sex behaviour is partly genetic, influenced by many genes, and not predictable for any individual, and that attempts to "change" orientation are linked to harm. The burden of lifelong celibacy is real, and this page does not pretend it is small. Every person has God-given dignity, and cruelty, violence and family rejection are wrong.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If you are gay, bisexual or same-sex attracted and Muslim, you are not alone, and you are not less of a Muslim for what you feel.** The tradition's own principle is that "a person is not held accountable for feelings they did not choose" (Yaqeen). God "does not charge a soul except [with that within] its capacity" (2:286).

**If you are in danger — from yourself, or from others — get help now.** Call your local emergency number. If you are thinking about suicide, call a crisis line in your country (findahelpline.com lists them). Naseeha, a Muslim helpline based in Canada, answers 24 hours a day on the North American toll-free number 1-866-627-3342 and by live chat, with counsellors in English, French, Arabic, Urdu, Hindi, Punjabi, Bengali and Gujarati; it is non-judgemental and confidential (except where there is a serious risk of harm), and you can stay anonymous.

**Practical steps.** You can pray, fast, go to the mosque and belong to your community; none of that depends on what you feel. Find one trusted, discreet person — a counsellor, an imam you trust, a friend. Avoid "conversion" programmes (see science, below). If your family's reaction might put you at risk, think about your safety before telling them. Muslims who hold the mainstream view and live celibate, and Muslims who hold the revisionist view, both exist; you will meet both, and you deserve to be treated with respect by both.

**If you are a parent or friend,** your first duty is to keep the person safe and loved. Rejection and shaming do not make anyone more faithful; they put lives at risk.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The story of Lot.** "And [We had sent] Lot when he said to his people, 'Do you commit such immorality as no one has preceded you with from among the worlds? Indeed, you approach men with desire, instead of women. Rather, you are a transgressing people.' But the answer of his people was only that they said, 'Evict them from your city! Indeed, they are men who keep themselves pure.' So We saved him and his family, except for his wife… And We rained upon them a rain [of stones]" (7:80–84), from Sura al-Aʿraf, Meccan.

**Other tellings.** "Do you approach males among the worlds / And leave what your Lord has created for you as mates? But you are a people transgressing" (26:165–166), Meccan. "Do you commit immorality while you are seeing? Do you indeed approach men with desire instead of women? Rather, you are a people behaving ignorantly" (27:54–55), Meccan. Other passages (11:77–83; 15:61–77; 29:28–35) tell of the people's attempt on Lot's guests; they were not quoted.

**How the classical commentators read them.** Ibn Kathir says the people of Lot had relations with males instead of females, and that "this evil practice was not known among the Children of Adam before"; he cites ʿAmr ibn Dinar: "Never before the people of Lut did a male have sex with another male." Al-Tabari, as Vaid shows, integrates 7:80–81 with "a simple and straightforward explanation" that the iniquity was "coming with desire unto men instead of women" (Vaid, pp. 61–62).

**4:15–16.** "Those who commit unlawful sexual intercourse of your women… confine the guilty women to houses… And the two who commit it among you, dishonor them both. But if they repent and correct themselves, leave them alone" (4:15–16), Medinan. Ibn Kathir reports Mujahid saying 4:16 "was revealed about the case of two men who do it", and that the ruling was later abrogated by the penalties for unlawful sex. Others read "the two" as a man and a woman.

#### sunnah — What the hadith reports say
**The key reports.** Ibn ʿAbbas (ra) reports the Prophet (pbuh) as saying: "If you find anyone doing as Lot's people did, kill the one who does it, and the one to whom it is done" ({hadith:abudawud:4462}; al-Albani: *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ*). Another report from Ibn ʿAbbas curses those who do "the act of the people of Lot"; Brown calls this the most reliable condemnation (p. 10); it was graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by al-Hakim, Ibn Hibban, al-Diyaʾ al-Maqdisi and al-Albani (p. 13).

**How strong are they?** The killing report was graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by Ibn al-Jarud, al-Tabari, al-Hakim and al-Suyuti (p. 9). Critics focus on its narrator ʿAmr ibn Abi ʿAmr: Ibn Maʿin and al-Nasaʾi said his hadiths were "not strong", while Ahmad ibn Hanbal and Abu Hatim said "there is nothing wrong with him" (p. 15). Ibn Hajar called the report weak in *Fath al-Bari* but noted disagreement in *Bulugh al-Maram* (p. 17); al-Tirmidhi noted conflicting evidence on the punishment (p. 16). A stoning report from ʿAli was judged weak by Ibn Hajar (p. 17). So the prohibition is firmly established; the specific penalty was disputed among hadith scholars themselves.

#### fiqh — What the four schools hold
**What *ḥadd* means.** A *ḥadd* (plural *ḥudūd*) is a fixed penalty set by God for a specific crime; *taʿzīr* is a discretionary penalty left to a judge.

**The schools' positions.** According to Brown (2017, pp. 4–5) and Sedick (2024):
- **Maliki** — the main position is death by stoning for both partners.
- **Shafiʿi** — al-Shafiʿi's early view was death for both. For the later school, the sources differ: Brown (p. 4) gives the dominant view as "the active partner is executed by the sword while the passive partner is punished with 100 lashes and exiled for one year"; Sedick, citing al-Nawawi's *Minhaj*, gives the *zinā* (unlawful sex) rule — death if married, a hundred lashes and a year's exile if not.
- **Hanbali** — Ahmad's early view was death for both; the later school treats it like *zinā*.
- **Hanafi** — Abu Hanifa held there is no fixed *ḥadd*; the penalty is *taʿzīr*, with execution possible for repeat offenders as a matter of governance (Brown). His students Abu Yusuf and Muhammad al-Shaybani treated it like *zinā* (Sedick).
- **Ibn Hazm** (Zahiri) held the act "categorically forbidden" but rejected a fixed *ḥadd*, requiring *taʿzīr* (Vaid, pp. 73, 76).

**Proof and process.** All the schools required four male witnesses to the act itself, or a clear, voluntary confession — standards Sedick calls "nigh impossible", meant to protect people from accusation. He adds that "no one is allowed to take the law into their own hands", and that these laws presuppose a full legal system with courts. In this framework, what could be punished was a proven act, not an inclination (Sedick; El-Rouayheb, below).

**Desire and act.** El-Rouayheb argues that the Arab-Islamic world of 1500–1800 "lacked the concept of homosexuality": it judged acts and roles, and its sources include a large body of Arabic love poetry. The modern idea of a fixed "orientation" did not exist; the law judged acts.

**What states do today.** According to ILGA World's 2024 report, 60 UN member states criminalise consensual same-sex acts by law and two more do so in practice; Uganda's 2023 law includes a possible death penalty, and reports surfaced of executions in Afghanistan and Yemen (*PinkNews*). Many of these countries are Muslim-majority, some are not. Whether such laws meet classical Islamic standards of proof is a separate question from whether they exist.

#### science — What science says
**Genes play a part, but no single gene decides.** [Established.] In a genome study of 477,522 people, Ganna and colleagues (*Science*, 2019) found "five loci significantly associated with same-sex sexual behavior". "In aggregate, all tested genetic variants accounted for 8 to 25% of variation", and the variants "do not allow meaningful prediction of an individual's sexual behavior". Twin and family studies show the behaviour is "partly genetically influenced". So sexuality is shaped by many genes, environment and experience; it is not a simple choice, and it is not a simple genetic switch.

**"Conversion" efforts are linked to harm.** [Moderate.] In a nationally representative US sample of 1,518 sexual-minority adults, Blosnich and colleagues (2020) found that those exposed to sexual orientation change efforts — 80.8% of them from a religious leader — had higher odds of suicidal ideation (adjusted odds ratio 1.92, 95% CI 1.01–3.64), planning (1.75, CI 1.01–3.06) and suicide attempts with no or minor injury (1.88, CI 1.01–3.50) than those not exposed, after controlling for adverse childhood experiences; for attempts with moderate or severe injury the difference (1.67, CI 0.76–3.64) was not statistically significant. Only 6.9% of the sample (108 people) were exposed, and all the intervals are close to 1. The authors note that the measure was new and that they could not time the change efforts relative to childhood adversity, so causation is not proven. A published reanalysis (Sullins, 2022) disputes the findings; it has not yet been consulted here.

#### reasoning — The hardest question
**"Why would God make someone gay and then forbid them love?"** The mainstream Islamic answer has several parts, and it is fair to say none is complete.

First, Islam does not teach that God created a person *in order to* sin. It teaches that every person has desires, some of which they may not act on, and that what is judged is what they choose to do (2:286). A same-sex-attracted Muslim who does not act on it "carries no blame", and "the effort to stay within God's limits can itself earn reward" (Yaqeen).

Second, the burden of not being able to marry is not unique to gay Muslims: many Muslims never find a spouse, or are widowed young, or are unable to marry for other reasons. *Limit:* this comparison is imperfect. For a gay Muslim, the burden is not a circumstance that might change, and it falls on a whole dimension of life; it is right to say this plainly.

Third, Islam does not reduce love to sex. Deep friendship, family, community and the love of God are open to everyone. *Limit:* this is true but can sound like a consolation prize to someone who wants a partner, and should be offered with humility, not as a dismissal.

Fourth, the answer finally rests on trust that God's commands come from knowledge and mercy, even when a person cannot see their wisdom. *Limit:* that is a theological answer, not a philosophical proof, and it will not persuade someone who does not share the premise.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**The revisionist argument (Kugle).** Scott Kugle, in *Homosexuality in Islam* (2010), argues that the Lot verses condemn rape and the violation of hospitality — the people tried to assault Lot's guests — "rather than forbidding same-sex intercourse"; that the hadith condemning such acts have weak chains or reflect later views; and that classical jurists had "no actual consensus on the nature of the act" (Rahemtulla's review). He treats the medieval jurist Ibn Hazm as "our guide" (Vaid, p. 72; page numbers from running heads may be off by one).

**Vaid's reply.** Mobeen Vaid (2017) argues that Kugle's rape reading depends on late storytelling material; that 7:81 itself names the act ("you approach men with desire, instead of women"); that "traditional exegetes do not mention any wives when commenting on Q. 26:165–66" (p. 71); and that "Ibn Hazm never demurred as to whether same-sex sexual behavior was prohibited" — Ibn Hazm called such conduct "horrible and disgusting" in *Ṭawq al-Ḥamāma* and only rejected a fixed *ḥadd* (pp. 73–76). The reviewer Rahemtulla praises Kugle's book as "groundbreaking" while criticising gaps in it.

**Assessment.** The revisionist case is worth reading because it raises real questions about what the texts emphasise. But the classical reading rests on the Quran's own words in 7:81, 26:165–166 and 27:55, which name desire for men instead of women, and on a broad consensus that even Ibn Hazm shared. *Limit:* the revisionists reply that ancient categories of sexual conduct do not map onto modern loving, committed relationships, and that question — whether the texts address what we now call orientation — is not fully settled by pointing to the verses.

**"The penalties are barbaric."** *Reply:* the schools differed widely; the Hanafis set no fixed penalty; all required near-impossible proof; and none allowed private violence. *Limit:* several classical schools did prescribe death, some states still do, and that history should not be hidden.

#### views — The range of views
- **All classical schools** — same-sex acts are prohibited; feelings are not punished.
- **Maliki** — death; **Shafiʿi, Hanbali (later)** — like *zinā*; **Hanafi (Abu Hanifa)** — discretionary; **Ibn Hazm** — discretionary.
- **Contemporary mainstream** (e.g. Yaqeen) — attraction is not sinful; celibacy is the path; support, not shame.
- **Revisionist minority** (Kugle) — the Quran does not forbid loving same-sex relationships; rejected by most scholars (Vaid).

### Questions readers ask next
**Is being gay a sin?** In mainstream Islamic teaching, no: feelings one did not choose are not sins. Acts are what the law addresses.

**What do 26:165–166 and 27:54–55 say?** They describe Lot's people approaching men "with desire instead of women" and leaving "what your Lord has created for you as mates".

**What does *ḥadd* mean, and what did schools other than Abu Hanifa's hold?** A fixed penalty. Malikis held death; later Shafiʿis and Hanbalis treated the act like *zinā*; Abu Hanifa held there was no fixed penalty.

**Is the punishment actually applied?** In a few states, laws exist and are sometimes enforced; in most Muslim-majority countries, the classical standards of proof are not what is applied. Vigilante violence is forbidden in Islamic law.

**Is the revisionist view worth taking seriously?** It is worth understanding. Most scholars think it misreads the texts (Vaid).

**How do gay Muslims live their faith in practice?** In many ways: some live celibate within mainstream teaching, with the support of friends and counsellors; some hold revisionist views; many are simply trying to stay close to God and safe. What helps all of them is a community that keeps their dignity and confidence.

### Honest limits
The mainstream position is clear, and the revisionist view is a minority that most scholars reject. The burden this position places on gay Muslims is heavy, and no answer here makes it light. Several classical schools prescribed death, and some states still do; that must not be hidden. Kugle's book and El-Rouayheb's book were read only through reviews and summaries; Kecia Ali's *Sexual Ethics and Islam* (2016) has not yet been consulted.
