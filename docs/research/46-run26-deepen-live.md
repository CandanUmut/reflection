## M7 — Dignity and diversity

### Sources read
- Saheeh International (Tanzil): 17:70, 49:11–13, 5:27–32, 30:22, 4:1.
- al-Tabari and al-Qurtubi on 17:70 (Arabic), https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/ibn-tabary-qortobi/sura17-aya70.html — in what the honour consists.
- Ibn Kathir on 49:13 (Arabic), https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/katheer/sura49-aya13.html — equal origin; honour by *taqwā*; the hadith he cites.
- Musnad Ahmad 23489 (hadithunlocked.com), with al-Arnaʾut's grade.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 1312 (hadithunlocked.com) — the Prophet (pbuh) standing for a Jewish funeral.
- Recep Şentürk, "Adamiyyah (Humanity) and ʿIsmah (Inviolability): Humanity as the Ground for Universal Human Rights in Islamic Law", in *The Islamic Tradition and the Human Rights Discourse* (Atlantic Council; file posted 2018).
- T. F. Pettigrew and L. R. Tropp, "How does intergroup contact reduce prejudice? Meta-analytic tests of three mediators", *European Journal of Social Psychology* 38 (2008) 922–934 — summarising their 2006 meta-analysis.

### In brief
"And We have certainly honored the children of Adam" (17:70): dignity belongs to every human being, given by God, not earned by belief, rank or origin. Differences of people and tribe are "that you may know one another", and the only rank before God is righteousness (49:13).

### Go deeper

#### meaning — What it means
**Karāma is honour that is given, not earned.** *Karrama* (17:70) means "to honour, to ennoble". al-Qurtubi explains it as giving honour, nobility and excellence — not wealth. The verb's subject is God and its object is "the children of Adam", every human.

**Taʿāruf is mutual knowing.** *Li-taʿārafū* (49:13) is "that you may know one another": a two-way verb, knowing and being known.

**Taqwā is the one measure.** *Atqākum* (49:13), "the most righteous of you" — the most mindful of God. It is a quality of the heart and deeds, not of birth.

**ʿIṣma bi-l-ādamiyya: inviolable by being human.** A legal phrase in Hanafi law: a person's life, property and honour are protected because they are human (Şentürk).

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Honour for all humans (17:70).** "And We have certainly honored the children of Adam and carried them on the land and sea and provided for them of the good things and preferred them over much of what We have created, with [definite] preference" (17:70). Sura al-Isrāʾ is Meccan. The addressee is humanity, named through its common ancestor, not believers only.

**One origin, many peoples (49:13).** "O mankind, indeed We have created you from male and female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you. Indeed, Allah is Knowing and Acquainted" (49:13). Sura al-Ḥujurāt is Medinan; this verse turns from "O you who have believed" (49:11–12) to "O mankind". Just before, believers are forbidden to ridicule another people, "perhaps they may be better than them", or to use "[offensive] nicknames" (49:11). Compare "O mankind, fear your Lord, who created you from one soul" (4:1).

**Colours and languages as signs (30:22).** "And of His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth and the diversity of your languages and your colors. Indeed in that are signs for those of knowledge" (30:22), Meccan. Diversity is placed beside the creation of the heavens and the earth.

**One life as all humanity (5:32).** "Because of that, We decreed upon the Children of Israel that whoever kills a soul unless for a soul or for corruption [done] in the land - it is as if he had slain mankind entirely. And whoever saves one - it is as if he had saved mankind entirely" (5:32), Medinan. The setting is the story of Adam's two sons, the first murder (5:27–31). The verse states a law given to the Children of Israel, with two exceptions (retaliation for a killing; "corruption in the land"), and the Quran presents it as a lesson. Applying it as a universal principle of human dignity is a common modern reading, and should be labelled as one.

**What the commentators said.**
- **On 17:70.** al-Tabari: the honour is God giving humans authority over other creatures and «تسخيرنا سائر الخلق لهم» ("making the rest of creation subject to them"); from Ibn Jurayj, the hand that brings food to the mouth. al-Qurtubi lists more: eating with the hand (Ibn ʿAbbas), «النطق والتمييز» ("speech and discernment", al-Dahhak), upright stature (ʿAtaʾ), beauty of form (Yaman), and concludes: «والصحيح الذي يعول عليه أن التفضيل إنما كان بالعقل» ("the correct view, to be relied on, is that the preference is by intellect").
- **On 49:13.** Ibn Kathir: all humans are equal in their origin from Adam and Eve; «إنما تتفاضلون عند الله بالتقوى لا بالأحساب» ("you are preferred before God only by *taqwā*, not by lineage"); the purpose of peoples and tribes is to know one another.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**No superiority except by piety.** In a sermon reported by a Companion, the Prophet (pbuh) said: "there is no virtue of an Arab over a non-Arab, nor of a non-Arab over an Arab, nor of a red-skinned over a black-skinned, nor of a black-skinned over a red-skinned except through piety" (Musnad Ahmad 23489; chain graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by Shuʿayb al-Arnaʾut).

**God looks at hearts and deeds.** Ibn Kathir, on 49:13, cites from Muslim: «إن الله لا ينظر إلى صوركم وأموالكم، ولكن ينظر إلى قلوبكم وأعمالكم» ("Allah does not look at your forms and your wealth, but He looks at your hearts and your deeds").

**"Is it not a soul?"** Sahl ibn Hunayf and Qays ibn Saʿd reported that a funeral passed the Prophet (pbuh) and he stood up. He was told it was the funeral of a Jew. He said: "Is it not a living being (soul)?" ({hadith:bukhari:1312}). Respect for a human being did not wait on their religion — even in death.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**The intellect as the ground of honour (al-Qurtubi).** Among many proposed reasons for 17:70, al-Qurtubi chose the intellect, adding that the Sharia is like the sun and the intellect like the eye that sees by it. If dignity rests on the capacity to reason and choose, it belongs to every person who has it.

**Inviolability through being human (Hanafi law).** Recep Şentürk explains the maxim "the right to inviolability (Ismah) is due for humanity (adamiyyah)": the inviolability of life, property, religion, mind, family and honour belongs to every human being, "regardless of sex, religion, race, or nationality", and "All Hanafi jurists uphold this perspective", as do some jurists in other schools. The Hanafi texts themselves, and the modern *maqāṣid* scholars Ibn ʿAshur and Jasser Auda, were not read for this page.

#### history — History
**The ideal and the record.** Muslim history includes both the Prophet's (pbuh) words above and real racism and ethnic hierarchy — including slavery (see "Slavery") and prejudice against Black Muslims. No historical study of race in Muslim societies was read for this page, so no examples are given; they should be added from named sources.

#### research — What research shows
**Contact between groups reduces prejudice.** [Established.] Pettigrew and Tropp's 2006 meta-analysis covered 515 studies with about a quarter of a million participants in 38 nations and found an average relationship between contact and lower prejudice of about r = .21; the more rigorous experimental studies gave a stronger effect (as summarised in Pettigrew and Tropp, 2008). Contact under good conditions — equal status, shared goals, cooperation, support from authorities — tended to reduce prejudice more. This fits 49:13's "that you may know one another": knowing people across groups lessens contempt.

#### practice — In daily life
**Know people unlike you.** Make friends across ethnicity, language and class: eat together, work together, visit.

**Check your words.** Jokes and nicknames that demean a group are forbidden: "do not call each other by [offensive] nicknames" (49:11).

**In the mosque, notice who is missing.** Converts, Black Muslims, refugees, the poor: who is welcomed, who sits alone, who is never asked to speak?

**Stand for a stranger's dignity.** The Prophet (pbuh) stood for a passing funeral because it was "a soul" (Bukhari 1312).

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you have faced racism, including from Muslims,** the Prophet's (pbuh) words are on your side: no one is above you by colour or origin. That some Muslims fail this teaching is their failure, not a flaw in your worth.

**If you find it hard to see dignity in someone who has wronged you,** the verse does not say they are good; it says they are human, and God honoured the children of Adam. Dignity is not approval.

### Questions readers ask next
**Does 17:70 apply to non-Muslims?** Yes: it speaks of "the children of Adam", all humans. The classical commentators discussed what the honour consists in (intellect, speech, upright form), not who is excluded.

**What does "that you may know one another" mean?** Ibn Kathir: knowing one another's lineage, with honour before God only through *taqwā*, "not by lineage". A wider reading — getting to know other peoples — is common today.

**Is 5:32 a universal rule against killing?** It states a law given to the Children of Israel, with two exceptions (retaliation and "corruption in the land"), and the Quran presents it as a lesson after the first murder. Reading it as a universal principle is a common modern extension.

**Is the "no Arab over non-Arab" sermon authentic?** The version in Musnad Ahmad 23489 has a chain graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by al-Arnaʾut.

**If Islam teaches equal dignity, why was there racism and slavery in Muslim history?** Because people fail their ideals. The teaching condemns contempt for others; history shows it persisted. The page states the teaching; it does not claim it was always lived.

**Does dignity mean all beliefs are equally true?** No. 49:13 ranks people before God by righteousness, and the Quran calls to faith. But dignity, and the right not to be despised, belong to every person regardless.

**Hardest objection: "Equal dignity, but unequal law — how is that honest?"** Classical law did treat Muslims and non-Muslims, and men and women, differently in some areas (see "Does Islam treat women unequally?" and the slavery page). The Hanafi principle of *ʿiṣma bi-l-ādamiyya* protected life, property and honour for all, but did not erase every legal difference. *Limit:* how far the principle should reshape law today is debated among Muslim scholars; this page does not settle it.

### Honest limits
Muslim history and present societies include racism and slavery; this page states the teaching, not a claim that it was lived. The Hanafi texts, Ibn ʿAshur and Auda were not read; the Hanafi principle is given through Şentürk. No historical study of race in Muslim societies was read. The contact research is correlational for the most part, though experimental studies point the same way.

## M6 — Forgiveness and anger

### Sources read
- Saheeh International (Tanzil): 3:133–135, 42:36–43, 24:22, 2:178, 5:45, 41:34, 16:126, 64:14.
- Ibn Kathir, al-Qurtubi and al-Saʿdi on 3:134 (Arabic), https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/ibn-saadi-katheer-qortobi/sura3-aya134.html.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 6116 (en.tohed.com) — "Do not become angry."
- Sahih al-Bukhari 6114 and 3231 (hadithunlocked.com).
- Sahih al-Bukhari 6679 (en.tohed.com) — Abu Bakr, Mistah and 24:22.
- Sunan Abi Dawud 4777 and 4782 (hadithunlocked.com) — restraining anger; sitting down.
- Sahih Muslim 2588 (hadithunlocked.com) — "no one forgives another except that Allah increases his honor".
- Wade, Hoyt, Kidwell & Worthington, *J Consult Clin Psychol* 82(1):154–170 (2014), as reported by VanderWeele, *AJPH* 108(2) (2018), and Toussaint et al., *Frontiers in Psychology* 11:671 (2020).
- *Child Development* (2022) meta-analysis of forgiveness education, PubMed 35452524.
- Harper et al., *Journal of Clinical Psychology* (2014).
- Brad J. Bushman, "Does venting anger feed or extinguish the flame?", *Personality and Social Psychology Bulletin* 28(6):724–731 (2002).

### In brief
The Quran praises those "who restrain anger and who pardon the people" (3:134), and it keeps the right to fair redress: "the retribution for an evil act is an evil one like it, but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation - his reward is [due] from Allah" (42:40). Forgiveness is a gift the wronged person may give; it is never something to be forced out of them.

### Go deeper

#### meaning — What it means
**Kaẓm al-ghayẓ is holding anger in.** al-Qurtubi: «وكظم الغيظ رده في الجوف» ("restraining *ghayẓ* is turning it back inside"); *ghayẓ* is anger that does not yet show on the limbs, unlike *ghaḍab*, which does.

**ʿAfw is giving up a right.** Pardon is letting go of the right to punish. **Ṣafḥ** is "turning the page", overlooking. **Maghfira** is covering a fault, used above all of God.

**Iḥsān is the highest step.** "And Allah loves the doers of good" (3:134): going beyond pardon to kindness.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Restraining anger and pardoning (3:134).** "Who spend [in the cause of Allah] during ease and hardship and who restrain anger and who pardon the people - and Allah loves the doers of good" (3:134). Medinan, in the passage after Uhud describing the people of Paradise (3:133); the next verse speaks of those who, when they sin, "remember Allah and seek forgiveness" (3:135).
- **The three steps.** Ibn Kathir: those who «لا يعملون غضبهم في الناس، بل يكفون عنهم شرهم» ("do not act on their anger against people, but hold back their harm"); restraint, then pardon. al-Saʿdi: «والعفو أبلغ من الكظم» ("pardon goes further than restraint"); and *iḥsān* is «أحسن وأعلى وأجل» ("the best, highest and most exalted").

**Justice first, pardon better (42:40).** "And the retribution for an evil act is an evil one like it, but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation - his reward is [due] from Allah. Indeed, He does not like wrongdoers" (42:40), Meccan. The surrounding verses praise those who "when they are angry, they forgive" (42:37) and those who "when tyranny strikes them, they defend themselves" (42:39), and end: "whoever is patient and forgives - indeed, that is of the matters [requiring] determination" (42:43). The verse keeps the wronged person's right to equal redress, invites something better, and names the *wrongdoer* as the one God does not like.

**Forgiving a relative who wronged your family (24:22).** "And let not those of virtue among you and wealth swear not to give [aid] to their relatives and the needy and the emigrants for the cause of Allah, and let them pardon and overlook. Would you not like that Allah should forgive you? And Allah is Forgiving and Merciful" (24:22), Medinan. Its occasion is told below.

**Repel evil with good (41:34).** "Repel [evil] by that [deed] which is better; and thereupon the one whom between you and him is enmity [will become] as though he was a devoted friend" (41:34), Meccan.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Abu Bakr and Mistah.** When ʿAʾisha (ra) was falsely accused, one of those who spread the slander was Mistah, a poor relative whom her father Abu Bakr (ra) had been supporting. Abu Bakr swore: "By Allah, I will never give anything (in charity) to Mistah, after what he has said about ʿAisha." Then 24:22 was revealed. Abu Bakr said: "Yes, by Allah, I like that Allah should forgive me", and resumed his support, saying: "By Allah! I will never withhold it from him" ({hadith:bukhari:6679}, from ʿAʾisha). The slander was against his own daughter; the forgiveness was costly, and it came as an invitation — "Would you not like that Allah should forgive you?"

**"Do not become angry."** Abu Hurayra reports that a man said to the Prophet (pbuh), "Advise me!" He said: "Do not become angry and furious." The man repeated his request several times, and each time he said the same ({hadith:bukhari:6116}).

**Sit down, then lie down.** "When one of you becomes angry while standing, he should sit down. If the anger leaves him, well and good; otherwise he should lie down" ({hadith:abudawud:4782}, from Abu Dharr; al-Albani: *ṣaḥīḥ*; al-Daraqutni preferred a *mursal* version).

**The reward of restraint.** "If anyone suppresses anger when he is in a position to give vent to it, Allah… will call him" before all creatures on the Day of Resurrection ({hadith:abudawud:4777}, from Muʿadh ibn Anas; al-Albani: *ḥasan*; al-Tirmidhi: *ḥasan gharīb*; al-Mundhiri noted weakness in Sahl ibn Muʿadh).

**Forgiving raises you.** "No one forgives another except that Allah increases his honor, and no one humbles himself for the sake of Allah except that Allah raises his status" ({hadith:muslim:2588}, from Abu Hurayra).

**True strength.** "The strong is not the one who overcomes the people by his strength", the Prophet (pbuh) said, but the one who controls himself when angry ({hadith:bukhari:6114}).

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Three degrees: restrain, pardon, do good.** Ibn Kathir and al-Saʿdi read 3:134 as rising steps (above). al-Saʿdi defines pardon as giving up the right to punish *and* being generous to the wrongdoer.

**A story the commentators loved.** al-Qurtubi tells of Maymun ibn Mihran, whose servant spilled hot broth on him. As he moved to strike her, she recited "who restrain anger" — he said he had restrained it; "and who pardon the people" — he said he had pardoned her; "and Allah loves the doers of good" — and he freed her for God's sake.

**Forgiveness is not the same as trust or reconciliation.** In 42:40 the Quran pairs pardon "and makes reconciliation" as a further good; it does not require a wronged person to return to a relationship that endangers them. al-Ghazali's book on anger in the *Iḥyāʾ* (Book 25) was not read for this page.

#### fiqh — What the four schools hold
**Redress is a right; pardon is the victim's choice.** "Prescribed for you is legal retribution for those murdered… But whoever overlooks from his brother anything, then there should be a suitable follow-up and payment to him with good conduct. This is an alleviation from your Lord and a mercy" (2:178); "for wounds is legal retribution. But whoever gives [up his right as] charity, it is an expiation for him" (5:45). On this basis the victim of bodily harm, or the family of someone killed, may demand retaliation (*qiṣāṣ*), accept compensation (*diya*), or pardon. That choice belongs to the victim or family. *The four schools' detailed rules were not read for this page*; they should be added from named works.

#### history — History
**Ṭāʾif.** After the people of Ṭāʾif rejected and stoned him, the Prophet (pbuh) refused the offer to have them destroyed and hoped that their descendants would worship God ({hadith:bukhari:3231}).

#### research — What research shows
**Forgiveness interventions work, moderately.** [Established; moderate effect.] A 2014 meta-analysis of 54 studies found that forgiveness interventions raised forgiveness by about half a standard deviation more than other treatments or no treatment, and reduced depression and anxiety while increasing hope (Wade et al., 2014, as reported by VanderWeele, 2018, and Toussaint et al., 2020). The more hours of intervention, the larger the effect.

**For children and teenagers too.** [Established.] In 20 studies of forgiveness education with 1,472 young people, forgiveness rose (g = 0.54) and anger fell (g = 0.29) (*Child Development*, 2022).

**A small trial.** [Moderate.] Harper and colleagues (2014) randomly assigned 41 university students to a six-hour forgiveness workbook or a wait-list; the workbook group showed less unforgiveness (d = −0.56) and more emotional forgiveness (d = 0.50). The sample was small and mostly women.

**Venting does not drain anger.** [Established, one strong experiment.] 600 angered students hit a punching bag while thinking about the person who angered them, or while thinking about fitness, or did nothing. "People in the rumination group felt angrier", and "Rumination increased rather than decreased anger and aggression"; "Doing nothing at all was more effective than venting anger" (Bushman, *PSPB*, 2002). This fits the Prophet's (pbuh) advice to change one's state — sit, lie down — rather than act it out.

#### practice — In daily life
**When anger rises, change your state.** Stop before speaking. Sit down; if it does not pass, lie down ({hadith:abudawud:4782}). Leave the room. Do not "let it out" on a person or object — venting feeds anger (Bushman, 2002).

**Separate the three.** Forgiving (letting go of revenge in your heart), trusting again, and reconciling are different. You can do the first without the other two.

**If you want to forgive but can't yet,** ask God to help you, and remember Abu Bakr's question: "Would you not like that Allah should forgive you?" (24:22).

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you were abused, you do not have to forgive in order to be a good Muslim, and you never have to go back.** The Quran keeps your right to justice (42:40). Forgiveness may come later, or not; it is yours to give. If anyone uses verses on forgiveness to keep you in danger, they are misusing them. Help is available (see the help box on "Does Islam treat women unequally?" and findahelpline.com).

**If your anger frightens you,** take it seriously: an anger-management course or counsellor can help, and seeking help is a form of the self-control the Prophet (pbuh) praised.

### Questions readers ask next
**Do I have to forgive?** No. 42:40 keeps your right to fair redress and invites pardon as a better path; it does not force it.

**Does forgiving mean going back?** No. Forgiveness, trust and reconciliation are different.

**What was the occasion of 24:22?** Abu Bakr's oath to stop supporting Mistah after the slander of ʿAʾisha, and his reversal when the verse came ({hadith:bukhari:6679}).

**What should I do when I'm angry?** Change your state: sit down, and if needed lie down ({hadith:abudawud:4782}); don't speak until it passes ({hadith:bukhari:6114}).

**Isn't anger sometimes right?** The Quran praises *restraining* anger, not the absence of it. Anger at injustice can lead to seeking justice, which 42:40 allows; it praises those who "defend themselves" against tyranny (42:39).

**Doesn't letting it out help?** No. In a large experiment, venting made people angrier and more aggressive; doing nothing worked better (Bushman, 2002).

**Hardest objection: "Teaching victims to forgive protects abusers."** It can, when misused. The Quran's structure answers it: redress is a right (42:40; 2:178; 5:45), pardon is the victim's choice and "his reward is [due] from Allah", and the verse ends by condemning the wrongdoer. *Limit:* communities have used forgiveness verses to silence victims; the page cannot prevent misuse, only state the teaching clearly.

### Honest limits
The four schools' detailed rules on *qiṣāṣ*, *diya* and pardon were not read; al-Ghazali's book on anger was not read. The forgiveness research is mostly from Western samples and university students; effects are moderate. The page states the teaching, not a claim that Muslim communities protect victims well.

## M11 — Humility and avoiding arrogance

### Sources read
- Saheeh International (Tanzil): 31:13–19, 25:63–64, 17:37–38, 49:13, 28:76–83, 57:23, 16:23, 7:12, 40:60.
- al-Tabari on 28:83 (Arabic), https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/tabary/sura28-aya83.html — *ʿuluww* and *fasād*.
- Sahih Muslim 91 (hadithunlocked.com) — the definition of pride.
- Sahih Muslim 2588 (hadithunlocked.com) — humility raises.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 676 (en.tohed.com) and Musnad Ahmad 25710 (hadithunlocked.com) — the Prophet (pbuh) at home.
- Jules Janssens, review of Mohammed Rustom's translation of al-Ghazali's Book 29, *Ilahiyat Studies* 11(2) (2020) 319–323.
- T. Porter et al., "Predictors and consequences of intellectual humility", *Nature Reviews Psychology* 1 (2022) 524–536.
- Ohio State University news (2021) on Kjærvik & Bushman, *Psychological Bulletin* (2021) — narcissism and aggression.
- Owens, Johnson & Mitchell, "Expressed Humility in Organizations: Implications for Performance, Teams, and Leadership", *Organization Science* 24(5):1517–1538 (2013), abstract (RePEc).

### In brief
The Prophet (pbuh) defined arrogance precisely: "Pride is disdaining the truth (out of self-conceit) and contempt for the people" (Muslim 91). Humility is therefore not looking shabby or thinking badly of yourself; it is being open to the truth and never looking down on others — "And do not walk upon the earth exultantly" (17:37).

### Go deeper

#### meaning — What it means
**Kibr is making oneself big.** From the root "to be big": making oneself big over others.

**Tawāḍuʿ is lowering oneself.** From "to put down": honesty about one's place before God and others, not self-hatred.

**ʿUluww fī al-arḍ is seeking to rise over others.** In 28:83, "exaltedness upon the earth": al-Tabari reports Ikrima, «العلو: التجبر» ("tyranny"), and Ibn Jurayj, «تعظُّما وتجبرا» ("self-glorification and tyranny").

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Luqman's advice to his son (31:18–19).** "And do not turn your cheek [in contempt] toward people and do not walk through the earth exultantly. Indeed, Allah does not like everyone self-deluded and boastful. And be moderate in your pace and lower your voice; indeed, the most disagreeable of sounds is the voice of donkeys" (31:18–19). Meccan; part of a father's advice that begins "do not associate [anything] with Allah" (31:13) and includes prayer and patience (31:17). Humility here is everyday bearing: the turned cheek, the swagger, the loud voice.

**The servants of the Most Merciful (25:63).** "And the servants of the Most Merciful are those who walk upon the earth easily, and when the ignorant address them [harshly], they say [words of] peace" (25:63), Meccan; followed by their night prayer (25:64). Humility shows in how one answers rudeness.

**You cannot reach the mountains (17:37).** "And do not walk upon the earth exultantly. Indeed, you will never tear the earth [apart], and you will never reach the mountains in height" (17:37), Meccan, in a list of commands that ends "All that - its evil is ever, in the sight of your Lord, detested" (17:38).

**The home of the Hereafter (28:83).** "That home of the Hereafter We assign to those who do not desire exaltedness upon the earth or corruption. And the [best] outcome is for the righteous" (28:83), Meccan, right after the story of Qarun, who "tyrannized" his people and was told "Do not exult" (28:76), and whom the earth swallowed (28:81). al-Tabari: *ʿuluww* is tyranny and self-glorification (Ikrima; Ibn Jurayj), «البغي» ("transgression", Saʿid ibn Jubayr); *fasād* is «أخذ المال بغير حق» ("taking wealth without right", Muslim al-Batin) or disobedience (Ibn Jurayj). He reports from ʿAli that even a man pleased that his sandal-strap is finer than his companion's comes under it.

**The first arrogance (7:12).** Iblis, asked why he did not prostrate: "I am better than him. You created me from fire and created him from clay" (7:12).

**Arrogance toward God (40:60).** "Indeed, those who disdain My worship will enter Hell [rendered] contemptible" (40:60).

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**The definition.** ʿAbdullah ibn Masʿud (ra) reports: "He who has in his heart the weight of a mustard seed of pride shall not enter Paradise." A man said: "Verily a person loves that his dress should be fine, and his shoes should be fine." He said: "Verily, Allah is Graceful and He loves Grace. Pride is disdaining the truth (out of self-conceit) and contempt for the people" ({hadith:muslim:91}). Caring how you look is not arrogance; refusing the truth and despising people is.

**Humility raises.** "No one humbles himself for the sake of Allah except that Allah raises his status" ({hadith:muslim:2588}).

**At home.** Al-Aswad asked ʿAʾisha (ra) what the Prophet (pbuh) did at home. She said: "He used to keep himself busy serving his family and when it was the time for prayer he would go for it" ({hadith:bukhari:676}; also Musnad Ahmad 25710, al-Arnaʾut: chain *ṣaḥīḥ*).

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Pride of knowledge and lineage (al-Ghazali).** Al-Ghazali devoted Book 29 of the *Iḥyāʾ* to the condemnation of pride and self-admiration. A review of its English translation (Janssens, 2020) notes his treatment of "pride because of lineage and noble descent" and of pride through knowledge. His full list of the sources of pride was not read for this page.

**The smallest pride (ʿAli, in al-Tabari).** The report that even pleasure at a finer sandal-strap falls under "exaltedness upon the earth" shows how far the commentators took 28:83: pride begins in small comparisons.

#### research — What research shows
**Intellectual humility.** [Moderate; mostly correlational.] Porter and colleagues (2022) define it as "a meta-cognitive ability to recognize the limitations of one's beliefs and knowledge", and review evidence linking it to less "myside" bias, more exposure to opposing views, less hostility toward other groups, and more motivation to learn. They caution that "much of this work is correlational", that intervention studies are small, and that "intellectual humility is not a panacea". This fits the first half of the hadith: arrogance is "disdaining the truth".

**Narcissism and aggression.** [Established.] A review of 437 studies with 123,043 participants found narcissism "a significant risk factor for aggressive and violent behavior across the board", especially when narcissistic people feel provoked (Kjærvik & Bushman, *Psychological Bulletin*, 2021, as reported by Ohio State University). This fits the second half: "contempt for the people".

**Humble leaders.** [Not established from what was read.] Owens, Johnson and Mitchell (2013) studied "leader-expressed humility" in eight samples, including its links to employee retention, job satisfaction and engagement; the abstract reports one result — that "expressed humility may compensate for lower general mental ability" — and the others were not read.

#### practice — In daily life
**When you are corrected, say "you may be right" and check.** Rejecting the truth because of who says it is the first half of the hadith's definition.

**Notice whom you ignore.** The cleaner, the new person, the one with less status: greet them first.

**Lower your voice in an argument** (31:19), and **answer rudeness with peace** (25:63), without giving up the truth.

**Serve at home.** The Prophet (pbuh) "used to keep himself busy serving his family" (Bukhari 676).

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you think badly of yourself, that is not humility.** Humility in the hadith is about how you treat truth and other people, not about despising yourself. You are among "the children of Adam" whom God honoured (17:70).

**If religious people have looked down on you** for being less observant, that contempt is what the hadith calls arrogance — in them, not in you.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is wanting to look good arrogance?** No. The Prophet (pbuh) said: "Allah is Graceful and He loves Grace"; pride is "disdaining the truth… and contempt for the people" (Muslim 91).

**How do I know if I'm arrogant?** Ask: do I resist being corrected? Do I feel better than others because of my knowledge, piety, family or money? Al-Tabari's report from ʿAli suggests starting with small comparisons.

**Is confidence arrogance?** No. Confidence in what is true, with openness to being wrong, is compatible with humility.

**What is "exaltedness upon the earth" (28:83)?** Tyranny and self-glorification (Ikrima; Ibn Jurayj), transgression (Saʿid ibn Jubayr), as reported by al-Tabari; the verse follows the story of Qarun.

**Why is Iblis the model of arrogance?** Because his refusal rested on "I am better than him" (7:12): comparing himself and despising another.

**Hardest objection: "Religious people are often the most arrogant."** Sometimes they are, and the hadith condemns exactly that: contempt for people is pride, whatever one's piety. Al-Ghazali treated pride in knowledge and worship as a real danger (Janssens' review). *Limit:* the page names the teaching; it does not claim religious communities live it.

### Honest limits
Al-Ghazali's Book 29 was read only through a review; his full list of the sources of pride is not given. The research on intellectual humility is mostly correlational; the humble-leadership results were not read beyond the abstract. Religious arrogance is one of the reasons people give for leaving faith; this page names the teaching, not a claim that communities live it.
