# Deep answers for Ask — Batch A (Q7, Q13, Q14, Q31)

Deep research output, 2026-10-31, run against `docs/research/DEEP-RESEARCH-PROMPT.md` plus the reader critic's "missing" items from `docs/reader-scores/round-1-ask-baseline/`. **Not yet source-checked:** run the `source-checker` agent before any of this reaches the site. The research tool's own gaps are listed at the end.

The classical tradition is stricter than many modern Muslims assume, but it is also more divided, and more careful about who God holds to account, than many critics assume. All four Sunni schools prescribed death for the adult male apostate, but some scholars, early and modern, separate private loss of faith from the crime the law punished. Most scholars hold that Hell is eternal, but Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn al-Qayyim left a real dissent on record. And the mainstream position on salvation holds people accountable only once the message has truly reached them.

**TL;DR**
- **Apostasy:** All four Sunni schools held that an adult male apostate who refuses to repent is to be executed. A minority view, reported from Sufyan al-Thawri and Ibrahim al-Nakha'i and revived by Taha Jabir Alalwani and Egypt's former Grand Mufti Ali Gomaa, separates private change of belief from rebellion. In 2019, 22 countries had apostasy laws. Among Muslims who favour sharia as law, Pew's 2013 survey found support for executing apostates ranging from 4% to 86%, depending on the country.
- **Hell and salvation:** Most Sunni scholars hold that Hell is eternal for those who knowingly reject God. Ibn Taymiyya's last treatise argued that the punishment of unbelievers will end. Jon Hoover finds Ibn al-Qayyim agnostic in two books and universalist in a later one. Separately, Sunni authorities from al-Ghazali to al-Albani hold that someone the message never reached, or reached only in distorted form, is not punished for unbelief.
- **Loved ones:** Islamic law forbids asking forgiveness for someone who died a non-Muslim (9:113). It does not forbid loving them, honouring them, praying for the living to be guided, grieving, attending their funeral (permitted in Shafi'i and Hanbali texts), or visiting their grave, as the Prophet (pbuh) visited his mother's. No one but God knows whether the message truly reached a particular person.

## Q7 — Is leaving Islam punishable by death?

### The question, at its strongest
"Your most trusted hadith book reports the Prophet saying, 'Whoever changes his religion, kill him.' It does not say 'whoever betrays the state.' Every Sunni school made that law, and jurists called it consensus. People are still sentenced under such laws, and in Pew's 2013 survey 86% of Egyptian Muslims who back sharia supported executing apostates. The 'treason' reading looks like a recent invention made under Western pressure. If Islam is true, why would God threaten my life for honestly concluding it is not?"

### Why people ask it
People who doubt ask because their own safety and their family relationships may depend on the answer. Outsiders see this as the clearest test of whether Islam accepts freedom of conscience. Students ask because the textual case for the classical ruling is strong, and they want to know whether the reformist case is honest scholarship.

### Short answer
All four Sunni schools held that an adult, sane man who leaves Islam and refuses to return is to be executed after being asked to repent. They base this on authentic hadith, so the ruling is not a late invention. Three schools apply it to women too; the Hanafis imprison women instead. A minority of early figures reportedly did not support execution: ʿUmar, Ibrahim al-Nakhaʿi and Sufyan al-Thawri. Some modern scholars argue that the Prophet (pbuh) never executed anyone only for changing belief, and that the hadith targeted apostasy joined with hostility to the community; Taha Jabir Alalwani holds this view, and so did Ali Gomaa in 2007, though his wording was later disputed. That reading is serious but contested, because the words of the hadith are general. Doubting is not a crime, and someone who doubts has not left Islam.

### The full answer

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The Quran condemns apostasy severely but names no worldly punishment for it.** Verse 2:217 warns that whoever "reverts from his religion [to disbelief] and dies while he is a disbeliever – for those, their deeds have become worthless in this world and the Hereafter". Alalwani surveyed every Quranic use of *ridda* and concludes that "None of the verses… makes any mention of an earthly punishment for the sin or crime of apostasy". He adds that the Quran "refers to individuals repeatedly returning to unbelief after having believed, but does not mention that they should be killed" (ch. 2). Reformists also rely on 2:256: "There shall be no compulsion in [acceptance of] the religion."

**Limit:** Jurists held that the Sunna can add rulings the Quran does not state. Alalwani records that al-Shafiʿi read 2:217, 8:39, 9:5 and 39:65 together in support of the death penalty.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet taught
**Two authentic hadith carry the legal weight.** The first is narrated by ʿIkrima. ʿAli had burnt some people, and Ibn ʿAbbas said: "Had I been in his place I would not have burnt them… No doubt, I would have killed them, for the Prophet said, 'If somebody (a Muslim) discards his religion, kill him'" ({hadith:bukhari:3017}; parallel {hadith:bukhari:6922}, where the people are called *zanādiqa*, hidden heretics). Both are sahih by inclusion in al-Bukhari. The second is the "three cases" hadith from Ibn Masʿud (ra): a Muslim's blood "cannot be shed except in three cases: In Qisas for murder, a married person who commits illegal sexual intercourse and the one who reverts from Islam (apostate) and leaves the Muslims" ({hadith:bukhari:6878}; {hadith:muslim:1676}). In Arabic the third case is *al-tārik li-l-jamāʿa*, "abandoning the community".

**Did the Prophet (pbuh) ever execute anyone only for leaving Islam?** Alalwani: "never in his entire life did the Prophet put an apostate to death". He argues that reported killings were "due to the individuals' participation in war crimes or murder, not due to their apostasy" (ch. 3). The men of ʿUrayna, for example, killed a herdsman and stole camels. Two further points support this. Many people who left after the Night Journey were not harmed. And the Treaty of Hudaybiyya let anyone who left the Muslim camp return to Quraysh "freely without reprisal".

**Limit:** This is partly an argument from silence, and it does not remove the general wording. The cases at the conquest of Mecca (Ibn Khatal, Ibn Abi Sarh) were not checked (see Gaps).

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**The "treason" reading is a reading of context, not of the words, and should be judged that way.** The skeptic is right that the hadith says "changes his religion." The reformist case rests on three arguments:
- **The Prophet's practice:** no recorded execution for belief alone.
- **The companion hadith:** it joins leaving the religion to abandoning the *jamāʿa*, and in seventh-century Arabia the religious community was also the political and military one.
- **The Medinan setting:** Alalwani links the hadith to a "serious security situation" (33:60–62). He concludes: "if the hadith… is sound, he will have had this serious security situation in mind" (ch. 4). He also argues that later jurists absorbed Byzantine and Persian allegiance laws and "confused apostasy in the political sense with apostasy in the sense of a change in personal beliefs" (ch. 5).

**How strong is it?** It is moderately strong as history and weaker as a reading of the text. In its favour, the "community" clause is authentic, and the wars of *ridda* involved refusal of allegiance and *zakāt*. Against it, Ibn ʿAbbas himself applied the hadith to heretics who had only held beliefs, and the classical jurists read it generally despite knowing the context. Alalwani's point that the hadith is *āḥād* (a single-chain report) persuades only those who already restrict such reports.

**Who questioned execution early?** Alalwani names "Companions ʿUmar ibn al-Khattab…, Ibrahim al-Nakhaʿi…, and Sufyan al-Thawri", whose views "did not receive publicity" (ch. 1). The usual basis for ʿUmar is the *Muwatta* report (no. 1420). Told that an apostate had been killed at once, he asked, "Didn't you imprison him for three days… and call on him to tawba?" and said, "I am not pleased." **Limit:** This shows that ʿUmar required a chance to repent, not that he rejected execution. Ibn Qudama in fact lists al-Nakhaʿi among those who held that the apostate is killed. The dissents are thinly documented.

**Who holds the "not for belief alone" view today?**
- **Taha Jabir Alalwani** (1935–2016, Azhar-trained jurist): apostasy without "anything else deemed a criminal act… remains strictly between God and the individual".
- **Ali Gomaa** (Grand Mufti of Egypt 2003–2013), writing in 2007: "can a person who is Muslim choose a religion other than Islam? The answer is yes… If the case in question is one of merely rejecting faith, then there is no worldly punishment." He still left a role for the courts where "undermining the foundations of the society" was involved. Gulf News (Ramadan Al Sherbini, 25 July 2007) then quoted him: "What I actually said is that Islam prohibits a Muslim from changing his religion and that apostasy is a crime, which must be punished." His position is real, but its wording is disputed.
- **Mohammad Hashim Kamali and Tariq Ramadan**: reported, with Alalwani, as holding that "the Qur'an does not prescribe a temporal punishment for apostasy" (secondary source).
- **Abdullah and Hassan Saeed**: argue similarly (secondary summary only).

#### fiqh — What the four schools hold
**All four schools prescribe death for the unrepentant adult male apostate. They differ on the waiting period and on women.** The waiting period is *istitāba*, the formal invitation to repent.
- **Hanafi:** al-Kasani (*Badaʾiʿ* 7/134) says asking him to repent is "recommended… but it is not obligatory". Al-Sarakhsi (as quoted) grants three days if the apostate asks. On women, al-Zaylaʿi says: "A female apostate is not to be killed; instead, she is to be detained until she embraces Islam." Alalwani notes that the Hanafis discussed apostasy under *siyar* (the law of war).
- **Maliki:** Khalil's *Mukhtasar*: "He is asked to repent for three days, without hunger, thirst or punishment; if he repents [he is left], otherwise he is killed." Women are treated like men.
- **Shafi'i:** al-Nawawi (*Minhaj* p. 293): asking the male and female apostate to repent is obligatory (one view: recommended) and immediate (one view: three days); "if they persist, they are killed."
- **Hanbali:** Ibn Qudama (*al-Mughni* 1538) quotes al-Khiraqi: "Whoever apostatises from Islam, man or woman, being adult and sane, is invited back for three days… otherwise he is killed." He adds a second narration from Ahmad that the invitation is only recommended.

**Is "not executing women" evidence that the penalty was about combat?** Only partly. The Hanafi exemption rests on the ban on killing women in war, which does treat apostasy like enemy status. But the Hanafis executed peaceful male apostates too, and the other schools executed women. The exemption shows that some jurists thought about apostasy through the law of war. It does not show that the penalty required actual fighting.

#### history — History and today's laws
**Apostasy laws are now a minority, concentrated in Muslim-majority states.**
- **Pew (2022):** in 2019, 79 of 198 countries and territories (40%) had blasphemy laws and 22 (11%) had apostasy laws. Blasphemy can carry death in Afghanistan, Brunei, Iran, Mauritania, Nigeria, Pakistan and Saudi Arabia. Brunei implemented death sentences for apostasy in 2019. In Algeria, converts lose inheritance rights.
- **Humanists International (2021):** apostasy is punishable by death in at least ten countries: Afghanistan, Iran, Malaysia, Maldives, Mauritania, Nigeria, Qatar, Saudi Arabia, the UAE and Yemen.
- **USCIRF** ("Apostasy, Blasphemy, and Hate Speech Laws in Africa", December 2019): in Africa, "at least 9 countries have apostasy laws", and the press release counts 26 countries with blasphemy laws.

**Enforcement is uneven, and the greater danger is often mobs and families rather than courts.** In Pakistan, "at least 17 individuals were sentenced to death on blasphemy charges in 2019… although the Pakistani government has never actually executed anyone for blasphemy" (Pew). Social penalties such as divorce, loss of child custody and disinheritance are more common than executions.

**Attitudes.** Pew's 2013 survey was based on more than 38,000 face-to-face interviews in 39 countries (2008–2012); it excluded Saudi Arabia, India, China and Syria. Among Muslims who favour making sharia official law, the share supporting "the death penalty for people who leave the Muslim religion" was:
- **Highest:** Egypt 86%, Jordan 82%, Afghanistan 79%, Pakistan 76%, the Palestinian territories 66%, Malaysia 62%.
- **Middle:** Bangladesh 44%, Thailand 27%, Tajikistan 22%, Indonesia 18%.
- **Lowest:** Albania 8%, Kazakhstan 4%.

These are shares of sharia supporters, not of all Muslims. The Washington Post's corrected all-Muslim figures (88% Egypt, 62% Pakistan) do not reconcile with Pew's tables (see Gaps). Pew reports that support for sharia "generally varies little by age, gender or education". No age breakdown and no trend data for the apostasy question were found.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"The treason reading is modern apologetics."** *Reply:* It builds on an authentic phrase, on the Hanafi law-of-war treatment, and on early dissent. **Limit:** As a developed position it is mostly modern, and the pre-modern record overwhelmingly favours execution.

**"A confident religion would not threaten leavers."** *Reply:* Classical jurists saw public apostasy as an attack on the public order founded on religion, much as treason is treated today. Many modern scholars hold that this reasoning does not apply to private belief in citizenship states. **Limit:** This explains the ruling historically; it does not justify the deaths of individuals killed for belief alone.

**"Will God punish me for doubting?"** *Reply:* Doubt is not disbelief. In Alalwani's reading, apostasy affects faith only when one "open[s] his or her heart consciously and willingly to a denial of the truth". *Istitāba* existed to answer doubts: al-Shirbini notes that the apostate "may have been struck by a doubt". **Limit:** The Sahih Muslim hadith that calls disturbing thoughts "clear faith" was not verified in this pass.

#### views — The range of views
- **Classical, all four schools:** death after *istitāba*; the Hanafis exempt women.
- **Indefinite repentance:** attributed to al-Nakhaʿi and al-Thawri.
- **Discretionary, under *siyāsa* (state policy):** noted by Alalwani.
- **No worldly penalty for belief alone:** Alalwani, Gomaa (2007), Kamali and Ramadan (reported).

Shiʿi and Ibadi views were not researched.

### Questions readers ask next
**Am I an apostate because I doubt?** No. Questioning is not *ridda*, which classical law defined as a clear, chosen renunciation.

**I have left Islam. How do I stay safe?** Check the law where you live; Pew and Humanists International list the relevant countries. In those countries, avoid public declarations and avoid posting online under your real name, and consult human-rights organisations before any legal step. Tell one trusted person outside your family, and know where you could go.

**What should a family do?** No family member has any right to harm or threaten them. Even classical rulings put any punishment in the hands of a court alone. Verses 31:15 and 60:8 require kindness. Keep the relationship and the conversation open.

**Does the hadith say "betrays the state"?** No. That reading is an argument from context, not a translation of the words.

**Do many Muslims reject the death penalty?** It depends heavily on the country: among sharia supporters, 4% supported it in Kazakhstan and 18% in Indonesia, against 86% in Egypt.

### Honest limits
The textual case for the classical ruling is strong. The reformist case depends on context and on the absence of recorded executions. The early dissents are known mainly from later reports, and the polling data are old and inconsistently reported.

## Q13 — Is eternal Hell just for finite sins?

### Sources read
- Jon Hoover, "Islamic Universalism: Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyya's Salafī Deliberations on the Duration of Hell-Fire," *The Muslim World* 99.1 (2009): 181–201, doi:10.1111/j.1478-1913.2009.01260.x (abstract and excerpts) — Ibn al-Qayyim's positions.
- Jon Hoover, chapter on Ibn al-Wazir in *Locating Hell in Islamic Traditions* (Brill, 2016), preview — Ibn Taymiyya, Ibn al-Qayyim, al-Subki. https://brill.com/previewpdf/book/edcoll/9789004301368/B9789004301368_011.xml
- Jon Hoover, *Ibn Taymiyya* (Oneworld, 2019), p. 8, as quoted on a third-party site — his summary of Ibn Taymiyya's conclusion.
- Excerpted translation of Ibn Taymiyya, *al-Radd ʿalā man qāla bi-fanāʾ al-janna wa-l-nār*, ed. al-Simhari (Riyadh, 1995) — structure of the treatise.
- E. Bazzano, review of Khalil, *AJIS* 30:1 (2013) — the meaning of *abadan*.
- Yaqeen Institute, "Clinicians, Imams, and the Whisperings of Satan"; IOCDF blog (July 2025); JBIMA qualitative study — scrupulosity.

### The question, at its strongest
"A life lasts seventy years. Every human sin is finite in act and in harm. Infinite torment for finite wrongs is out of all proportion. 'It was against an infinite God' proves too much, because then every sin would merit infinity. 'They would have kept sinning' (6:28) punishes crimes that were never committed, and it assumes the very thing in question. Even Ibn Taymiyya apparently concluded that the punishment ends. Why should I believe a merciful God torments most of humanity for ever?"

### Why people ask it
It is the most direct moral challenge to Islamic teaching about the afterlife. It weighs most heavily on those who have lost non-Muslim loved ones and on those with religious anxiety.

### Short answer
Most Sunni scholars hold that Hell is eternal for those who knowingly and persistently reject God. Al-Subki, the leading Shafiʿi judge of 14th-century Damascus, declared the contrary view unbelief. But Ibn Taymiyya's last treatise argued that while the Garden is eternal, the punishment of unbelievers will end. His student Ibn al-Qayyim was agnostic in two books and leaned toward ultimate mercy in a third. Islam also holds that no one is punished until the message has truly reached them, so "eternal Hell" does not mean "everyone who was not Muslim." The proportionality objection has real replies, but none removes all of its force. On this question the tradition itself contains a minority of great names.

### The full answer

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The Quran says the people of the Fire abide "therein forever" (*khālidīna fīhā abadan*) at 4:169, 33:65 and 72:23. In Sura Hud it attaches an exception.** Verse 11:107: "[They will be] abiding therein as long as the heavens and the earth endure, except what your Lord should will. Indeed, your Lord is an effective accomplisher of what He intends." Of the people of the Garden, 11:108 adds: "a bestowal uninterrupted." The minority argued from this contrast: the Garden is described as uninterrupted, while the Fire carries an exception. They also cited 78:23's *aḥqāb* ("ages"), a long but countable period.

**Limit:** al-Tabari, al-Qurtubi, Ibn Kathir and al-Razi on these verses were not read in this pass (see Gaps). According to Hoover's summary, the majority treats the clear "forever" verses as governing the exception.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Ibn Taymiyya did not merely say "there is no consensus"; he argued that the punishment ends.** According to Jon Hoover, the treatise *al-Radd ʿalā man qāla bi-fanāʾ al-janna wa-l-nār* appears to be his last work, written in answer to Ibn al-Qayyim. It rejects Jahm ibn Safwan's view that both abodes perish. Hoover summarises its conclusion: "the reward of Paradise is everlasting while the chastisement of unbelievers in Hell-Fire will end". He adds that Ibn Taymiyya's reasoning "leads to the conclusion that God will use Hell-fire to bring all creatures into complete accord with the purposes for which they were naturally constituted". In Hoover's words, for both men "Hell is therapeutic and reformative." The treatise cites Companion reports from ʿUmar and Ibn Masʿud, among others. It takes *fanāʾ* to mean that the punishment ceases, not necessarily that the Fire ceases to exist. Hoover has also documented later attempts to deny the treatise or play it down: al-Albani's claim that it was written "before mastering the religious sciences", and ʿAli al-Harbi's 1990 book (Hoover, in *Debating Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn Qayyim*, de Gruyter 2013, 377–399).

**Ibn al-Qayyim: agnostic, then universalist, with disputed passages.** Hoover finds that in *Ḥādī al-Arwāḥ* and *Shifāʾ al-ʿAlīl* Ibn al-Qayyim presents an "agnostic option on the duration of chastisement… as his own view". In *Mukhtaṣar al-Ṣawāʿiq* he argues "for an Islamic universalism without reserve". Hoover also notes that he may have "had more doubts… than his master", partly because of al-Subki. Al-Harbi cites four passages in which he affirms eternity. A clean "change of view" therefore cannot be established.

**Al-Subki** (d. 1355) "issued a sharp refutation of Ibn Taymiyya, declared assent to the annihilation of the Fire unbelief and reasserted" its eternity, resting chiefly on the claim of consensus (*ijmāʿ*). **Limit:** only summaries of his treatise were read.

**Plain terms.** The Ashʿari school (after al-Ashʿari, d. 935) and the Maturidi school (after al-Maturidi, d. 944) are the main Sunni theological schools. "Athari" refers to the tradition-based creed linked to Ahmad ibn Hanbal and Ibn Taymiyya. All three, as institutions, teach that Hell is eternal for unbelievers; the dissent came from within the Athari line. To "knowingly and persistently reject God" means to turn down a truth made clear (Q14), with understanding and freedom, until death. Never hearing, hearing a distortion, or doubting are not included.

#### reasoning — How philosophers and theologians argue about it
**There are four main replies to the proportionality objection.**
1. **Gravity:** punishment tracks what is rejected, not how long the act lasted. **Limit:** this proves too much unless only final, total rejection of God carries that weight. Doctrine does say this, since sinful believers eventually leave the Fire.
2. **A fixed state:** Hell is the continuing state of a soul that has made itself unwilling to turn to God. Verse 6:28 ("if they were returned, they would return to that which they were forbidden") is read this way. **Limit:** as a proof, 6:28 is circular for anyone who does not already accept the Quran. Whether a soul's state can change after death is exactly what Ibn Taymiyya and Ibn al-Qayyim questioned.
3. **The punishment ends:** the Ibn Taymiyya line. **Limit:** it sits uneasily with *abadan*, and most Sunni scholars reject it.
4. **Trust:** God has promised that "no soul will be treated unjustly at all" (21:47). **Limit:** this asks for trust rather than giving an explanation.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Most people who ever lived will burn for ever."** *Reply:* That does not follow. Verse 17:15 says "never would We punish until We sent a messenger", and those the message did not truly reach are excused or tested (Q14). **Limit:** those who heard clearly and rejected remain condemned on the majority view.

**"Fear of Hell harms people."** *Reply:* For some people it does. Clinicians call this scrupulosity, a religious form of OCD (obsessive-compulsive disorder), known among Muslims as *waswās*. The Yaqeen paper stresses: "waswasah is not a 'Muslim disease,' but rather a religious presentation of OCD within Muslims." A participant in a UK study said: "'Oh no, I'm going to Hell'… I was just constantly thinking to myself about Hell fire." A clinician quoted by the IOCDF says: "Islam allows for you to go with what you know and leave the doubt." **Limit:** no controlled studies of Muslims specifically were found.

#### heart — For the grieving and the anxious
**The doctrine is not a verdict on any one person.** Ibn al-Qayyim held that whether proof was established against a particular individual "is a matter to be decided by Allah." If you are tormented by certainty that you are damned, by endless checking and reassurance-seeking, this may be OCD rather than piety. It responds to treatment, ideally with a therapist who understands religious life.

### Questions readers ask next
**Did Ibn Taymiyya believe Hell ends?** According to Hoover, yes: he held that the punishment of unbelievers ends, though some Salafi scholars dispute the attribution.

**Did Ibn al-Qayyim change his mind?** His books differ, so a single, final view cannot be established.

**Is believing Hell may end unbelief?** Al-Subki said so; Ibn Taymiyya disagreed. Most Sunni scholars hold that Hell is eternal.

**What about those who never heard?** They are not condemned for unbelief (Q14).

**Can someone in Hell change?** The majority says no; the Ibn Taymiyya line says the Fire purifies.

### Honest limits
The justice of eternal Hell cannot be demonstrated to someone outside faith. The classical commentaries on 11:107 and al-Subki's full arguments were not checked directly, and the psychological data are thin.

## Q14 — Are good people of other faiths saved? What about those who never heard?

### Sources read

### The question, at its strongest
"My neighbour is kinder and more honest than most Muslims I know. She has read about Islam and simply does not find it true. Your Prophet says that any Jew or Christian who 'hears of me' and dies without believing is in the Fire. Your Quran says any other religion 'will never be accepted' (3:85). So she goes to Hell for ever, for an honest conclusion. And 'maybe she didn't hear it properly' sounds like an escape hatch. Who decides?"

### Why people ask it
It is the question every Muslim with non-Muslim friends eventually faces, and the one outsiders find most alienating.

### Short answer
There are two different cases. The first is the person the message never reached, or reached only in distorted form. Mainstream Sunni scholars, from al-Ghazali to Ibn al-Qayyim, al-Nawawi and al-Albani, hold that such people are not punished for unbelief, and many add that they will be tested on the Day of Judgement. The second is the person who heard the message clearly and accurately and still rejected it. The strict mainstream position, based on Muslim 153 and 3:85 as Ibn Kathir reads them, is that such a person is not saved. Al-Ghazali calls such people "the unbelievers who deny the revelation." The real uncertainty is where the line falls between the two cases, and only God can draw it for any individual. Most Muslims Pew surveyed in 2013 said that Islam alone leads to salvation.

### The full answer

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The Quran contains both a broad promise and an exclusive claim.** Verse 2:62 (cf. 5:69): "Indeed, those who believed and those who were Jews or Christians or Sabeans [before Prophet Muhammad] – those [among them] who believed in Allah and the Last Day and did righteous work – will have their reward with their Lord, and no fear will there be concerning them, nor will they grieve." Verse 3:85: "And whoever desires other than Islam as religion – never will it be accepted from him, and he, in the Hereafter, will be among the losers." Verse 17:15 states the governing principle: "And never would We punish until We sent a messenger."

**The abrogation report.** Ibn Kathir records that "ʿAli bin Abi Talhah narrated from Ibn ʿAbbas, about [2:62]… that Allah revealed the following Ayah afterwards, [3:85]." Ibn Kathir does not simply declare 2:62 cancelled (*naskh* is the replacement of an earlier ruling by a later one). He comments that the report "indicates that Allah does not accept any deed or work from anyone, unless it conforms to the Law of Muhammad that is, after Allah sent Muhammad." He reads 2:62 as praising the "earlier nations who were righteous and obedient."

**What does "Islam" mean in 3:85?** Ibn Kathir uses both senses. He describes Islam as "the religion that He sent His Books and Messengers with, which is the worship of Allah Alone", and he adds that "after Allah sent Muhammad, whoever meets Allah following a path other than Muhammad's, it will not be accepted of him." **Limit:** al-Tabari on 2:62 was not read (see Gaps).

#### sunnah — What the Prophet taught
**Muslim 153.** Abu Hurayra (ra): "By Him in Whose hand is the life of Muhammad, he who amongst the community of Jews or Christians hears about me, but does not affirm his belief in that with which I have been sent and dies in this state (of disbelief), he shall be but one of the denizens of Hell-Fire" ({hadith:muslim:153}). It is sahih by its inclusion in Sahih Muslim.

**Why "hears of me" is read as hearing accurately, and who says so.** Scholars disagree on how much hearing counts.
- **Al-Qari** (d. 1606, a Hanafi commentator) reads it minimally: "if anyone knows about my message."
- **Al-Nawawi** (d. 1277) infers the reverse case: "whoever was not reached by the call of Islam will be excused" (*Sharh Sahih Muslim* 2/188).
- **Al-Albani** (d. 1999) sets a strict standard for what counts as reaching: "If the message of Islam reached the people in a forged manner… altered… changed… then, I am the first one to say that the message has not reached such people, because what is meant by the message having reached them, is the pure clear message" (Islamweb 243244).

**The testing of the people of *fatra*.** *Fatra* means an interval between prophets. Al-Aswad ibn Sariʿ (ra) reports that four people will plead on the Day of Resurrection: "a deaf man who cannot hear anything, a mad man, a senile man, and a man who died in the period before Islam." The last will say, "O Lord, no messenger from You came to me." They are commanded to enter a fire: "if they enter the fire, it will become cool and peaceful" (Musnad Ahmad 16301). The parallel from Abu Hurayra adds: "whoever does not enter it will be dragged to it."

**Gradings of the testing hadith:**
- **Darussalam/al-Arnaʾut:** hasan (good) as a hadith, though this chain is broken.
- **Al-Albani:** sahih (*al-Sahiha* 3/418–419).
- **Ibn al-Qayyim:** reports that al-Bayhaqi and ʿAbd al-Haqq graded it sahih (*Ahkam Ahl al-Dhimma* 2/1149).
- **Ibn Kathir:** the reports "strengthen" one another (*Tafsir* 5/58).
- **Ibn Hajar:** "proven to be sound via saheeh isnaads" (*Fath al-Bari* 3/246).

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Al-Ghazali's three categories.** In *Fayṣal al-Tafriqa* (*The Decisive Criterion*), al-Ghazali (d. 1111) writes:

> "I hold that most Christians of the present time… are included in the divine mercy, Allah Most High willing… One group has never even heard the name of Muhammad… so are excused. A second group has heard of his name, his character, and the miraculous signs — these are the unbelievers who deny the revelation. And a third group… have heard of the name of Muhammad… but not of his character and attributes, since childhood, they have heard that a lying impostor named Muhammad claimed to be a prophet. Such people fall into the same category as the first group."

**The Ashʿari position.** Al-Jurjani: "There is no accountability upon the one whom the call of a prophet definitively did not reach." Faraz Rabbani traces the view through al-Sanusi and al-Bajuri and calls it "solidly mainstream Sunni theology." The Maturidi position could not be sourced (see Gaps).

**The Athari position.** Ibn Taymiyya (*al-Jawab al-Sahih* 2/298) and Ibn al-Qayyim (*Ahkam Ahl al-Dhimma* 2/1137) favour testing in the Hereafter. Ibn al-Qayyim also answers the question "who decides?" (*Tariq al-Hijratayn* 2/900):

> "He will not punish anyone except those against whom proof is established through the Messengers… But as for the question of whether or not proof is established against any particular individual, this is a matter to be decided by Allah and we cannot issue rulings concerning specific individuals in that regard."

**The strict answer, stated plainly.** Suppose someone received the message as al-Ghazali's second group did, with "his name, his character, and the miraculous signs", and died rejecting it. Al-Ghazali, Ibn Kathir and Ibn al-Qayyim all hold that such a person is not saved. Rabbani adds that "amnesty for those who are not reached by the message does not constitute validation of other religions." **Limit:** the same scholars forbid declaring this of any named person.

**Khalil's study.** Mohammad Hassan Khalil (Michigan State University), in *Islam and the Fate of Others* (OUP, 2012), studies four figures: al-Ghazali ("Damnation as Exception"), Ibn ʿArabi ("All Paths Lead to God"), Ibn Taymiyya ("The Redemption of Humanity") and Rashid Rida ("The Modern Scene"). Bazzano summarises the finding as "not a monolithic consensus but… a cacophony of opinions… which overwhelmingly envisions a heaven filled with Muslims and non-Muslims." This describes Khalil's influential sample, not the whole tradition.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"'True hearing' is an escape hatch."** *Reply:* The tradition says explicitly that God decides. The standard rests on 17:15, and al-Ghazali stated it in the 11th century, long before modern pluralism. **Limit:** nobody can check it on earth, and it gives no one certainty in either direction.

**"Honest error is punished."** *Reply:* What the mainstream holds culpable is rejection after the truth has become clear, not mistaken belief, and only God knows the inner state. **Limit:** for someone who truly understood and was not persuaded, the strict view offers no comfort, and Muslims should say so plainly.

#### views — The range of views
**Scholarly views range from strict exclusivism to perennialism.**
- **Strict exclusivism:** excuses are read narrowly.
- **The mainstream "message-reached" view:** al-Ghazali, the Ashʿaris, Ibn Taymiyya, Ibn al-Qayyim, al-Albani.
- **Wider hope:** Ibn ʿArabi, and Ibn Taymiyya's view on the end of Hell (Q13).
- **Modern pluralist readings of 2:62:** a minority.
- **Not documented here:** Perennialism (Schuon, Nasr: all major religions as valid paths), and Mutahhari's Shiʿi distinction between the culpable and non-culpable ignorant (*jāhil muqaṣṣir/qāṣir*) (see Gaps).

**What ordinary Muslims believe.** Pew (2013) found that in 34 of 38 countries at least half of Muslims say Islam is "the one true religion that can lead to eternal life in heaven." The figure was 96% in Egypt and Jordan and 95% in Iraq, but 29% in Kazakhstan (where 49% said many religions can) and 37% in Albania. Muslims who pray several times a day held this view more often; in Russia the gap was 41 points. US Muslims are "much more open" to many paths.

#### heart — For the grieving reader
**If someone you love died outside Islam, neither you nor anyone else can know that the message truly reached them.** Al-Ghazali hoped that most Christians of his day were within God's mercy. Ibn al-Qayyim forbade judging any individual. You may leave your loved one with God, and hope.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is my sincere friend who knows Islam well going to Hell?** On the strict view, yes, if she truly understood and rejected. Whether she did is known only to God.

**Was 2:62 abrogated?** A report from Ibn ʿAbbas says so. Ibn Kathir reads it as meaning that after Muhammad (pbuh), only his law is accepted.

**What happens to people who never heard?** They are excused or tested. Musnad Ahmad 16301 is graded sahih by al-Albani and hasan by al-Arnaʾut.

**Is the "never heard properly" excuse fringe?** No. Al-Ghazali, al-Nawawi, al-Jurjani, Ibn al-Qayyim and al-Albani all hold it.

**Do most Muslims believe only Muslims are saved?** In most countries Pew surveyed, yes.

### Honest limits
No one has defined precisely how much accurate knowledge counts as "hearing." The Maturidi, Shiʿi and Perennialist views were not documented, and survey answers do not capture nuance.

## Q31 — Are my non-Muslim loved ones lost?

### Sources read
- Sahih al-Bukhari 1360, 4772, 2620, 4392; Sahih Muslim 976, 203, 2524 (sunnah.com; partly via subagent).
- al-Suyuti, *Masalik al-Hunafaʾ*, trans. G. F. Haddad, https://www.livingislam.org/suyuti_e.html (via subagent).
- Federal Territory Mufti (Malaysia), *Irsyad al-Fatwa* 321, citing al-Nawawi and Malik's *Mudawwana* 1/261 (https://www.muftiwp.gov.my/index.php/en/artikel/irsyad-fatwa/irsyad-fatwa-umum-cat/3720-irsyad-al-fatwa-series-321-attending-the-funeral-of-non-muslim-neighbour); Islamweb fatwa 367663, citing Ahmad via al-Khallal (https://www.islamweb.net/en/fatwa/367663/attending-funeral-of-non-muslim-relative); *al-Fatawa al-Hindiyya* 5/348 (via subagent).
- Islamweb fatwa 243244.
- J. H. Wortmann & C. L. Park, *Death Studies* 32 (2008): 703–736.
- T. Cheung, P. H. Lee & P. S. F. Yip, *BMC Research Notes* 10:242 (2017). https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s13104-017-2588-7

### The question, at its strongest
"My mother is not Muslim. My father died last year as a Christian. They raised me and loved me. Am I meant to believe they are lost for ever, and that I may not even ask God to forgive my father? Even the Prophet was not allowed to pray for his own mother. What is the point of my love?"

### Why people ask it
Converts, children of mixed families and the bereaved live with this every day. It shapes how they grieve, pray and visit graves.

### Short answer
Islam forbids asking God's forgiveness for someone who died a non-Muslim (9:113). It does not ask you to stop loving them. That ruling applies "after it has become clear" that they are people of the Fire, and no one knows that about a particular person, because no one knows whether the message truly reached them (Q14). You may pray for a living relative's guidance, honour non-Muslim parents, grieve, attend the funeral (permitted in Shafiʿi and Hanbali texts), and visit the grave, as the Prophet (pbuh) did his mother's. The strict majority holds that someone who clearly heard and rejected is not saved, but the "never heard clearly" excuse is mainstream, not fringe.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**Your love is not a sin; the Prophet (pbuh) wept at his mother's grave.** Abu Hurayra (ra): "The Messenger of Allah visited the grave of his mother and he wept, and moved others around him to tears, and said: I sought permission from my Lord to beg forgiveness for her but it was not granted to me, and I sought permission to visit her grave and it was granted to me so visit the graves, for that makes you mindful of death" ({hadith:muslim:976}). The visit, the tears and the remembering were all permitted. Grief for a non-Muslim parent is part of the Prophet's own life.

**Leave the judgement with God.** You are not asked to know where they are. You are asked to be faithful to God and good to them.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The verse and its occasion.** Verse 9:113: "It is not for the Prophet and those who have believed to ask forgiveness for the polytheists, even if they were relatives, after it has become clear to them that they are companions of Hellfire." It was revealed after Abu Talib died, the uncle to whom the Prophet (pbuh) had said: "I will keep on asking Allah's forgiveness for you unless I am forbidden (by Allah) to do so" ({hadith:bukhari:1360}). Verse 28:56 was also revealed about him ({hadith:bukhari:4772}).

**Kindness to non-Muslim parents is commanded.** Verse 31:15: "do not obey them but accompany them in [this] world with appropriate kindness." Verse 60:8: "Allah does not forbid you from those who do not fight you because of religion and do not expel you from your homes – from being righteous toward them and acting justly toward them."

#### sunnah — What the Prophet taught
**Asmaʾ bint Abi Bakr (ra) and her mother:** "My mother came to me… and she was a pagan… shall I keep good relations with her?' The Prophet said, 'Yes, keep good relation with her'" ({hadith:bukhari:2620}).

**Praying for guidance.** When the tribe of Daws refused Islam, the Prophet (pbuh) prayed: "O Allah, guide the tribe of Daws and bring them to me" ({hadith:bukhari:4392}; {hadith:muslim:2524}).

**The Prophet's father.** Anas (ra) reports that a man asked where his father was. The Prophet (pbuh) said, "in the Fire," then called him back: "Verily my father and your father are in the Fire" ({hadith:muslim:203}).

**How scholars read Muslim 203 and 976.** Mulla ʿAli al-Qari and Ibrahim al-Halabi read them literally. Al-Suyuti (d. 1505) argued in *Masalik al-Hunafaʾ*: "The Prophet's parents died before he was sent as Prophet and there is no punishment for them, since 'We never punish until We send a messenger' (17:15). Our Ashʿari Imams… agree… that one who dies while daʿwa has not reached him, dies saved." **Limit:** his view requires setting aside the plain sense of two sahih hadiths. Many scholars rejected it, and some reports he used are judged fabricated.

#### fiqh — What the four schools hold
- **Forgiveness for a deceased non-Muslim** is forbidden (9:113; Muslim 976). Islamweb: "it is not permissible to supplicate for him or seek forgiveness for him."
- **Prayer for a living relative's guidance** is permitted, following the Daws precedent.
- **Shafiʿi:** al-Nawawi: "There is no problem for a Muslim to follow the funeral of a non-Muslim relative" (*Minhaj* p. 62). He also reports that visiting the grave is allowed according to the majority (*al-Majmuʿ* 5/144).
- **Maliki:** Malik (*Mudawwana* 1/261) says a Muslim should not wash his disbelieving father or follow the bier, "unless he fears that he may be neglected, in which case he may bury him."
- **Hanbali:** Ahmad said of a non-Muslim parent or relative: "if… he attended it, then there is no harm." He cited ʿUmar's instruction to Abu Waʾil about his Christian mother: "Ride in her funeral procession and be in front of her" (al-Khallal, via Islamweb 367663).
- **Hanafi:** condolence to a non-Muslim is allowed, with a formula of comfort (*al-Fatawa al-Hindiyya* 5/348).
- **Visiting the grave and grieving** are permitted on the basis of Muslim 976.
- **A minority position:** some modern Hanafi (Deobandi) fatwas forbid attending a non-Muslim's funeral. TheMufti (IslamQA.org), citing *Ahsanul Fataawa* 4/243, states that "it is not permissible for a Muslim to attend the funeral of a non-Muslim". By contrast, *al-Bahr al-Raʾiq* 2/205, cited by Faraz Rabbani, allows that "one may follow their funeral from afar."

#### science — What grief research says
**Religion tends to help bereaved people, but not consistently.** Wortmann and Park (2008) reviewed 73 studies and found the relations "generally positive but inconsistent and vary depending in part on how religion/spirituality is measured." A systematic review of 32 studies (Becker et al., *Palliative Medicine* 21(3), 2007) found some positive effect in 94% of them: 22 positive, 6 limited positive, 2 with no effect, and 1 in which religion made depression worse. Even so, it concluded that "available data do not allow for a definite answer on whether religious/spiritual beliefs effectively influence bereavement." Among 850 Hong Kong nurses, faith was "weakly associated with lower self-reported depression in bereavement" (Cheung, Lee & Yip, 2017). **Limit:** none of these studies covers grief for a loved one of a different faith, and no data on mixed-faith families were found.

#### views — The range of views
- **Strict:** those who clearly heard and rejected are lost, and funeral attendance is discouraged (Malik; some modern Hanafi fatwas).
- **Mainstream:** no prayer for forgiveness, but kindness, funerals (Shafiʿi, Hanbali), grave visits and grief are all allowed. The individual's fate is left to God.
- **Wider hope:** al-Suyuti on the Prophet's parents; al-Ghazali's hope for the Christians of his time.

### Questions readers ask next
**May I pray for my living non-Muslim mother?** Yes: for her guidance and her wellbeing.

**May I say "God have mercy on him" for my late father?** Asking forgiveness is forbidden where it is clear he died a non-Muslim. No school-by-school ruling on general words of mercy was found, so ask a scholar you trust.

**May I attend the funeral?** Shafiʿi and Hanbali texts allow it for relatives; Malik restricted it.

**Is it wrong to hope they are saved?** No. The "message did not truly reach them" excuse is mainstream.

**How do I live with missing them?** Visit the grave, keep doing the good things they loved, be kind to the family who remain, and bring your grief to God, as the Prophet (pbuh) did at his mother's grave.

### Honest limits
The plain sense of Muslim 203 and 976 is strict, and al-Suyuti's alternative is a minority view. Several practical rulings were not verified school by school, and no research on grief in mixed-faith families was found.
