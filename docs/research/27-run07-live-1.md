## M1 — Justice even against oneself

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via the site's verified Tanzil data (`src/data/quran.json`) — 4:135, 5:8, 16:90, 4:58 — the verses quoted.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 3475 (English via ccistudyapp.com, https://ccistudyapp.com/hadith/bukhari/3475) — the Makhzumi woman.
- Sahih Muslim 2605a (via hadithunlocked.com, https://hadithunlocked.com/muslim:2605a) — the Prophet's words and al-Zuhri's addition on lying.
- Ibn al-Qayyim, *al-Ṭuruq al-Ḥukmiyya* 1/13, as quoted in English by Abu Amina Elias, "Ibn Qayyim on Sharia: Justice is the objective of law in Islam" (6 May 2013), https://www.abuaminaelias.com/dailyhadithonline/2013/05/06/ibn-al-qayyim-sharia-justice/ — translator not named.
- "Rulings pertaining to the testimony", islamicfiqh.net, https://islamicfiqh.net/en/newmuslims/22/103/articles/252/ — testimony for and against relatives; women's testimony.
- G. D. Walters and P. C. Bolger, "Procedural justice perceptions, legitimacy beliefs, and compliance with the law: a meta-analysis", *Journal of Experimental Criminology* 15 (2019), pp. 341–372, https://dx.doi.org/10.1007/s11292-018-9338-2 — abstract.

### In brief
Justice in the Quran is owed to everyone, including people you dislike, and it binds you even when it costs you or your family: "be persistently standing firm in justice, witnesses for Allah, even if it be against yourselves or parents and relatives" (4:135). The Prophet (pbuh) refused to let the powerful escape the law, saying that earlier nations were destroyed because they punished the poor and spared the noble.

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Two words for justice.** *ʿAdl* means putting things in balance, giving each their due; *qisṭ* means fairness, especially in measures and judgements. 4:135 uses *qisṭ* ("standing firm in justice", *qawwāmīna bi-l-qisṭ*); 16:90 uses *ʿadl*. Both are about giving what is due, not about being kind or soft.

**"Witnesses for Allah."** A witness (*shāhid*) tells what happened. To be a witness "for Allah" means testifying as if before God, not for your side.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Justice against yourself and your family (4:135).** "O you who have believed, be persistently standing firm in justice, witnesses for Allah, even if it be against yourselves or parents and relatives. Whether one is rich or poor, Allah is more worthy of both. So follow not [personal] inclination, lest you not be just. And if you distort [your testimony] or refuse [to give it], then indeed Allah is ever, with what you do, Acquainted" (4:135). This is a Medinan verse in Sura al-Nisaʾ, addressed to the believers. What it commands is justice (*qisṭ*); the means it names is honest testimony — not distorting it and not withholding it. It names the pressures that bend testimony: love of self, of family, and a bias for or against someone because they are rich or poor.

**Justice toward enemies (5:8).** "O you who have believed, be persistently standing firm for Allah, witnesses in justice, and do not let the hatred of a people prevent you from being just. Be just; that is nearer to righteousness" (5:8), from Sura al-Maʾida, Medinan. Here the pressure is hatred: even toward people you have reason to dislike, justice is required.

**Justice as a command, with good conduct (16:90).** "Indeed, Allah orders justice and good conduct and giving to relatives and forbids immorality and bad conduct and oppression" (16:90), from Sura al-Nahl, usually counted as Meccan. *Good conduct* here is *iḥsān* (see "Mercy and iḥsān"): justice is the floor, *iḥsān* goes beyond it.

**Judging between people (4:58).** "Indeed, Allah commands you to render trusts to whom they are due and when you judge between people to judge with justice" (4:58). The command is to judges and those in authority, about "people" in general — not only Muslims.

**What 4:135 does and does not say.** It commands justice and honest testimony. It does not command saying every true thing in every situation; Muslim scholars allowed some untruths to protect people (see "Ethics of speech"), and al-Ghazali (*Iḥyāʾ*, Book of the Banes of the Tongue) even required a lie when the truth would shed the blood of a Muslim hiding from an oppressor; al-Nawawi gives the same rule and case in *Riyāḍ al-Ṣāliḥīn* (ch. 261), adding that an indirect answer (*tawriya*) is the more cautious course. Applying it to any innocent person is a modern extension of their example.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**The story of the Makhzumi woman.** ʿAʾisha (ra) reports that the Quraysh "worried about the lady from Bani Makhzum who had committed theft." The Makhzum were a powerful clan of Mecca. People asked who could intercede for her with the Prophet (pbuh), and said: "No one dare to do so except Usama bin Zaid the beloved one to Allah's Messenger." When Usama (ra) spoke to him, the Prophet (pbuh) said: "Do you try to intercede for somebody in a case connected with Allah's Prescribed Punishments?" Then he gave a sermon: "What destroyed the nations preceding you, was that if a noble amongst them stole, they would forgive him, and if a poor person amongst them stole, they would inflict Allah's Legal punishment on him. By Allah, if Fatima, the daughter of Muhammad stole, I would cut off her hand" ({hadith:bukhari:3475}).

**What the story is about.** Its point is equality before the law: the same rule for a noble family as for a poor one, and no special pleading by people close to the ruler. Usama was the Prophet's (pbuh) beloved adopted grandson; Fatima (ra) his own daughter. Naming them is the strongest possible way of saying "no exceptions".

**The punishment itself.** The story involves the classical punishment for theft, cutting off the hand, which the Quran prescribes (5:38). Classical law surrounded it with conditions — a minimum value, proof, and the absence of doubt — but those conditions were not read in a named source for this page (see Gaps). Many Muslim-majority states today do not apply it. Readers who find the punishment itself troubling are right to ask about it; this page's subject is the equality the story teaches, and the question of the punishments belongs on a page of its own.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Ibn al-Qayyim: justice is the goal of the law.** Ibn al-Qayyim (d. 1350), a Hanbali scholar, writes in *al-Ṭuruq al-Ḥukmiyya* (1/13) that God's purpose in sending His law is "to establish justice between His servants and for people to behave fairly", and that "Whichever path leads to justice and fairness is part of the religion and does not contradict it" (English as quoted by Abu Amina Elias; translator not named). The often-quoted line "wherever justice is, there is God's law" is a paraphrase of this passage; its exact wording was not found in this run.

**Al-Shatibi and the purposes of the law.** The current page cites al-Shatibi's *al-Muwāfaqāt*, the classic work on the *maqāṣid* (purposes) of the Sharia; it was not read for this run (see Gaps).

#### fiqh — What the jurists hold
**Testimony for and against relatives.** According to islamicfiqh.net, testimony "by parents in favour of their children, or by children in favour of their parents, is not acceptable according to the majority of scholars", and "a testimony by one spouse in favour of the other is unacceptable" — because of the risk of favouritism. But "a testimony against such close relatives is acceptable, as it cannot be motivated by favouritism." This is 4:135 in practice: you may not be a witness *for* your family, but you must be one *against* them if justice requires. The page attributes a minority view, accepting testimony for parents or children from a witness of good character, to ʿUmar ibn al-Khattab, the judge Shurayh, ʿUmar ibn ʿAbd al-ʿAziz, Ibn al-Mundhir, and "al-Shafiʿi in one of his two views." The source does not name each school's position separately.

**Women and non-Muslims as witnesses.** Classical law did not treat all witnesses equally. In the same source, women were not accepted as witnesses in marriage, divorce and similar non-financial matters; in financial matters two men, or one man and two women, were needed; and in matters mostly known to women (birth, breastfeeding), "the testimony of one woman of probity is accepted." These rules are discussed on the page "Does Islam treat women unequally?". The source read does not address non-Muslim witnesses in general (see Gaps).

#### history — History
**Courts in practice.** Readers ask how Muslim courts actually applied this teaching. No historical study of court records was read for this run, so no examples are given here (see Gaps). Honest examples exist in the scholarly literature, both of judges ruling against the powerful and of the powerful escaping; they should be added from named studies, not from popular stories.

#### research — What research shows
**People obey law they see as fair, mainly because fairness builds trust.** [Moderate.] Walters and Bolger (2019) combined 64 studies (95 samples) published between 1990 and 2018. Fair treatment by authorities (procedural justice) predicted belief that the authorities are legitimate, and legitimacy predicted obeying the law. The direct link from fairness to obeying the law was "relatively weak" and "could not be reliably established in longitudinal datasets"; the results varied a lot between studies. In plain words: fairness matters mainly because it makes people trust the system.

**The Arab Barometer.** The current page cites the Arab Barometer on falling trust in religious leaders. That is a real survey, but it measures trust and religiosity, not whether Muslim societies live by 4:135, so it was not used here.

#### practice — In daily life
**At home.** If your child or sibling is in the wrong in a dispute with someone else, say so — kindly, but clearly. Family loyalty does not include taking the wrong side.

**At work.** Evaluate people by the same standard whether you like them or not (5:8). If you have to give a reference or a testimony, give the true one.

**In disputes.** Before taking a side, ask: would I judge this the same way if the roles were reversed, or if the other person were rich, or poor?

**When the injustice comes from religious authorities.** 4:135 makes no exception for them. Speak through proper channels — a complaint to the institution, a lawyer, or the authorities — and do not let reverence for a title stop you from naming wrong. The first of al-Nawawi's cases where speaking about someone is allowed is complaining of an injustice to someone who can remedy it (see "Ethics of speech").

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you were wronged and no one stood up for you**, the Quran is on your side: it commands people to stand up for justice even against their own families. That others failed you is their failure, not a sign that you did not deserve justice.

**If you are being asked to testify against someone you love**, the verse knows how hard that is; it names exactly that pressure. You can be honest and still be kind: say what is true, and nothing crueller than the truth.

### Questions readers ask next
**How should I think about cutting the hand in the Bukhari 3475 story?** The story's lesson is that the law must be the same for rich and poor. The punishment comes from 5:38; classical law hedged it with conditions that are not covered here, and many Muslim states do not apply it. It deserves its own careful page.

**What does standing firm in justice look like when the injustice comes from religious authorities?** The same as with anyone else: honest testimony and complaint through those who can remedy it. 4:135 makes no exceptions.

**What do "pbuh" and "maqāṣid" mean?** "Peace be upon him", said after the Prophet's name. *Maqāṣid* are the purposes of Islamic law, such as protecting life, faith, mind, family and property.

**Does the law give equal standing to women and non-Muslims as witnesses?** Not in classical law: women's testimony counted differently in some areas (see "Does Islam treat women unequally?"); non-Muslim testimony was not checked for this page.

**Which work of Ibn al-Qayyim says justice is the goal of the law?** *Al-Ṭuruq al-Ḥukmiyya*, 1/13: "Whichever path leads to justice and fairness is part of the religion."

**How do the schools apply 4:135 to testimony about relatives?** The majority reject testimony *for* parents, children or a spouse and accept testimony *against* them.

### Honest limits
Muslim societies, like others, often fail this teaching; this page states the ideal and does not claim it is lived everywhere. Classical rules on witnesses were not equal for men and women. The conditions of the theft punishment, al-Shatibi's work, and historical court records were not read for this run.

## M2 — Mercy and iḥsān

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 21:107, 7:156, 3:159, 16:90.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English), on 7:156, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-156-sora-7.html.
- Hadith Jibril: Sahih Muslim 8 and Sahih al-Bukhari 50, as given by Hadith Answers, https://hadithanswers.com/hadith-jibril/.
- Sahih Muslim 1955a (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/muslim:1955.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 3231 (via hadithunlocked.com), https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:3231.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 3194 (sunnah.com) — "My Mercy overpowers My Anger".

### In brief
God says of Himself: "My mercy encompasses all things" (7:156), and the Prophet (pbuh) was sent "as a mercy to the worlds" (21:107). *Iḥsān* — doing things beautifully, as if you see God — turns mercy from a feeling into a way of acting, down to how an animal is slaughtered.

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Raḥma.** The root *r-ḥ-m* gives *raḥma* (mercy) and *raḥim* (womb). The two names of God at the start of almost every sura, *al-Raḥmān* and *al-Raḥīm*, both come from it; they are usually translated "the Entirely Merciful" and "the Especially Merciful".

**Iḥsān.** From *ḥ-s-n*, "beauty, goodness". *Iḥsān* means doing something well and beautifully. In the Hadith Jibril it is the highest level of religion (below).

#### quran — What the Quran says
**"My mercy encompasses all things" — and the clause after it (7:156).** The verse is a dialogue. Moses (pbuh) prays: "And decree for us in this world [that which is] good and [also] in the Hereafter; indeed, we have turned back to You." God answers: "My punishment — I afflict with it whom I will, but My mercy encompasses all things. So I will decree it [especially] for those who fear Me and give zakah and those who believe in Our verses" (7:156), from the Meccan Sura al-Aʿraf. So the verse makes two claims: mercy reaches everything, and a special, written mercy is for those with *taqwa* (God-consciousness). Ibn Kathir says the first phrase "testifies to His encompassing mercy", and glosses the second: "I will ordain My mercy for them, as a favor and kindness from Me to them." He cites a hadith (from Salman, recorded by Imam Ahmad and Muslim): "Allah… has a hundred mercies", of which He "has kept ninety-nine mercies with Him for the Day of Resurrection." On this reading, everything receives some of God's mercy in this world, and the fullest mercy of the Hereafter is promised to those who turn to Him.

**Gentleness as the source of the Prophet's influence (3:159).** "So by mercy from Allah, [O Muhammad], you were lenient with them. And if you had been rude [in speech] and harsh in heart, they would have disbanded from about you. So pardon them and ask forgiveness for them and consult them in the matter" (3:159). This Medinan verse comes in the passage about the battle of Uhud, where some of the Prophet's (pbuh) own followers had disobeyed orders and fled. The verse tells him to pardon *them*, pray for them, and still consult them.

**"A mercy to the worlds" (21:107).** "And We have not sent you, [O Muhammad], except as a mercy to the worlds" (21:107), Meccan. "The worlds" (*al-ʿālamīn*) is the same word as in "Lord of the worlds": all creation, not only Muslims.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**The definition of iḥsān.** In the Hadith Jibril, the angel Gabriel, appearing as a man, asked the Prophet (pbuh) about Islam, faith and iḥsān. Of iḥsān he said: "That you worship Allah as if you are seeing him, and even though you do not see Him, [you know] He sees you" (Sahih Muslim 8; Sahih al-Bukhari 50, as given by Hadith Answers). Iḥsān is living with the awareness that God sees — and so doing everything as well as you can.

**Iḥsān in everything, even slaughter.** Shaddad ibn Aws (ra) reports: "Verily Allah has enjoined goodness to everything; so when you kill, kill in a good way and when you slaughter, slaughter in a good way. So every one of you should sharpen his knife, and let the slaughtered animal die comfortably" ({hadith:muslim:1955a}). "Goodness" here translates *iḥsān*. The hadith applies it to the moment where mercy seems least present — taking a life — and requires care even there.

**Mercy to enemies: the day of Ṭāʾif.** ʿAʾisha (ra) asked the Prophet (pbuh) whether he had faced a day harder than the battle of Uhud. He said the worst was "the day of ʿAqaba when I presented myself to Ibn ʿAbd-Yalail bin ʿAbd-Kulal and he did not respond to my demand" — the leaders of Ṭāʾif had rejected him. "So I departed, overwhelmed with excessive sorrow." At Qarn al-Thaʿalib he saw Gabriel in a cloud, with the angel of the mountains, who said: "O Muhammad! Order what you wish. If you like, I will let Al-Akh-Shabain (i.e. two mountains) fall on them." He replied: "No but I hope that Allah will let them beget children who will worship Allah Alone, and will worship None besides Him" ({hadith:bukhari:3231}). Offered revenge on people who had just humiliated him, he chose hope for their children.

**Mercy first.** "When Allah completed the creation, He wrote in His Book which is with Him on His Throne, 'My Mercy overpowers My Anger'" ({hadith:bukhari:3194}).

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Readers asked for al-Ghazali and Ibn al-Qayyim on mercy.** Al-Ghazali's *al-Maqṣad al-Asnā* (on the names of God, translated by David Burrell and Nazih Daher) discusses *al-Raḥmān* and *al-Raḥīm*, and what share of each name a human being can have; Ibn al-Qayyim wrote widely on mercy. Neither text was read for this run, so neither is quoted (see Gaps).

#### fiqh — What the jurists hold
**Slaughter (dhabḥ).** Muslim 1955 is the basis of the jurists' rules that the knife be sharp and the animal not be made to suffer needlessly. How each of the four schools details this (for example, not sharpening the knife in front of the animal, not slaughtering one animal in sight of another) was not read in named works for this run (see Gaps).

#### research — What research shows
**No research specifically tests the Islamic teaching on mercy,** and studies of compassion were not read for this run. This page does not claim scientific support.

#### practice — In daily life
**Iḥsān in ordinary work.** Do the task as if it will be seen by the One who sees everything: the report no one will check, the repair no one will inspect, the meal for a guest.

**Mercy to those who wronged you.** You are not required to trust them again (see "Forgiveness and anger"), but you can hope for their good, as the Prophet (pbuh) did at Ṭāʾif.

**Mercy to animals.** Feed them, do not overload them, and never cause needless pain (Muslim 1955).

**For parents.** Correct children without harshness: 3:159 says harshness drives people away. Pardon, pray for them, and consult them about what concerns them.

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you feel beyond God's mercy**, 7:156 says it "encompasses all things", and the hadith says God kept ninety-nine parts of it for the Day of Resurrection. Turning back to God, which is what Moses (pbuh) does in the verse ("we have turned back to You"), is the door the verse names.

**If someone who claims to be religious has been harsh with you**, that harshness contradicts the example the Quran praises (3:159).

### Questions readers ask next
**How does "My mercy encompasses all things" fit with eternal punishment, and with the second half of 7:156?** Ibn Kathir reads the first phrase as mercy reaching everything, and the second as the special, written mercy for those with *taqwa*. Whether mercy will finally reach even those in Hell is a long-debated question, discussed on the page on eternal Hell.

**Was the Prophet (pbuh) merciful to his enemies?** At Ṭāʾif he refused revenge and hoped for their descendants ({hadith:bukhari:3231}); after Uhud he was told to pardon his own fleeing followers (3:159).

**What do iḥsān, al-Raḥmān and zakāh mean?** *Iḥsān*: worshipping as if you see God, and doing everything well. *Al-Raḥmān*: the Entirely Merciful. *Zakāh*: the obligatory annual alms.

**How does mercy fit with harsh punishments and war?** The tradition holds that justice and mercy both belong to God, and that law includes punishments; the pages on the Prophet's wars and on justice discuss these. This page does not claim the tension is easy.

**What is the full Hadith Jibril definition of iḥsān?** "That you worship Allah as if you are seeing him, and even though you do not see Him, [you know] He sees you."

**How do jurists apply Muslim 1955 in slaughter?** It is the basis for requiring a sharp knife and avoiding needless suffering; school-by-school detail was not checked here.

### Honest limits
The relation between God's mercy and punishment is debated, and this page does not settle it. Al-Ghazali's and Ibn al-Qayyim's writings on mercy, and the schools' rules on slaughter, were not read for this run.

## M3 — Parents and family

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 17:23–24, 31:14–15, 4:1, 30:21, 4:36.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 5971 (via hadithunlocked.com, https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:5971).
- Sahih al-Bukhari 3004 (via hadithunlocked.com, https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:3004).
- Sunan al-Nasaʾi 3104 (via hadithunlocked.com, https://hadithunlocked.com/nasai:3104), with grades.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 2587 (via hadithunlocked.com, https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:2587).
- Jamiʿ al-Tirmidhi 3895 (via hadithunlocked.com, https://hadithunlocked.com/tirmidhi:3895), with grades.
- Shaykh Amjad Rasheed, "Is obedience to parents absolute?", trans. Shazia Ahmad, Qibla (via islamqa.org), https://islamqa.org/shafii/qibla-shafii/33386/is-obedience-to-parents-absolute/ — citing Ibn Hajar al-Haytami's *Tuḥfat al-Muḥtāj*.

### In brief
In the Quran, kindness to parents comes straight after the worship of God: "your Lord has decreed that you not worship except Him, and to parents, good treatment" (17:23). It is owed even to parents of another faith, but obedience stops where they command wrong (31:15) — and family duties run in every direction, from parents to children and between spouses.

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Birr and iḥsān.** *Birr al-wālidayn* means goodness and devotion to parents; the Quran's word in 17:23 is *iḥsān*, "good treatment" done beautifully. *ʿUqūq* is the opposite: harming or cutting off parents.

**Ṣilat al-raḥim.** "Joining the womb": keeping ties with relatives. The word *raḥim* (womb) shares its root with *raḥma* (mercy).

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Not even "uff" (17:23–24).** "And your Lord has decreed that you not worship except Him, and to parents, good treatment. Whether one or both of them reach old age [while] with you, say not to them [so much as], 'uff,' and do not repel them but speak to them a noble word. And lower to them the wing of humility out of mercy and say, 'My Lord, have mercy upon them as they brought me up [when I was] small'" (17:23–24), from the Meccan Sura al-Isra. The verse pictures the hardest stage: parents in old age, living with you. "Uff" is a small sound of irritation; even that is ruled out.

**Gratitude, and its limit (31:14–15).** "And We have enjoined upon man [care] for his parents. His mother carried him, [increasing her] in weakness upon weakness, and his weaning is in two years. Be grateful to Me and to your parents… But if they endeavor to make you associate with Me that of which you have no knowledge, do not obey them but accompany them in [this] world with appropriate kindness" (31:14–15), from Sura Luqman, Meccan. In Mecca, many young converts had idolater parents who pressured them to return. The verse says: do not obey them in that, but keep treating them well. The limit of obedience is set by God; the duty of kindness remains.

**Spouses (30:21).** "And of His signs is that He created for you from yourselves mates that you may find tranquillity in them; and He placed between you affection and mercy" (30:21). Marriage is described by what each gives the other: tranquillity, affection (*mawadda*), mercy.

**Wider family (4:1, 4:36).** God is the One "through whom you ask one another, and the wombs" (4:1): you must fear God regarding your ties of kinship. And: "to parents do good, and to relatives, orphans, the needy, the near neighbor…" (4:36).

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Your mother, three times.** A man asked: "O Messenger of Allah! Who is more entitled to be treated with the best companionship by me?" The Prophet (pbuh) answered "your mother" three times, and then "your father" ({hadith:bukhari:5971}, narrated by Abu Hurayra).

**Serving parents before volunteering for battle.** A man asked the Prophet's (pbuh) permission to take part in jihad. He asked: "Are your parents alive?" The man said yes. He said: "Then strive in their service" ({hadith:bukhari:3004}, narrated by ʿAbdullah ibn ʿAmr). In a related report, Jahima asked about going out to fight; the Prophet (pbuh) asked, "Do you have a mother?" and said: "Then stick with her, for Jannah is beneath her feet" (al-Nasaʾi 3104; graded sound in chain by al-Hakim and good-sound by al-Albani; other scholars, including al-Daraqutni and Ibn Hajar, discussed which version of the chain is correct — hadithunlocked.com). The setting in both is voluntary fighting; staying to care for parents was the greater duty.

**What parents owe children: fairness.** Al-Nuʿman ibn Bashir (ra) reports that his father wanted the Prophet (pbuh) to witness a gift to one son only. The Prophet (pbuh) said: "Be afraid of Allah, and be just to your children" ({hadith:bukhari:2587}). Parents must not favour one child over another.

**What spouses owe each other.** ʿAʾisha (ra) reports: "The best of you is the best to his wives, and I am the best of you to my wives" (al-Tirmidhi 3895; al-Tirmidhi: *ḥasan gharīb ṣaḥīḥ*; al-Albani: *ṣaḥīḥ*). The measure of a man's goodness is how he treats the people with the least power to complain about him.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**The maqāṣid and the family.** The current page links family to the *maqṣad* of preserving lineage (*ḥifẓ al-nasl*), one of the classical purposes of the law. No named work was read on this point for this run.

#### fiqh — The limits of obedience
**No obedience in sin.** Shaykh Amjad Rasheed, a contemporary Shafiʿi scholar, writes that "it is not obligatory to obey any of them if they command something that contravenes the shariah", and that obeying in such a case is "not even permissible" — for example, if parents stop a child from praying.

**No obedience in what harms the child.** Citing Ibn Hajar al-Haytami's *Tuḥfat al-Muḥtāj* (the standard late Shafiʿi legal work), he writes that "it is not obligatory to obey one's parents if they request something that entails fitna to the child" — that is, serious harm or trial — and that the child "must be soft in disobeying the parents so that he does not get harmed by them."

**Some exceptions for the child's interest.** He adds that some scholars exempt matters "due to what is best in the child's interests", such as "travelling to attain knowledge, even if recommended". Otherwise, in what is permissible, the child should comply.

**Marriage and career.** Readers ask whether a parent can choose their spouse or career. The source read does not address these directly, and the other schools' positions were not read (see Gaps). On marriage, the page "Does Islam treat women unequally?" and the run 4 research on guardianship (*wilāya*) discuss whose consent is required.

#### research — What research shows
**Family cohesion and adolescent wellbeing.** The current page says family cohesion correlates with adolescent wellbeing. No named study was read for this run, so no numbers are given (see Gaps).

#### practice — In daily life
**With ageing parents.** Speak gently, especially when you are tired; visit or call regularly; let them keep their dignity — ask their opinion even when you will decide yourself.

**When a parent tries to control a decision that is yours.** Listen, take their worry seriously, explain your reasons, and give them time. If the choice is permissible and right for you, you may make it while staying respectful (Rasheed, above). Ask a trusted relative or imam to mediate if needed.

**As a parent.** Be fair between children in gifts and attention ({hadith:bukhari:2587}). Explain rather than only command. Remember that your children's rights over you are as real as yours over them.

**With a spouse.** Kindness in small things: the way you speak, help with the work of the home, patience with faults (Tirmidhi 3895; 30:21).

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If your parent is abusive or controlling**, Islam does not require you to accept abuse. Kindness to parents does not mean obeying commands that harm you (Ibn Hajar, via Rasheed). You can keep distance to stay safe, while not cutting them off with cruelty: a message on holidays, a prayer for them. If you are in danger, contact the police or a domestic-violence service; in many countries a confidential helpline is listed at findahelpline.com.

**If your parents were not good to you**, the command to honour them is not a claim that what they did was right. Many people find it helpful to separate "I honour my parents' place" from "I accept what they did". A counsellor can help you work through this.

**If you are caring for a parent with dementia**, 17:23–24 describes your situation. It is exhausting; asking for help is not a failure of *birr*.

### Questions readers ask next
**Where is the line between kindness and accepting control?** You owe kindness always; you owe obedience in permissible things that do not harm you. A parent cannot command sin, or something that brings serious harm to you (Ibn Hajar, via Rasheed).

**What do parents owe their children?** Fairness between them ({hadith:bukhari:2587}), care, and the same mercy they ask for in old age (17:24).

**What do husbands and wives owe each other?** Tranquillity, affection and mercy (30:21), and kindness as the measure of a good person (Tirmidhi 3895). Detailed rights in law are on the page on marriage.

**What do (ra), (pbuh) and "kith and kin" mean?** (ra): "may God be pleased with him/her", said after a Companion. (pbuh): "peace be upon him", after the Prophet. Kith and kin: friends and relatives.

**What is the context of al-Nasaʾi 3104?** A man asked to go to battle; the Prophet (pbuh) told him to stay with his mother, for "Jannah is beneath her feet". Bukhari 3004 makes the same point about parents in general.

**Can my parents make me divorce, or choose my career?** Not checked in the four schools for this run; the Shafiʿi source read limits obedience to permissible matters that do not harm the child.

### Honest limits
Families often fall short of this ideal, and some parents abuse the religious authority this teaching gives them. The other schools' views on obedience, and research on family cohesion, were not read for this run.

## M4 — Ethics of speech

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 49:11–12, 4:135.
- Sahih Muslim 2589 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/muslim:2589 — definition of backbiting.
- Sahih Muslim 2605a (via hadithunlocked.com), https://hadithunlocked.com/muslim:2605a — permitted untruth.
- Al-Nawawi, "Guarding the Tongue", al-Ibaanah e-book, reposted at AbdurRahman.org (16 April 2015), https://abdurrahman.org/2015/04/16/what-type-of-backbiting-is-permissible-imaam-an-nawawee — the six cases; translator not named, and the source book (*al-Adhkār* or *Riyāḍ al-Ṣāliḥīn*) not stated.
- Al-Ghazali, *The Banes of the Tongue* (*Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*, Book 24), trans. Mohamed Fouad Aresmouk and Abdurrahman Fitzgerald (Fons Vitae, 2024) — introduction, https://fonsvitae.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/02/March-10th-2024-final-0000-Intro-KLH-1.pdf.

### In brief
The Quran forbids mockery, insulting nicknames, suspicion, spying and backbiting, and gives backbiting a striking image: "Would one of you like to eat the flesh of his brother when dead? You would detest it" (49:12). But speaking about someone's wrong is not always forbidden: scholars set out cases — complaining of injustice, warning others of harm — where it is allowed or even required.

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Ghība and buhtān.** *Ghība* (backbiting) is saying about someone, in their absence, what they would dislike — even if it is true. *Buhtān* (slander) is saying what is false. *Namīma* (talebearing) is carrying words between people to cause trouble.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Six forbidden things (49:11–12).** "O you who have believed, let not a people ridicule [another] people; perhaps they may be better than them; nor let women ridicule [other] women… And do not insult one another and do not call each other by [offensive] nicknames… O you who have believed, avoid much [negative] assumption. Indeed, some assumption is sin. And do not spy or backbite each other. Would one of you like to eat the flesh of his brother when dead? You would detest it. And fear Allah; indeed, Allah is Accepting of repentance and Merciful" (49:11–12). Sura al-Hujurat is Medinan and addressed to the believers. The forbidden list moves from public (mockery, nicknames) to private (suspicion, spying, backbiting). The image of eating a dead brother's flesh fits: the person is absent and cannot defend himself.

**Does "your brother" include non-Muslims?** The verse addresses believers and speaks of "his brother". Whether the prohibition extends to non-Muslims was not checked in a named commentary for this run. The general commands of justice (5:8) and good speech apply to everyone, and slander is forbidden against anyone (see Gaps).

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**What backbiting is.** The Prophet (pbuh) asked: "Do you know what is backbiting?" and answered: "Backbiting implies your talking about your brother in a manner which he does not like." Asked what if it is true, he said: "If (that failing) is actually found (in him) what you assert, you in fact backbited him, and if that is not in him it is a slander" ({hadith:muslim:2589}, narrated by Abu Hurayra). So truth is not a defence: true but hurtful gossip is still backbiting.

**When an untruth is allowed.** Umm Kulthum bint ʿUqba (ra) reports the Prophet (pbuh) as saying: "The liar is not the one who reconciles between people: he says good, and conveys good." The narrator Ibn Shihab al-Zuhri then adds: "I have not heard of permission being granted in anything that people call lying except in three cases": war, reconciling people, and what a husband and wife say to each other ({hadith:muslim:2605a}). The list of three is al-Zuhri's, not the Prophet's (pbuh) words. What the Prophet (pbuh) said is narrower: making peace by saying good things is not lying.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Al-Nawawi's six cases where speaking about someone is allowed.** Al-Nawawi (d. 1277), the great Shafiʿi scholar, lists them (as translated in "Guarding the Tongue"):
1. **Complaining of injustice** to a ruler, judge or anyone who can give justice: "Such and such person wronged me."
2. **Seeking help to stop a wrong**, from someone able to stop it — the aim must be ending the wrong.
3. **Asking for a religious ruling** — ideally without naming the person, though naming is allowed; he cites Hind's complaint that her husband Abu Sufyan was stingy, which the Prophet (pbuh) did not forbid.
4. **Warning others of harm** — about an unreliable witness or hadith narrator, or someone entering a marriage, business or other dealings: "It is obligatory on you to mention to that individual what you know." He cites the Prophet's (pbuh) frank description of two suitors to Fatima bint Qays when she asked his advice. Only what the warning needs may be said.
5. **Someone who openly sins**, about the sin they make public — not their other faults.
6. **Identifying someone by a known nickname**, not to demean, and a neutral description is better.

**Al-Ghazali's banes of the tongue.** In Book 24 of the *Iḥyāʾ*, al-Ghazali (d. 1111) lists twenty banes of the tongue, "beginning with the least serious to the most egregious", including excessive speech, lying, backbiting, scandalmongering, obscenity, arguing, disputation and exposing others' faults (translators' introduction, Fons Vitae, 2024). The full list was not available in the introduction read.

#### fiqh — What the jurists hold
**Warning is sometimes required, not just allowed.** In al-Nawawi's fourth case, telling someone what you know about a prospective spouse or partner who asks you is "obligatory". This answers a common worry: warning about an abuser or a fraud is not backbiting when it protects someone and says only what is needed.

#### history — History
**Hadith criticism as permitted speech.** Al-Nawawi's fourth case includes the critique of hadith narrators — the basis of the whole science of judging chains. Scholars openly called narrators liars or weak in memory, and held that this was not forbidden backbiting because it protected the religion from false reports.

#### research — What research shows
**No research specifically on Islamic speech ethics was read for this run.** This page makes no scientific claims.

#### practice — In daily life
**Before speaking about someone, ask three questions.** Would they dislike it? Is there a need — justice, protection, advice? Am I saying only what the need requires?

**When someone backbites in front of you**, change the subject, defend the absent person, or leave.

**Warning about an abuser or a dishonest partner.** Tell the person who needs to know, stick to facts you know, and do not spread it further. If it is a crime, report it to those who can act.

**Online.** The same rules apply to posts, comments and group chats, where words spread faster and further.

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you have been the target of gossip**, the Quran calls what was done to you like eating your flesh. You are allowed to clear your name and to complain to those who can help (al-Nawawi's first case).

**If you have backbitten someone**, the verse ends: "Allah is Accepting of repentance and Merciful" (49:12). Stop, ask God's forgiveness, and, where it will not cause more harm, put things right with the person or speak well of them where you spoke badly.

### Questions readers ask next
**When am I allowed to speak about someone's wrongdoing?** In al-Nawawi's six cases: seeking justice, seeking help to stop a wrong, asking for a ruling, warning someone of harm, speaking of sin they make public, and neutral identification.

**Does "your brother" in 49:12 include non-Muslims?** Not checked in a named commentary for this run. Slander and injustice are forbidden against anyone (5:8).

**Are there times Islam allows lying, for example to make peace?** The Prophet (pbuh) said that someone who reconciles people by saying good is not a liar. The list of three cases (war, peace-making, between spouses) is the narrator al-Zuhri's addition.

**How does this relate to free speech and blasphemy laws?** These ethics are about personal conduct. Modern free-speech and blasphemy laws raise legal and political questions that this page does not cover; Muslim scholars and states disagree about them.

**What are al-Nawawi's six exceptions to ghība?** See above.

**What are al-Ghazali's calamities of the tongue?** Twenty "banes" in Book 24 of the *Iḥyāʾ*, from excessive talk to the most serious, including lying and backbiting.

### Honest limits
The source for al-Nawawi's six cases is an English e-book whose translator and original work were not named. The full list of al-Ghazali's twenty banes was not read. Free speech and blasphemy laws are not discussed. Muslim communities, like others, often fail this teaching — gossip is common — and this page does not claim otherwise.

## M5 — Gratitude and patience

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 14:7, 2:153, 2:155–157, 39:10, 94:5–6.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English), on 14:7, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-7-sora-14.html, and on 39:10, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-10-sora-39.html.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 1283 (sunnah.com, https://sunnah.com/bukhari:1283) and 1302 (via hadithunlocked.com) — the woman at the grave.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 1303 (sunnah.com, https://sunnah.com/bukhari:1303) — the death of the Prophet's son Ibrahim.
- Al-Ghazali, *On Patience and Thankfulness* (*Iḥyāʾ*, Book 32), trans. H. T. Littlejohn (Islamic Texts Society) — publisher's description, https://fonsvitae.com/?p=1903.
- A. Kirca, J. M. Malouf and J. Meynadier, "The Effect of Expressed Gratitude Interventions on Psychological Wellbeing: A Meta-Analysis of Randomised Controlled Studies", *International Journal of Applied Positive Psychology* 8 (2023), pp. 63–86, https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s41042-023-00086-6.pdf.

### In brief
"If you are grateful, I will surely increase you [in favor]" (14:7), and "Allah is with the patient" (2:153). Gratitude is for what is given and patience for what is taken; the Prophet (pbuh) wept when his son died and still said only what pleased God.

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Shukr and ṣabr.** *Shukr* (gratitude) means recognising a gift, its Giver, and using it well. Its opposite is *kufr al-niʿma*, "covering over" a blessing — the same root as *kufr*, unbelief. *Ṣabr* (patience) means holding oneself steady: not despairing, not lashing out, continuing to do right under pressure.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Gratitude and increase (14:7).** "And [remember] when your Lord proclaimed, 'If you are grateful, I will surely increase you [in favor]; but if you deny, indeed, My punishment is severe'" (14:7). In Sura Ibrahim (Meccan), Moses (pbuh) reminds the Children of Israel of this, after their rescue from Pharaoh (14:6). Ibn Kathir explains "increase" as: "if you appreciate My favor on you, I will give you more of it"; denying is being "not thankful for My favors, covering and denying, them"; and the punishment includes "depriving you of the favor and punishing you for being unappreciative of it."

**What "increase" does and does not mean.** The verse does not say how, when or in what form the increase comes, or that a grateful person's life will improve in worldly terms. Many reflective Muslims read it as including increase in the blessing of contentment itself; that is a reading, not the verse's words. And "My punishment is severe" in Ibn Kathir's reading is first the loss of the favour — not a threat aimed at someone who *struggles* to feel grateful, but at the one who denies the gift.

**Patience and prayer (2:153, 2:155–157).** "O you who have believed, seek help through patience and prayer. Indeed, Allah is with the patient" (2:153). Two verses later: "And We will surely test you with something of fear and hunger and a loss of wealth and lives and fruits, but give good tidings to the patient, Who, when disaster strikes them, say, 'Indeed we belong to Allah, and indeed to Him we will return.' Those are the ones upon whom are blessings from their Lord and mercy" (2:155–157). The words of 2:156, *innā li-llāhi wa-innā ilayhi rājiʿūn*, are what Muslims say on hearing of a death.

**Reward "without account" (39:10).** "Say, 'O My servants who have believed, fear your Lord. For those who do good in this world is good, and the earth of Allah is spacious. Indeed, the patient will be given their reward without account'" (39:10), Meccan. Ibn Kathir cites Mujahid on "the earth of Allah is spacious": "So emigrate through it and strive hard and keep away from idols", and al-Awzaʿi on "without account": "Their reward will not be weighed or measured; they will be given an immense reward." The verse was spoken to Meccan believers under persecution.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**The woman at the grave.** Anas (ra) reports that the Prophet (pbuh) "passed by a woman who was weeping beside a grave. He told her to fear Allah and be patient. She said to him, 'Go away, for you have not been afflicted with a calamity like mine.' And she did not recognize him." When she was told who he was, she went to his house — "and there she did not find any guard" — and said, "I did not recognize you." He said: "Verily, the patience is at the first stroke of a calamity" ({hadith:bukhari:1283}; a short version is {hadith:bukhari:1302}). He did not scold her for weeping; he told her that patience counts most at the first shock — and he received her at his door without guards.

**The Prophet (pbuh) wept for his son.** Anas (ra) reports that when the Prophet's (pbuh) infant son Ibrahim was dying, his eyes filled with tears. ʿAbd al-Rahman ibn ʿAwf asked about it, and he said: "O Ibn ʿAuf, this is mercy." Then: "The eyes are shedding tears and the heart is grieved, and we will not say except what pleases our Lord, O Ibrahim! Indeed we are grieved by your separation" ({hadith:bukhari:1303}). Patience in Islam is not the absence of tears; it is not letting grief turn into words against God.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Al-Ghazali's book on patience and gratitude.** Book 32 of al-Ghazali's *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn* is devoted to the two; its first part explains patience and why it is "considered to be half of faith", and its second explains gratitude (publisher's description of Littlejohn's translation). The well-known saying that faith is "half patience and half gratitude" is often attributed to this book or quoted as a hadith; neither the passage nor the hadith's grading was checked for this run (see Gaps).

#### research — What research shows
**Gratitude exercises raise wellbeing a little.** [Moderate; small effect.] Kirca, Malouf and Meynadier (2023) combined 25 randomised trials (about 6,700 participants) of "expressed gratitude" exercises — such as writing thank-you letters. Compared with neutral activities, they improved happiness, life satisfaction and positive feelings slightly (g = 0.22, 95% CI 0.11–0.33). Compared with other active exercises, the difference was small and not statistically significant (g = 0.12, CI −0.02 to 0.26). The review did not measure depression or anxiety.

**What this means.** Practising gratitude seems to help a little, but not much more than other good habits. It is not a cure, and it should not be pressed on someone who is depressed as if it were.

#### practice — In daily life
**Name three gifts at the end of each day**, including small ones, and thank God for them.

**Say 2:156 at the first shock** of bad news, even if your heart is not yet calm. That is the "first stroke" the Prophet (pbuh) spoke of.

**Let yourself grieve.** Tears are mercy ({hadith:bukhari:1303}); patience is in what you say and do, not in pretending not to feel.

**Pair patience with action.** Patience does not mean accepting injustice that can be changed; it means steadiness while you do what can be done.

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you can't feel grateful because your life is getting worse**, you are not the person 14:7 warns. The verse warns those who deny God's gifts, not those who are exhausted or grieving. Start with patience: 2:155–157 promises "blessings… and mercy" to those who hold on through loss.

**If you have been told "be patient" in a way that silenced you**, the Prophet (pbuh) did not silence the grieving woman; he spoke to her gently, and received her when she came to him.

### Questions readers ask next
**How do I stay grateful when 14:7 promises increase but my life is getting worse?** The verse does not say how or when the increase comes. Gratitude for what remains, and patience for what is lost, are both called for; neither requires pretending.

**What does "My punishment is severe" mean for someone who struggles to feel grateful?** Ibn Kathir explains it as losing the favour as a result of denying it. It is aimed at denial, not at someone in pain.

**What is 2:156?** "Indeed we belong to Allah, and indeed to Him we will return" — said at the news of a death or loss.

**What does "without account" in 39:10 mean?** Al-Awzaʿi: the reward "will not be weighed or measured".

**Is "faith is half patience and half gratitude" a hadith?** It is widely quoted; its authenticity was not checked for this run. Al-Ghazali's Book 32 discusses patience as "half of faith".

**What is the full story behind "patience is at the first stroke"?** See the woman at the grave above ({hadith:bukhari:1283}).

### Honest limits
Gratitude research shows small effects. The passage in al-Ghazali on "two halves" and the hadith behind it were not checked. The page's earlier citation of Cregg and Cheavens (2021) was not re-checked.

## M6 — Forgiveness and anger

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 3:134, 42:40, 24:22.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 6114 (via hadithunlocked.com, https://hadithunlocked.com/bukhari:6114).
- Sahih al-Bukhari 6679 (sunnah.com, https://sunnah.com/bukhari:6679) — Abu Bakr, Mistah and 24:22.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 3231 (via hadithunlocked.com) — Ṭāʾif.
- J. Harper et al., "Efficacy of a Workbook to Promote Forgiveness: A Randomized Controlled Trial with University Students", *Journal of Clinical Psychology* (2014), https://core.ac.uk/download/215482224.pdf.

### In brief
The Quran praises those "who restrain anger and who pardon the people" (3:134), and it keeps the right to fair redress: "the retribution for an evil act is an evil one like it, but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation — his reward is [due] from Allah" (42:40). Forgiveness is a gift the wronged person may give; it is never something to be forced out of them.

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**ʿAfw, ṣafḥ, maghfira.** *ʿAfw* is pardoning — letting go of the right to punish. *Ṣafḥ* is "turning the page", overlooking. *Maghfira* is covering a fault, used above all of God. *Kaẓm al-ghayẓ* (3:134) is restraining anger, literally holding it in like a closed water-skin.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Restraining anger and pardoning (3:134).** "Who spend [in the cause of Allah] during ease and hardship and who restrain anger and who pardon the people — and Allah loves the doers of good" (3:134). The verse is Medinan, in the passage after Uhud describing the people of Paradise (3:133). Restraining anger and pardoning are listed together with generosity, as signs of *iḥsān* ("the doers of good").

**Justice first, pardon better (42:40).** "And the retribution for an evil act is an evil one like it, but whoever pardons and makes reconciliation — his reward is [due] from Allah. Indeed, He does not like wrongdoers" (42:40). The verse keeps the wronged person's right to equal redress, and invites — not commands — something better. It ends by naming the wrongdoer, not the person who seeks justice, as the one God does not like.

**Forgiving a relative who wronged your family (24:22).** "And let not those of virtue among you and wealth swear not to give [aid] to their relatives and the needy and the emigrants for the cause of Allah, and let them pardon and overlook. Would you not like that Allah should forgive you?" (24:22). This Medinan verse has a known occasion (below).

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Abu Bakr and Mistah.** When ʿAʾisha (ra) was falsely accused of adultery, one of those who spread the slander was Mistah, a poor relative whom her father Abu Bakr (ra) had been supporting. Abu Bakr swore: "By Allah, I will never give anything (in charity) to Mistah, after what he has said about ʿAisha." Then 24:22 was revealed: "And let not those among you who are good and are wealthy swear not to give…" Abu Bakr said: "Yes, by Allah, I like that Allah should forgive me", and resumed his support, saying he would never withhold it ({hadith:bukhari:6679}, narrated by al-Zuhri from ʿUrwa and others, from ʿAʾisha). The slander was against his own daughter; the forgiveness was costly, and it was asked of him as an invitation — "Would you not like that Allah should forgive you?"

**True strength.** "The strong is not the one who overcomes the people by his strength", the Prophet (pbuh) said, but the one who controls himself when angry ({hadith:bukhari:6114}, narrated by Abu Hurayra; the second half is given here in summary).

**Refusing revenge at Ṭāʾif.** When offered the destruction of the people who had rejected and humiliated him, the Prophet (pbuh) refused and hoped for their descendants ({hadith:bukhari:3231}; see "Mercy and iḥsān").

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Forgiveness is not the same as trust or reconciliation.** In 42:40 the Quran pairs pardon "and reconciliation" as a further good; it does not require a wronged person to return to a relationship that endangers them. Al-Ghazali's book on anger in the *Iḥyāʾ* (Book 25) was not read for this run (see Gaps).

#### fiqh — What the jurists hold
**Redress is a right.** In Islamic criminal law, the victim of bodily harm, or the family of someone killed, may demand legal retaliation (*qiṣāṣ*), accept compensation (*diya*), or pardon. That choice belongs to the victim or family, not to the community. School-by-school details were not read for this run.

#### research — What research shows
**Forgiveness can be learned, and it helps.** [Moderate.] Harper and colleagues (2014) randomly assigned 41 university students to a six-hour forgiveness workbook or a wait-list. Those who did the workbook showed less unforgiveness (Cohen's d = −0.56) and more emotional forgiveness (d = 0.50) than controls; the authors judged it "at least as efficacious as" group therapy using the same REACH model. The sample was small and mostly women. The larger meta-analyses cited on the current page (Wade et al., 2014; a 2022 *Child Development* meta-analysis) were not read in this run.

#### practice — In daily life
**When anger rises**, stop before speaking. Change your position, make ablution, or leave the room. The strong person is the one who controls himself ({hadith:bukhari:6114}).

**Separate the three.** Forgiving (letting go of revenge in your heart), trusting again, and reconciling are different. You can do the first without the other two.

**If you want to forgive but can't yet**, ask God to help you. Abu Bakr's forgiveness came with a question that moved him: "Would you not like that Allah should forgive you?"

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you were abused, you do not have to forgive in order to be a good Muslim, and you never have to go back.** The Quran keeps your right to justice (42:40). Forgiveness may come later, or not; it is yours to give. If anyone uses verses on forgiveness to keep you in danger, they are misusing them. Help is available (see the help box on "Does Islam treat women unequally?" and findahelpline.com).

**If your anger frightens you**, that is worth taking seriously: an anger-management course or counsellor can help, and seeking help is a form of the self-control the Prophet (pbuh) praised.

### Questions readers ask next
**Do I have to forgive someone who hurt me?** No. 42:40 keeps your right to fair redress and invites pardon as a better path; it does not force it.

**Does forgiving mean trusting them again?** No. Forgiveness, trust and reconciliation are different.

**What is the story behind 24:22?** Abu Bakr's oath to stop supporting Mistah after the slander of ʿAʾisha, and his reversal when the verse came ({hadith:bukhari:6679}).

**How do I control anger in the moment?** Stop, change your state, and do not speak until it passes ({hadith:bukhari:6114}).

**Is anger always wrong?** The Quran praises *restraining* anger, not the absence of it. Anger at injustice can lead to seeking justice, which 42:40 allows.

### Honest limits
The larger meta-analyses on forgiveness cited on the current page and al-Ghazali's book on anger were not read for this run. The rules of *qiṣāṣ* are only outlined.

## M7 — Dignity and diversity

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 17:70, 49:13, 5:32, 30:22.
- Musnad Ahmad 23489 (via hadithunlocked.com, https://hadithunlocked.com/ahmad:23489), with grades.
- T. F. Pettigrew and L. R. Tropp, "How does intergroup contact reduce prejudice? Meta-analytic tests of three mediators", *European Journal of Social Psychology* (2008), p. 922 — summarising their 2006 meta-analysis (*Journal of Personality and Social Psychology*), https://courses.washington.edu/pbafhall/563/Readings/pettigrew.pdf.
- Ibn Kathir on 49:13 (as read for run 2).

### In brief
"And We have certainly honored the children of Adam" (17:70): dignity belongs to every human being, not only to believers. Differences of people and tribe are "that you may know one another", and rank before God is by righteousness alone (49:13).

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Karāma.** *Karrama* (17:70) means "to honour, to ennoble". The dignity is given by God to "the children of Adam" — all humans — and so is not earned or lost by belief or behaviour.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Honour for all humans (17:70).** "And We have certainly honored the children of Adam and carried them on the land and sea and provided for them of the good things and preferred them over much of what We have created, with [definite] preference" (17:70), Meccan. The addressee is humanity, named through its common ancestor.

**Difference for knowing one another (49:13).** "O mankind, indeed We have created you from male and female and made you peoples and tribes that you may know one another. Indeed, the most noble of you in the sight of Allah is the most righteous of you" (49:13), Medinan, addressed to all people. Ibn Kathir reads "that you may know one another" as knowing lineage, and adds that honour with God comes "on account of Taqwa, not family lineage".

**Languages and colours as signs (30:22).** "And of His signs is the creation of the heavens and the earth and the diversity of your languages and your colors. Indeed in that are signs for those of knowledge" (30:22). Human diversity is placed alongside the heavens and the earth as a sign of God.

**One life as all humanity (5:32).** "Because of that, We decreed upon the Children of Israel that whoever kills a soul unless for a soul or for corruption [done] in the land — it is as if he had slain mankind entirely. And whoever saves one — it is as if he had saved mankind entirely" (5:32). The verse comes right after the story of Adam's two sons, one of whom killed the other (5:27–31); "Because of that" refers to that first murder. It states a law given to the Children of Israel, which the Quran repeats as a lesson. It contains two exceptions — killing "for a soul" (legal retaliation) and "for corruption in the land" — which should be quoted, not cut, when the verse is cited.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**No superiority except by piety.** In a sermon reported by a Companion, the Prophet (pbuh) said: "there is no virtue of an Arab over a non-Arab, nor of a non-Arab over an Arab, nor of a red-skinned over a black-skinned, nor of a black-skinned over a red-skinned except through piety" (Musnad Ahmad 23489; chain graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by Shuʿayb al-Arnaʾut; al-Haythami: its narrators are those of the *Ṣaḥīḥ*).

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Inviolability through being human.** The current page cites the Hanafi principle that a person is protected (*maʿṣūm*) by being human (*bi-l-ādamiyya*), and the modern *maqāṣid* scholars Ibn ʿAshur and Jasser Auda. None of these works was read for this run (see Gaps).

#### history — History
**The ideal and the record.** Muslim history includes both the Prophet's (pbuh) words above and real racism and ethnic hierarchy — including slavery (see "Slavery") and prejudice against Black Muslims. No historical study was read for this run, so no examples are given; they should be added from named sources.

#### research — What research shows
**Contact between groups reduces prejudice.** [Established.] Pettigrew and Tropp's 2006 meta-analysis covered "515 studies involving a quarter of a million participants in 38 nations" and found an average relationship between contact and lower prejudice of r = .21; "the more rigorous experimental studies yield a stronger mean effect of r = .33" (Pettigrew and Tropp, 2008, p. 922). Contact under Allport's "optimal conditions" — equal status, shared goals, cooperation, support from authorities — typically produced larger reductions. This fits 49:13's "that you may know one another": knowing people across groups reduces contempt.

#### practice — In daily life
**Know people unlike you.** Make friends across ethnicity, language and class: eat together, work together, visit.

**Check your words.** Jokes and nicknames that demean a group are forbidden (49:11; see "Ethics of speech").

**In the mosque.** Notice who is not welcome or not seen — converts, Black Muslims, refugees — and change that.

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you have faced racism, including from Muslims**, the Prophet's (pbuh) words are on your side: no one is above you by colour or origin. That some Muslims fail this teaching is their failure, not a flaw in your worth.

### Questions readers ask next
**Does 17:70 include non-Muslims?** Yes: it speaks of "the children of Adam", all humans.

**What does "that you may know one another" mean?** Ibn Kathir: knowing lineage; honour with God comes by *taqwa*, not lineage. A wider reading — getting to know other peoples — is common today.

**Is 5:32 a universal rule against killing?** It states a law given to the Children of Israel, with two exceptions (retaliation and "corruption in the land"), and the Quran presents it as a lesson.

**Is the Farewell Sermon hadith authentic?** The version in Musnad Ahmad 23489 has a chain graded *ṣaḥīḥ* by al-Arnaʾut.

**Why do Muslim societies still have racism?** Because people fail their ideals. The teaching condemns it; history shows it persisted.

### Honest limits
Muslim history and present societies include racism and slavery; this page states the teaching, not a claim that it was lived. The Hanafi, Ibn ʿAshur and Auda sources were not read for this run.

## M8 — Wealth, charity, fair trade, and the ban on exploitation

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 2:261, 83:1–3, 59:7, 9:34, 2:177, 107:1–3.
- Mufti of the Federal Territory (Malaysia), "Irsyad al-Fatwa Series 38: Zakah for Women's Adornments", https://muftiwp.gov.my/en/artikel/irsyad-fatwa/irsyad-fatwa-umum-cat/1980-irsyad-fatwa-series-38-zakah-for-women-s-adornments.
- IslamOnline, "Zakah on jewelry: scholars' debate", https://fiqh.islamonline.net/en/zakah-on-jewelry-scholars-debate (gold *niṣāb*; Monzer Kahf).
- Mohammad Hashim Kamali, "Jurisprudence of Minorities (*Fiqh al-Aqalliyyāt*) Revisited", *Islam and Civilisational Renewal* 7:1, pp. 120–123, https://www.icrjournal.org/index.php/icr/article/download/287/271/1321 — the 1999 European Council ruling on mortgages.
- AMJA, fatwa 85172, "Student loans", Dr. Main Khalid al-Qudah (8 November 2011), https://www.amjaonline.org/fatwa/en/85172/student-loans.
- BAZNAS (Indonesia), "Zakat and Poverty Alleviation Report BAZNAS RI 2024" — summary, https://puskasbaznas.com/publications/officialnews/2178-zakat-and-poverty-alleviation-report-baznas-ri-2024.

### In brief
Wealth in Islam is a trust: earned fairly, shared — the needy have a right in it, not a favour — and kept moving, "so that it will not be a perpetual distribution among the rich from among you" (59:7). Charity multiplies "like a seed [of grain] which grows seven spikes; in each spike is a hundred grains" (2:261), and cheating in trade is condemned (83:1–3).

### Go deeper
#### meaning — What it means
**Zakāh and ṣadaqa.** *Zakāh* is the obligatory annual alms, usually 2.5% of savings above a threshold (*niṣāb*) held for a lunar year. The root means "purification" and "growth". *Ṣadaqa* is voluntary charity. **Ribā** is usury or interest on loans, forbidden in the Quran (2:275–280).

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Charity that multiplies (2:261).** "The example of those who spend their wealth in the way of Allah is like a seed [of grain] which grows seven spikes; in each spike is a hundred grains. And Allah multiplies [His reward] for whom He wills" (2:261), Medinan.

**Righteousness includes giving (2:177).** "[True] righteousness is [in] one who believes in Allah… and gives wealth, in spite of love for it, to relatives, orphans, the needy, the traveler, those who ask [for help], and for freeing slaves; [and who] establishes prayer and gives zakah" (2:177). Giving "in spite of love for it" is named before prayer.

**Cheating in trade (83:1–3).** "Woe to those who give less [than due], Who, when they take a measure from people, take in full. But if they give by measure or by weight to them, they cause loss" (83:1–3), Meccan.

**Wealth must not circulate only among the rich (59:7).** "And what Allah restored to His Messenger from the people of the towns — it is for Allah and for the Messenger and for [his] near relatives and orphans and the [stranded] traveler — so that it will not be a perpetual distribution among the rich from among you" (59:7). The verse is about *fayʾ*, property gained without fighting (here, from the Banu Nadir), and how it was divided. Using its principle as a general rule against concentration of wealth is a scholarly extension, and should be labelled as one.

**Hoarding (9:34).** "And those who hoard gold and silver and spend it not in the way of Allah — give them tidings of a painful punishment" (9:34), Medinan.

**Neglecting the poor (107:1–3).** "Have you seen the one who denies the Recompense? For that is the one who drives away the orphan And does not encourage the feeding of the poor" (107:1–3), Meccan: denying religion is shown in how one treats the poor.

#### fiqh — What the four schools hold
**Zakāh on worn jewellery.** For the Hanafi school, zakāh "is obligatory when the nisab is sufficient", whether the jewellery alone or together with other wealth reaches it; for the Maliki, Shafiʿi and Hanbali schools, on permissible adornments that are worn, "zakat is not obligatory" (Mufti of the Federal Territory, Malaysia, citing al-Qaradawi). The named works of each school were not read (see Gaps). Monzer Kahf, an economist of Islamic finance, notes that jewellery kept as a store of wealth rather than worn is widely treated as zakatable (IslamOnline).

**The niṣāb.** "The renown nisab for gold is 85 grams" (Mufti of the Federal Territory; IslamOnline). The silver *niṣāb* (commonly given as 595 grams) was not confirmed in a source for this run.

**Mortgages for Muslims in the West.** The European Council for Fatwa and Research, at its fourth session (27–31 October 1999), reaffirmed that interest is forbidden but allowed a Muslim in Europe to take a conventional mortgage if they do not own a house and cannot buy one without a loan, "until" Islamic alternatives are available. Its grounds: a need (*ḥāja*) can be "elevated to a necessity (*ḍarūra*)" when it causes hardship, under the maxim "Necessities make the unlawful lawful", and renting would restrict where Muslims can live — for example near a mosque or school (Kamali). Yusuf al-Qaradawi endorsed the ruling. Many other scholars reject it; their arguments were not read for this run (see Gaps).

**Student loans.** The Assembly of Muslim Jurists of America (AMJA), in a 2011 fatwa by Dr. Main Khalid al-Qudah, holds that "the default is the forbiddance of interest-based loans", but allows a student loan as an "urgent necessity" if all alternatives (scholarships, grants, work-study, subsidised loans) are exhausted, the loan is the only way to study, only the needed amount is borrowed, qualified scholars assess the need, and it is repaid as soon as possible. The necessity "removes the sin even though the ruling of impermissibility remains."

**Pensions.** Rulings on pensions were not read for this run.

#### research — What research shows
**Zakat programmes report people lifted out of poverty, but the evidence is weak.** [Mixed.] Indonesia's national zakat agency BAZNAS reports that in 2024 zakat programmes lifted 1,350,227 people out of poverty, including 721,748 from extreme poverty, measured by an annual "Zakat Impact Assessment survey" against five poverty standards. The summary does not explain how the effect was attributed to zakat, gives no comparison group, and its provider breakdown (adding to about 1,486,920) does not match the total. These are self-reported programme figures, not independent evaluations.

**Giving and the giver's wellbeing.** The current page cites a meta-analysis of kindness experiments (Curry et al., 2018) and prosocial-spending studies (Dunn et al.); neither was re-read for this run.

#### practice — In daily life
**Calculate zakāh each year**: savings, gold and silver above the *niṣāb*, business stock; 2.5%. Ask a local scholar which school's rule on jewellery you follow.

**Check your trade.** Correct weights, honest descriptions, fair prices, no hidden defects (83:1–3).

**Give "in spite of love for it"** (2:177): something you would rather keep, not only what is left over.

**If you have a mortgage, student loan or pension**, find out what the scholars you trust say, look for Islamic alternatives, and avoid the guilt-driven extremes of either ignoring the question or despairing over it.

#### heart — For the one who is struggling
**If you are poor or in debt**, the Quran names you as having a right in the wealth of others, and tells creditors to give time to debtors in hardship (2:280). Zakāh is for you; asking for it is not shameful. Debt advice services in your country can help with practical steps.

**If you already have an interest-bearing loan and feel you are sinning**, you are not alone, and scholars differ on the conditions under which it is excused (the European Council; AMJA). Talk to a scholar you trust about your situation, and do what you can to reduce interest.

### Questions readers ask next
**Do I owe zakāh on my jewellery?** In the Hanafi school, yes, once it reaches the *niṣāb*; in the Maliki, Shafiʿi and Hanbali schools, not on jewellery that is worn.

**What is the *niṣāb*?** 85 grams of gold (or its value). The silver figure was not confirmed here.

**I have a mortgage — am I sinning?** Interest is forbidden. The European Council for Fatwa (1999) allowed a mortgage for a first home in Europe under strict need; others disagree. Ask a scholar about your case.

**Is a student loan allowed?** AMJA (2011) allows it only as a last resort, under strict conditions.

**Does zakāh actually reduce poverty?** Indonesia's agency reports large numbers, but without independent evaluation; the evidence is not strong.

**Does 59:7 forbid inequality?** It is about *fayʾ*; using it as a general economic principle is a scholarly extension.

### Honest limits
The four schools' own works on zakāh, the arguments against the European Council's mortgage ruling, rulings on pensions, and independent evaluations of zakāh were not read. Muslim societies have high inequality, and zakāh collection is often far below its potential; no data on this was read for this run.
