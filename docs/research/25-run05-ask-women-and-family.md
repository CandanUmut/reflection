## Q6 — Does Islam treat women unequally?

### The question, at its strongest
"The Quran gives a son twice a daughter's share of inheritance (4:11). In a debt contract, it asks for two women witnesses where one man would do (2:282). It says 'men are in charge of women' and, if a wife is defiant, allows her husband to 'strike' her (4:34). A man may marry four wives; a woman, one husband. A man can divorce by his word; a woman usually needs a court. Saying 'equal in worth but different in roles' sounds like what every unequal system says about itself. And these rules still shape the lives of millions of women."

### Why people ask it
Many women, and many men, meet these verses through their own experience of patriarchy — in families, courts and mosques — and ask whether the religion itself is the cause. Others meet them through critics who quote them without context.

### Short answer
The Quran affirms that men and women were created "from one soul" (4:1), that they have the same spiritual duties and rewards (33:35; 16:97), and that wives have rights "similar to what is expected of them" (2:228). It also contains rulings that treat men and women differently: inheritance in most cases, testimony in financial contracts, the husband's leadership and maintenance duty (*qiwāma*), and a husband's permission to "strike" in 4:34. Classical scholars tied these differences to the husband's duty to provide, and limited them in various ways; all pre-modern jurists, however, allowed some form of hitting, and modern Muslim scholars disagree on how to read that verse today. Some injustices that women suffer come from custom rather than text; others are real features of classical law. Domestic violence is wrong, and help is listed below.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If you are being hurt at home, your safety comes first, and no verse requires you to stay in danger.** Call your local emergency number if you are in danger now. In the United States, the National Domestic Violence Hotline is 1-800-799-7233 (24 hours, with interpreters); Peaceful Families Project lists Muslim-specific services in the US and Canada, with a few international networks. In the UK, the Muslim Women's Network Helpline is 0800 999 5786 (weekdays, 10am–4pm). Elsewhere, findahelpline.com lists free services by country. The Prophet (pbuh) "never beat anyone with his hand, neither a woman nor a servant" (Muslim 2328), and said of husbands who beat their wives: "They… are not the best among you" (Abu Dawud 2146). Seeking help is not betraying your faith or your family.

**If you are angry at what you have seen done to women in the name of Islam,** your anger is often justified. The question this page asks is narrower: what do the texts and the classical law actually say, and what do Muslim scholars do with them now.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Equal origin, equal worth, equal reward.** "O mankind, fear your Lord, who created you from one soul and created from it its mate and dispersed from both of them many men and women" (4:1), Medinan, addressed to all people. "Indeed, the Muslim men and Muslim women, the believing men and believing women…" (33:35) lists ten virtues for men and women alike and promises both "forgiveness and a great reward". "Whoever does righteousness, whether male or female, while he is a believer - We will surely cause him to live a good life" (16:97), Meccan.

**Mutual rights in marriage.** "They are clothing for you and you are clothing for them" (2:187). "And due to the wives is similar to what is expected of them, according to what is reasonable. But the men have a degree over them [in responsibility and authority]" (2:228), Medinan, in a passage on divorce; the bracketed words are the translator's reading of "a degree". "Live with them in kindness" (4:19).

**Inheritance.** "Allah instructs you concerning your children: for the male, what is equal to the share of two females" (4:11). The same verse gives each parent "a sixth of his estate if he left children", the same for father and mother. 4:12 gives maternal half-siblings equal shares: "for each one of them is a sixth".

**4:34.** "Men are in charge of women by [right of] what Allah has given one over the other and what they spend [for maintenance] from their wealth. So righteous women are devoutly obedient, guarding in [the husband's] absence what Allah would have them guard. But those [wives] from whom you fear arrogance - [first] advise them; [then if they persist], forsake them in bed; and [finally], strike them. But if they obey you [once more], seek no means against them" (4:34), Medinan, addressed to husbands. The bracketed words are the translator's. The key words: *qawwāmūn* ("in charge", from a root meaning to stand up for, maintain), *nushūz* ("arrogance", rebellion, the wife's serious breach of the marriage), and *iḍribūhunna* ("strike them"). The next verse sets out arbitration: "send an arbitrator from his people and an arbitrator from her people" (4:35); and 4:128 addresses the case where the husband is the one at fault.

**2:282 (testimony).** In the long verse on recording debts, the Quran asks for two male witnesses, or one man and two women, so that if one woman errs the other can remind her. The verse is about financial contracts.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught and did
**He never struck a woman.** Aisha (ra) said that he "never beat anyone with his hand, neither a woman nor a servant" ({hadith:muslim:2328}).

**He condemned husbands who did.** Iyas ibn ʿAbdullah reports that the Prophet (pbuh) said: "Do not beat Allah's handmaidens". When ʿUmar (ra) complained that "Women have become emboldened towards their husbands", the Prophet (pbuh) allowed it, then said: "Many women have gone round Muhammad's family complaining against their husbands. They (those husbands who take to beating their wives) are not the best among you" ({hadith:abudawud:2146}; al-Albani: *ṣaḥīḥ*). The words in brackets are the translator's.

**Women as scholars.** Mohammad Akram Nadwi's *al-Muhaddithat* documents women scholars of hadith across Islamic history — among them Umm al-Dardaʾ and Hafsa bint Sirin — and a later Arabic dictionary of his has "around 10,000 entries" (Hashim's review). Women transmitted hadith on the same terms as men.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**What *qiwāma* means.** Ibn Kathir says the man is "responsible for the woman, and he is her maintainer, caretaker and leader who disciplines her if she deviates", giving as reasons men's suitability "for certain tasks" and the duty to pay the dowry and expenses. Shaykh Anas al-Musa (SeekersGuidance, 2024), citing al-Zuhayli's *al-Tafsir al-Munir*, says it means "managing the affairs of the family and home" and "does not entail unjust dominance or oppression". Asma Barlas argues in *"Believing Women" in Islam* that male authority over women conflicts with God's unity and justice, and that the Quran must be read as a whole against patriarchal exegesis (Egyptian Streets review).

**What classical scholars said about "strike".** Ayesha Chaudhry's study of pre-colonial exegesis and law concludes that pre-modern scholars "unanimously interpreted *wadribuhunna* as hitting, striking or beating" and disagreed only on "the procedure and the extent" (Mariam Noor, review in *LUMS Law Journal* 3:1, 2016, pp. 110–118). The range of what was allowed ran "from hitting with a handkerchief to lashing the wife hundred or more times with a whip" (same review). Within that, they set limits:
- Ibn ʿAbbas (ra), asked what "without severity" means, said: "the siwak (tooth-stick) and the like" (via al-Tabari, as reported by *Wikipedia*); Ibn Kathir reports him and al-Hasan al-Basri as requiring "a beating that is not violent" or "not severe", and cites a hadith from Muʿawiya ibn Hayda forbidding "striking her face or cursing her" ({hadith:abudawud:2142}; al-Albani: *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ*).
- **Shafiʿi school:** al-Shafiʿi held that "hitting is permitted, but not hitting is preferable" (as reported by *Wikipedia*); the school classed it as discouraged (*makrūh*) (Chaudhry, per the review).
- **Hanafi:** al-Jassas required "a non-violent blow with siwak" or similar (Wikipedia); the school gave the husband disciplinary power "with minimal legal accountability" (Chaudhry, per the review).
- **Maliki:** made the husband liable for compensation if discipline caused harm (Chaudhry, per the review).
- **ʿAtaʾ ibn Abi Rabah**, a leading Meccan scholar, advised a husband not to beat his wife even if she ignored him, and to express his anger another way (Jonathan Brown, as reported by *Wikipedia*).
Chaudhry's summary of the pre-colonial tradition: "None of them believed it to be unacceptable or forbidden for husbands to hit their wives" (review).

**Modern readings.** Chaudhry identifies four strategies: traditionalists who keep the permission as a last resort; neo-traditionalists for whom "the hitting is meant to be symbolic rather than punitive"; progressives who hold "there is no room for husbands to hit their wives"; and reformists who read the word as "separation" (review). Laleh Bakhtiar's translation renders the verb "go away from them" (Wikipedia).

**Testimony.** Ibn Rushd reports general agreement on 2:282 in financial matters. Abu Hanifa accepted women's testimony in matters such as marriage and divorce; Malik did not; most scholars accepted women's testimony alone in matters men rarely witness, such as childbirth (Wikipedia, "Status of women's testimony"). Ibn al-Qayyim (d. 1350) argued that a second woman is added where forgetting is feared, while "the woman is equal to the man in honesty, trust, and piety"; Ibn Taymiyya said testimony where error is not feared is not "half of a man" (same source). Taha Jabir al-ʿAlwani (1996) argues that the verse's instructions were "recommended or merely instructional (*irshad*) and without legal import", and that such transactions "were not often matters of" women's concern under "the prevailing social conditions" (abstract).

**Hadith criticism.** Hidayet Şefkatli Tuksal, a Turkish theologian, studied gender bias in the hadith literature and "has called for the resolution of contradictory assertions about the rights of women in the hadiths" (Wikipedia). Her book has not yet been consulted.

#### fiqh — What the law holds
**Inheritance: how it actually works.** Dar al-Iftaʾ of Egypt explains that shares are set by three criteria — closeness of kinship, generation (the young inherit more than the old), and financial responsibility — and that "it is in this category alone that shares of inheritance differ according to gender". It counts "only four cases where a woman inherits half the share of a man", many where she inherits equally (for example maternal half-siblings; and, in 4:11 itself, father and mother each take a sixth when the deceased has children), "more than ten cases where a woman inherits more" than a male in a comparable position, and cases where she inherits and he gets nothing. The double share for a son over a daughter is the best-known case and is the one 4:11 names.

**Maintenance.** The classical justification is that the husband must pay the dowry and maintain his wife and children (Ibn Kathir; al-Musa). That a wife's own property and earnings remain hers, and how each school enforces maintenance, have not yet been checked in a named source.

#### reasoning — Equality and sameness
**What "equality vs. sameness" means.** Two people can be equal in worth and rights while having different duties — as parents, or as employer and employee. The classical argument is that the Quran gives men and women equal worth (4:1; 33:35) and a balance of different rights and duties in the family (2:228): the man pays the dowry and maintains the family; inheritance shares follow that balance. *Limit:* this argument depends on the husband actually providing. Where women earn, support families, or are left without support, the balance the classical rules assumed no longer holds — which is exactly the reader's question.

**"What about today, when women earn and support families?"** This is the reader's strongest question. The shares are set out in the Quran's own words (4:11), and Tunisia has debated changing them; but no named scholar on either side of that debate was read, so the arguments are not set out here. This page does not claim the question is settled.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"4:34 permits wife-beating."** *Reply:* the verse places hitting as the last of three steps, after advice and separation in bed, followed by arbitration (4:35); some classical scholars set tight limits (Ibn ʿAbbas: a toothstick; al-Shafiʿi: better not to hit), while others allowed much more (Chaudhry, per Noor's review); the Prophet (pbuh) never hit a woman and condemned those who did (Muslim 2328; Abu Dawud 2146). *Limit:* every pre-modern scholar studied by Chaudhry allowed some hitting, and some allowed far more than a toothstick. Modern readings that forbid it entirely are minority or modern views; they may be right, but they should not be presented as the classical consensus.

**"Equal worth, different roles is how every patriarchy talks."** *Reply:* the Quran's affirmations of equal worth and mutual rights are strong and specific (4:1; 33:35; 2:228), and some differences favour women (the husband's duty to pay the dowry and maintenance). *Limit:* a framework can be internally coherent and still produce injustice when social conditions change; that is the reformers' point.

**"Two women equal one man."** *Reply:* the verse concerns financial contracts; most schools accepted women's sole testimony in some matters, Ibn al-Qayyim held women equal to men "in honesty, trust, and piety", and Ibn Taymiyya held that women's testimony is not always "half of a man". *Limit:* in the areas where classical law applied the rule, it did count women's testimony as less than men's.

#### views — The range of views
- **Traditionalist** — qiwāma as leadership; hitting as a permitted last resort with limits.
- **Neo-traditionalist** — hitting only symbolic; domestic violence condemned.
- **Progressive** (e.g. Barlas) — no male authority over women; no hitting.
- **Reformist** — *iḍribūhunna* means "separate" or "go away" (Bakhtiar).
- **On inheritance today** — fixed shares (majority) vs. adaptation where maintenance no longer operates (some reformers).

### Questions readers ask next
**How does inheritance actually work?** Shares follow kinship, generation and financial duty. The "twice the share" rule applies in a few cases; in many others women inherit equally or more (Dar al-Iftaʾ).

**What does 4:34 permit, and is any hitting allowed?** All pre-modern scholars studied by Chaudhry allowed some hitting; some set tight limits (a toothstick), others allowed far more, and al-Shafiʿi said not hitting is better. Progressives such as Barlas say it is never allowed (Chaudhry, per Noor's review). The Prophet (pbuh) never hit a woman ({hadith:muslim:2328}).

**What does *ḍarb* mean, and why do some say "symbolic"?** It ordinarily means "to strike". Modern neo-traditionalists call the permitted act "symbolic", pointing to Ibn ʿAbbas's toothstick report (Chaudhry, per Noor's review). Other meanings of the root have not yet been checked in a lexicon.

**What does *qiwāma* mean?** Responsibility for, maintenance of, and leadership in the family, tied in the verse to what the husband spends.

**What about divorce, polygyny, hijab and marriage age?** See the pages "Polygyny", "Hijab and coercion" and "Aisha's age at marriage". Divorce rules deserve their own page.

**Is it Islam, or culture?** Some harms women suffer — honour violence, denial of inheritance, forced marriage — contradict the texts. Others, like the inheritance shares, are in the texts. Honest answers keep these apart.

### Honest limits
Some rulings do treat men and women differently, and the classical tradition allowed husbands to hit within limits. How those rulings apply today is contested among Muslim scholars. Several works (Kecia Ali, Tuksal, Barlas, Nadwi) were read here only through reviews or summaries. Divorce law was not covered.

## Q23 — Polygyny

### Sources read

### The question, at its strongest
"A man may marry up to four wives; a woman may have only one husband. The Quran makes it conditional on justice and then says men 'will never be able to be equal' between wives (4:129) — yet the law kept it. The Prophet himself had more than four. Studies find more depression and anxiety among co-wives. And in practice, a second wife is often taken in secret, through an unregistered religious ceremony, leaving her and her children without legal rights."

### Why people ask it
Women fear it, and some live with it. In several countries, including Türkiye, religious-only marriages leave women without legal protection. Men ask whether it is a right or a burden.

### Short answer
The Quran permits a man up to four wives, on a strict condition — "if you fear that you will not be just, then [marry only] one" (4:3) — and then says full equality between wives is impossible (4:129). Many read 4:129 as being about love, which a man cannot control, and 4:3 as being about fair treatment, which he can, and so kept polygyny as a conditional permission (Saheeh's bracket "[in feeling]" reflects this reading; a named classical source for it has not yet been consulted here). Modern reformers such as Muhammad ʿAbduh read the two verses together as a strong restriction that the state may enforce; Tunisia banned polygyny in 1956 on that basis. In the Hanbali school, a wife may make monogamy a binding condition of her marriage contract. Polygyny is rare: about 2% of the world's population lives in polygamous households, and fewer than 1% of Muslim men in countries like Pakistan and Egypt. Research links it to worse mental health for women. Religious-only marriages that bypass the civil law put women at serious risk.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If you are a wife whose husband has taken, or wants to take, another wife,** your feelings are not a lack of faith. The Quran itself admits that full equality between wives is beyond men (4:129), and the Prophet (pbuh) warned that a man who leans toward one wife will come on the Day of Resurrection "with a side hanging down" (Abu Dawud 2133). You have rights to fair treatment and maintenance, and many legal systems give a wife a way to seek divorce for harm through a court; ask a family lawyer what applies to you. If you are in a religious-only marriage, find out now what your legal position is (see below). A family lawyer, a women's rights organisation or the helplines on the page "Does Islam treat women unequally?" can help.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**4:3 and its occasion.** "And if you fear that you will not deal justly with the orphan girls, then marry those that please you of [other] women, two or three or four. But if you fear that you will not be just, then [marry only] one or those your right hand possesses. That is more suitable that you may not incline [to injustice]" (4:3), Medinan. ʿUrwa ibn al-Zubayr asked Aisha (ra) about the verse; she explained: "An Orphan girl used to be under the care of a guardian with whom she shared property. Her guardian, being attracted by her wealth and beauty, would intend to marry her without giving her a just Mahr", and "such guardians were forbidden to do that unless they did justice to their female wards" ({hadith:bukhari:4574}). So the verse begins as a protection for orphan girls against exploitative guardians.

**"Or those your right hand possesses."** The verse also permits, as an alternative to a second wife, relations with a slave woman. That part of the verse belongs to the system discussed on the page "Slavery", and has no application now that slavery is prohibited.

**4:129.** "And you will never be able to be equal [in feeling] between wives, even if you should strive [to do so]. So do not incline completely [toward one] and leave another hanging" (4:129), Medinan. The words "[in feeling]" are the translator's interpretation, reflecting the classical reading.

**The Prophet's (pbuh) marriages.** The Quran permitted him marriages "only for you excluding the [other] believers" (33:50), and later forbade him any further marriages: "Not lawful to you, [O Muhammad], are [any additional] women after [this], nor [is it] for you to exchange them for [other] wives, even if their beauty were to please you" (33:52). So his marriages above four were a special permission, and the Quran itself then closed it. The circumstances of his marriages have not yet been checked.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Unfairness between wives.** Abu Hurayra (ra) reports that the Prophet (pbuh) said: "When a man has two wives and he is inclined to one of them", he "will come on the Day of resurrection with a side hanging down" ({hadith:abudawud:2133}; al-Albani: *ṣaḥīḥ*).

**ʿAli and a second wife.** Al-Miswar ibn Makhrama (ra) reports that the Prophet (pbuh) said from the pulpit that Banu Hisham ibn al-Mughira had asked him to let them marry their daughter to ʿAli: "I don't give permission, and will not give permission… unless Ali bin Abi Talib divorces my daughter", for "Fatima is a part of my body" ({hadith:bukhari:5230}). How classical commentators explained this has not yet been consulted. A *modern reading* takes it to show that a second marriage was not treated as a husband's unconditional right when it would harm the first wife; if the ruling was specific to this case, that general rule does not follow.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**The common reading.** On a widely held reading, the justice required in 4:3 is justice in what a man can control — maintenance, housing, time — and 4:129 is about feelings, which he cannot fully control; then the two verses do not cancel each other. Saheeh's bracket "[in feeling]" reflects this reading. Ibn Kathir and al-Tabari on 4:129 have not yet been consulted, so no classical holder is named.

**ʿAbduh and Riḍā.** Muhammad ʿAbduh (d. 1905) and Rashid Riḍa (d. 1935), in *Tafsir al-Manar*, concluded that polygyny was "an injustice to both women and children" and "should only be allowed in cases of necessity" (as reported by Rasha Dewedar for Musawah, cited in *Wikipedia*; the work is not named there). Their reading takes 4:3 and 4:129 together as showing that the condition is rarely met. *Tafsir al-Manar* itself has not yet been consulted.

**Why men and not women?** The classical answer is lineage: a Hanafi teacher writes that "one of the most primary and central aims (*maqāṣid*) of the Shariʿah is preservation of lineage and the family (*nasab*)" and that "polyandry negates this completely" (Azam). Before modern genetics, a child's father could only be known if a woman had one husband. The same answer adds: "For most people in our times, it may well be impermissible and sinful."

**The critique of the asymmetry.** Critics answer that lineage explains why a woman cannot have two husbands, but not why a man may have four wives; that the classical framework assumes women's dependence on men; and that the stated protections (for widows and orphans) can be met in other ways today. *Limit:* the lineage argument is a reason for the rule as it stands, not an argument that polygyny is good; mainstream scholars do not claim it is an ideal.

#### fiqh — What the four schools hold
**Permission with conditions.** The four schools' detailed rules on polygyny (equal treatment in maintenance, housing and time; whether the first wife's consent is required) have not yet been consulted in named works.

**The monogamy clause.** The schools differ on whether a wife can stipulate in her marriage contract that her husband will not marry another. Abu Hanifa and al-Shafiʿi held the marriage valid but the condition void. The Hanbali jurists, following ʿUmar ibn al-Khattab and Saʿd ibn Abi Waqqas, held the condition binding: if he marries again, the first wife may choose divorce or remain. Ibn Qudama (d. 1223) says of this view: "This was the opinion of the Companions, and we do not know any one who disagreed… who disagreed with them in their lifetime" (IslamOnline). The Maliki position was not covered in the source read. This is one of the most practical tools a woman has where the law allows it.

**Divorce for harm.** The schools' rules on a wife ending a marriage through a judge for harm have not yet been consulted.

#### history — Law and practice today
**How common is it?** [Established.] "Only about 2% of the global population lives in polygamous households", and in most countries under 0.5% (Pew, 2020). In sub-Saharan Africa the figure is 11%, highest in Burkina Faso (36%), Mali (34%) and Nigeria (28%); 25% of Muslims in Africa live in such households. "Fewer than 1% of Muslim men live with more than one spouse in Afghanistan, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Iran and Egypt."

**Legal bans and restrictions.** Türkiye banned polygyny in 1926 on secular grounds (Wikipedia). Tunisia's 1956 Code of Personal Status made a second marriage punishable by "a year's imprisonment and… a fine"; President Bourguiba argued that equal treatment could not be assured, and the Tunisian scholar Muhammad al-Fadil ibn ʿAshur later supported the Code as a possible interpretation of Islam (Wikipedia, citing Perkins). Morocco restricted it in 1958; Iran and Pakistan require the first wife's permission shown to a court (Wikipedia).

**What *ijtihād* means here.** *Ijtihād* is a qualified scholar's independent reasoning from the sources where no text decides the case. The Tunisian argument was an *ijtihād*: since 4:3 forbids a second wife to a man who fears injustice, and 4:129 says full justice is impossible, the state may conclude that the condition is not met and close the door. The majority of classical scholars did not reach that conclusion; it is a modern legal reading.

**Religious-only marriage (*imam nikahı*) in Türkiye.** Turkish law "exclusively recognises civil marriages", and polygamy is a crime under Penal Code article 230 (Musawah, 2019). Women in religious-only marriages lack marital rights, "(such as alimony, participation of acquired property, inheritance in the event of husband's death etc.)"; if a couple is not legally married, "they cannot be legal inheritors" (Musawah). In 2015 the Constitutional Court struck down the penalty for a religious ceremony without civil marriage; the Federation of Turkish Women's Associations warned of "men marrying multiple women, underage marriages" (*Al Jazeera*). In a 2014 survey of Turkish women who married below 18, 77% had religious ceremonies by an imam (Musawah). *Practical advice:* a woman offered a religious-only marriage should know that, in Türkiye and many other countries, it gives her no legal protection; insisting on civil registration is her strongest safeguard.

#### science — What research shows
**Mental health.** [Moderate.] A systematic review of 22 studies found "a more significant prevalence of mental-health issues in polygynous women" than in monogamous women, including higher rates of "somatization, depression, anxiety, hostility, psychoticism and psychiatric disorder", and lower life and marital satisfaction; the author rates this "with moderate confidence", noting the studies' "mixed methodological quality" (Shepard, 2013).

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"If 4:129 says justice is impossible, polygyny is effectively forbidden."** *Reply:* on the common reading, 4:129 is about feelings, not conduct, so the condition of 4:3 can be met. *Limit:* ʿAbduh, Riḍā and the Tunisian legislators read the verses together and concluded otherwise; both are serious readings, and the common one is not the only Islamic one.

**"It protects widows and orphans."** *Reply:* that was the setting of 4:3 (Bukhari 4574). *Limit:* most polygynous marriages today are not about widows or orphans, and the research suggests real harm to women.

**"The Prophet had more than four."** *Reply:* the Quran calls his marriages "only for you" (33:50), and then forbade him further marriages (33:52). *Limit:* critics are not persuaded by an exception granted to the person proclaiming the law; Muslims understand it as part of his particular role.

#### views — The range of views
- **The common reading** — permitted with justice in conduct; 4:129 about feelings (classical holders not checked here).
- **Hanbali** — a monogamy clause in the contract is binding (Ibn Qudama).
- **ʿAbduh and Riḍā** — only in necessity; the state may restrict.
- **Tunisia (1956)** — banned; **Türkiye (1926)** — banned on secular grounds.
- **Indonesia, Pakistan, Iran, Morocco** — allowed with court control or the first wife's permission.

### Questions readers ask next
**Why may men have four wives but women only one husband?** The classical answer is lineage (*nasab*). Critics say this explains only half the rule.

**If full justice is impossible, why was it not banned?** Because on the common reading 4:129 is about love and 4:3 about conduct.

**Why did the Prophet (pbuh) have more than four wives?** A special permission (33:50), later closed (33:52).

**How common is polygyny?** About 2% of people worldwide; under 1% of Muslim men in Afghanistan, Pakistan, Bangladesh, Iran and Egypt (Pew, 2020).

**What is *imam nikahı*?** A religious-only marriage without civil registration. In Türkiye it gives a wife no legal rights to maintenance, property or inheritance.

**Can I put a monogamy clause in my marriage contract?** In the Hanbali school, yes, and it is binding; many modern family laws also allow it.

### Honest limits
"The Quran bans polygyny" and "the Quran encourages polygyny" both distort the text. The asymmetry has a classical explanation (lineage) but no answer that will satisfy every reader. Restricting it is a legitimate *ijtihād*, but it is a modern one. *Tafsir al-Manar*, Mawdudi and the Indonesian study have not yet been consulted.

## Q24 — Hijab and coercion

### The question, at its strongest
"One state forces women to cover and arrests them on the street; Mahsa Amini died in the custody of Iran's morality police in 2022. Another state banned the headscarf from universities and public jobs, as Türkiye did for decades, and France still bans it in schools. Some families force their daughters to wear it; others force them to take it off. In every case, someone else decides what a woman wears. And if the Quran never says 'cover your hair' in so many words, why do scholars say it is obligatory?"

### Why people ask it
Women have been hurt by coercion in both directions — by bans and by mandates, by states and by families. Young Muslim women ask whether covering is really required, and what to do when the pressure is at home.

### Short answer
The four Sunni schools hold that a free woman must cover all her body except her face and hands before men outside her close family, which includes the hair. They base this on 24:31, which tells believing women to draw their *khimār* — a word that in Arabic meant a head covering — over their chests, on 33:59, and on the hadith. A modern minority (for example Muhammad Shahrur) disagrees. Whether to wear it is a question of conscience before God; who may enforce it is a separate question. The Quran says "there shall be no compulsion in [acceptance of] the religion" (2:256); Egypt's Dar al-Iftaʾ says a father must advise but must not force, beat or harm his daughter, because "any compulsion will lead her to be hypocritical". Bans harm women too: France's 2004 school ban reduced Muslim girls' secondary education. This site's firm line is against coercion in either direction.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If your family is pressuring you — to wear it, or to take it off —** you are not alone, and your own relationship with God matters. Practical steps: talk to someone you trust, outside the conflict if possible — a teacher, a counsellor, an imam or a scholar known for gentleness. Learn what the scholars actually say (below): even those who hold covering obligatory say it must not be forced. If pressure turns into threats, violence, or confinement, that is abuse, and the helplines on the page "Does Islam treat women unequally?" can help (in the UK, the Muslim Women's Network Helpline also handles forced marriage and honour-based abuse: 0800 999 5786).

**If you wear it and have been mocked, excluded or banned,** that was a wrong done to you, not a failure on your part.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**24:31.** "And tell the believing women to reduce [some] of their vision and guard their private parts and not expose their adornment except that which [necessarily] appears thereof and to wrap [a portion of] their headcovers over their chests and not expose their adornment except to their husbands, their fathers…" (24:31), from Sura al-Nur, Medinan, addressed to believing women after a parallel command to men (24:30). The word Saheeh translates "headcovers" is *khumur*, plural of *khimār*; "chests" translates *juyūb*, openings at the neck of a garment.

**33:59.** "O Prophet, tell your wives and your daughters and the women of the believers to bring down over themselves [part] of their outer garments. That is more suitable that they will be known and not be abused" (33:59), Medinan. The word for "outer garments" is *jalābīb* (singular *jilbāb*). The verse gives a reason: to be recognised and not harassed.

**What the verses say, and what they do not.** 24:31 assumes that women wore a *khimār* and tells them to draw it over the chest. It does not separately say "cover your hair"; the obligation to cover the hair comes from what *khimār* meant and how the first Muslims understood the verse.

#### sunnah — How the first Muslims understood it
**The women of Medina.** Aisha (ra) said: "May Allah bestow His Mercy on the early emigrant women". When the verse was revealed, "they tore their Murat (woolen dresses or waist-binding clothes or aprons etc.)" and "covered their heads and faces with those torn Muruts" ({hadith:bukhari:4758}). The words in brackets are the translator's.

**The word *khimār*.** Ibn Kathir defines it as "something that covers, and is what is used to cover the head". Akram Nadwi argues that Arabic dictionaries and pre- and post-Islamic poetry use it for a head covering, that hadith speak of wiping over the *khimār* in ablution — so that, in his words, "The khimar in both hadiths cannot mean the covering of the chest" — and that a narration from Aisha ({hadith:abudawud:641}; al-Albani: *ṣaḥīḥ*) says God does not accept a grown woman's prayer without a *khimār*. He reports that scholars "unanimously agreed on the obligation for a woman to cover her head during prayer" (Nadwi, 2024).

#### fiqh — What the four schools hold
**The hair: obligatory in the four Sunni schools.** The school texts below make all of a free woman's body *ʿawra* (to be covered) before non-relatives except the face and hands, which includes the hair (IslamOnline). Nadwi's report of unanimous agreement concerns covering the head in prayer. The Jaʿfari (Twelver Shiʿi) position has not yet been checked.

**The face and hands: the schools differ.** Ibn Kathir reports that Ibn Masʿud (ra) read "except that which [necessarily] appears thereof" as "such as clothes and outer garments"; Ibn ʿAbbas (ra) read it as the face and hands (IslamOnline). According to the texts quoted by IslamOnline:
- **Hanafi** (*al-Ikhtiyar*): one may look at a free woman's "face and hands once there is no fear of sin"; Abu Hanifa also excluded the feet.
- **Maliki** (al-Dardir, *Aqrab al-Masalik*): her *ʿawra* (what must be covered) before non-relatives is "all her bodily parts save for her face and hands".
- **Shafiʿi** (al-Shirazi, *al-Muhadhdhab*): "all her body is ʿAwrah save for her face and hands"; al-Nawawi is cited on the arms "to the two elbows". Later Shafiʿi views on the face veil have not yet been checked.
- **Hanbali** (Ibn Qudama, *al-Mughni*): she may uncover her face in prayer; "According to Imam Ahmad, niqab… is an obligation" (IslamOnline).
So the face veil (*niqāb*) is obligatory in a view reported from Imam Ahmad and not required in the Hanafi and Maliki schools; the hair is required in all four.

**Not covering is not unbelief.** In Sunni theology, a Muslim who fails to fulfil an obligation she still accepts does not leave Islam; no named source for this specific case was read here.

#### scholars — The minority argument
**Muhammad Shahrur** (Syria, d. 2019) read the Quran through a "theory of limits" (*ḥudūd*): God sets a minimum and a maximum, and Muslims may act anywhere between. For women's dress, he took the minimum to be covering *al-juyūb* — which he read as regions of the body such as the area of the breast and under the armpits — and the lower intimate area, and the maximum to be covering everything except the face and palms (Khikmatiar, 2019, abstract, paraphrased).

**Hüseyin Atay** (Türkiye) treats the Quran as the only unaltered source and critically reviews inherited scholarship (Atçeken, 2008).

**Assessment.** The minority view relies on reading *khimār* in a general sense and on setting aside the hadith and the understanding of the first generation. The majority relies on the meaning of the word, the report of the Medinan women (Bukhari 4758), and the four schools' texts. Presenting the two as equally weighted would be misleading; presenting the minority as non-existent would also be untrue.

#### reasoning — Three separate questions
**1. What does God require?** On the majority view, covering the hair. This is a matter between a woman and God.

**2. Who may enforce it, and how?** 2:256 was revealed, according to Ibn ʿAbbas (via Abu Dawud and al-Nasaʾi), when some Medinan families whose children had been raised Jewish wanted to keep them from leaving with the Banu Nadir; the verse forbade forcing them into Islam (Wikipedia, "Al-Baqarah 256"). Its direct subject is conversion. Ibn Taymiyya is quoted as saying "Most of the salaf" considered it "neither specific nor abrogated", and *Wikipedia*, citing Jonathan Brown (*Misquoting Muhammad*, p. 186), calls it as absolute and universal a statement as one finds ({hadith:abudawud:2682} for the occasion; al-Albani: *ṣaḥīḥ*). Classical law also had *ḥisba*, the duty to enjoin right and forbid wrong; how far it was used to enforce dress has not yet been checked. The modern argument, made by Dar al-Iftaʾ in its fatwa, is practical and moral as well as textual: a father "must, to the best of his ability, persuade his daughter", "must refrain from beating or harming her", and must keep supporting her, because "any compulsion will lead her to be hypocritical of religion and insincere". Worship done under force is not what the obligation asks for. *Limit:* 2:256 is about conversion; applying it to enforcement among Muslims is an extension, supported by the verse's general wording and by Dar al-Iftaʾ's reasoning, but not the only classical position.

**3. What does a ban do?** It forces women to act against their conscience in the other direction. Bans deny women education and work they have a right to.

#### history — History
**Türkiye.** A 1982 regulation required female civil servants' heads to be uncovered; the first widespread university ban came in 1984 and was enforced much more strictly after the 1997 military memorandum. In 1999, the MP Merve Kavakçı was prevented from taking her oath in parliament; in 2000, Nuray Bezirgan, who wore a headscarf to her exams in 2000, was later sentenced to six months for "obstructing the education of others". The European Court of Human Rights upheld the ban in 2004 and 2006. A 2008 amendment to lift it was annulled by the Constitutional Court; the ban was lifted in universities and the civil service on 8 October 2013, in high schools in 2014, and in the judiciary, police and military in 2015–2017. Headscarved women were disciplined or lost public jobs, and found it harder to become teachers, judges, lawyers or doctors in public service (Wikipedia, "Headscarf rights in Turkey").

**France.** The "2004 French headscarf ban" applied to state schools (Abdelgadir and Fouka). Abdelgadir and Fouka (2020) found that "the law reduces the secondary educational attainment of Muslim girls", affects "their trajectory in the labor market and family composition in the long run", "operates through increased perceptions of discrimination", and "strengthens both national and religious identities". [Moderate.]

**Iran.** In 1979 Khomeini said women should observe Islamic dress; hijab became mandatory in public offices in 1980 and for all women in 1983, with imprisonment of ten days to two months or fines under Penal Code article 638. In 2022, Mahsa Amini, 22, "was killed in custody of the Guidance Patrol in Tehran", sparking nationwide protests (Wikipedia).

#### science — What research says about forced religion
**Pressure and inner commitment.** [Moderate.] Ryan, Rigby and King (1993) distinguished two ways people take on religious beliefs: *introjection*, "characterized by self- and other-approval-based pressures", and *identification*, "adoption of beliefs as personal values", "characterized by greater volition"; in four samples, these related differently to mental health. The samples were Christian, and the abstract read does not give the specific outcomes; the finding supports, but does not prove, the claim that coercion damages faith. Dar al-Iftaʾ's warning about hypocrisy makes the same point from within Islamic ethics.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"2:256 is about conversion, not about Muslims' obligations."** *Reply:* correct as to its occasion; the argument against coercion within the family rests also on the purpose of worship and on scholars such as Dar al-Iftaʾ. *Limit:* classical law had the institution of *ḥisba*, and the classical position on enforcement was not fully checked here.

**"If covering is obligatory, a Muslim state should enforce it."** *Reply:* Dar al-Iftaʾ's reasoning — compulsion produces hypocrisy — applies to the state as much as to a father, and Iran's experience shows enforcement producing resentment and violence. *Limit:* this is a political-ethical argument; classical jurists did not all draw the line here.

**"The minority is right: the Quran never says 'hair'."** *Reply:* the word *khimār* meant a head covering, and the first Muslim women understood the verse that way (Bukhari 4758). *Limit:* the minority replies that the obligation should be stated explicitly if it were binding; the majority answers that word meaning and early practice are how all Quranic terms are understood.

#### views — The range of views
- **All four Sunni schools** — covering the hair obligatory; face and hands differ by school.
- **A view reported from Imam Ahmad** — face veil obligatory.
- **Shahrur** — covering the hair above the minimum (minority).
- **Dar al-Iftaʾ (Egypt)** — obligatory, but parents must persuade, not force.

### Questions readers ask next
**What exactly must be covered, and how do the schools differ?** All schools: the hair and body except face and hands. The Hanafi and Maliki schools do not require the face to be covered; a view reported from Imam Ahmad does.

**How does *khimār* get to "hair"?** It was the Arabic word for a head covering (Ibn Kathir; Nadwi). 24:31 tells women to extend it over the chest.

**What should I do if my family pressures me?** Seek a trusted, calm person; learn the scholars' view that compulsion is wrong; and if pressure becomes abuse, get help.

**Why did Türkiye ban the headscarf?** As part of a strict state secularism, enforced through the 1980s and especially after 1997; lifted in 2013–2017.

**Is a woman who does not cover still a Muslim?** Yes. Failing an obligation one accepts does not take a person out of Islam in Sunni theology.

**What is the minority argument in full?** Shahrur's theory of minimum and maximum limits, and the claim that *khimār* means any cover; see above.

### Honest limits
The majority view that covering the hair is obligatory is strong and old; presenting the minority as equal would mislead. Applying 2:256 to enforcement among Muslims is an extension, supported by modern scholars but not by every classical one. The research on coercion and faith is suggestive, not conclusive. Atay's own text, Apaydın's and Karaman's articles, al-Qurtubi and Leila Ahmed have not yet been consulted.
