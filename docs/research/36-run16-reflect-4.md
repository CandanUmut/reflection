## S26 — Darkness in the deep sea

### Sources read

### In brief
24:40 is a parable, not a description of the ocean: the deeds of those who reject faith are "like darknesses within an unfathomable sea", layer on layer, so dark that a man "can hardly see" his own hand — and the verse ends with its point, that light is God's gift.

### Verse note
"Or [they are] like darknesses within an unfathomable sea which is covered by waves, upon which are waves, over which are clouds - darknesses, some of them upon others. When one puts out his hand [therein], he can hardly see it. And he to whom Allah has not granted light - for him there is no light" (24:40), Medinan, in Sura al-Nūr ("The Light").

- **Setting.** 24:40 is the second of two parables. The first (24:39, not quoted here because it was not in the verified data used) is the parable of the mirage; both describe those who reject faith, as al-Jalālayn's gloss on 24:40 makes explicit. Both come right after the Light Verse (24:35): "Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth. The example of His light is like a niche within which is a lamp … Allah guides to His light whom He wills" (24:35). The sura sets the believer's heart, lit from within, against a heart sunk in layered dark.
- **Whom it describes.** The subject of "Or [they are]" carries over from 24:39: those who reject faith, or their deeds (al-Ṭabarī; al-Jalālayn). It is not addressed to sailors.
- **Key words.** *Baḥr lujjī* ("an unfathomable sea"): from *lujja*, the deep, vast body of water; al-Qurṭubī explains it as "the sea whose bottom cannot be reached" (below). *Mawj min fawqihi mawj* ("waves, upon which are waves"). *Ẓulumāt baʿḍuhā fawqa baʿḍ* ("darknesses, some of them upon others"). *Lam yakad yarāhā* ("he can hardly see it"): Arabic *lam yakad* means "barely" or "all but not".

### Classical tafsir
**Al-Ṭabarī** (d. 923, Baghdad; the great early commentator who collected the reports of the first generations). For him the darkness stands for what the disbelievers do: "ومثل أعمال هؤلاء الكفار" ("the likeness of the deeds of these disbelievers"); "فجعل الظلمات مثلا لأعمالهم" ("He made the darknesses a likeness for their deeds"). On the last line, the one with no light is "من لم يرزقه الله إيمانا وهدى من الضلالة" ("whoever God does not grant faith, and guidance out of misguidance").

**Ibn Kathir** (d. 1373, Damascus; his commentary explains the Quran by the Quran, hadith and early reports). In the abridged English: "This is the parable of the heart of the disbeliever whose ignorance is simple", one "who merely follows and does not know the true nature of the one whom he follows". The hand is unseen "because it is so intensely dark." On the last line: "One whom Allah does not guide is ignorant and doomed". In the Arabic text he reports the Companion Ubayy ibn Kaʿb (ra) that such a person "يتقلب في خمسة من الظلم" ("moves about within five darknesses"): his speech, his deeds, his coming in, his going out, and his end in the Fire. He closes with a prayer that God "يجعل في قلوبنا نورا، وعن أيماننا نورا، وعن شمائلنا نورا" ("place light in our hearts, light on our right and light on our left").

**Al-Qurṭubī** (d. 1273, Córdoba and Egypt; a Maliki jurist whose commentary gathers earlier views). He calls it "another parable for the disbelievers" ("ضرب تعالى مثلا آخر للكفار"). He gives the physical layers first — "ظلمة السحاب، وظلمة الموج، وظلمة الليل، وظلمة البحر" ("the darkness of the clouds, of the waves, of the night and of the sea") — then a reported reading: "وقيل : أراد بالظلمات أعمال الكافر ، وبالبحر اللجي قلبه" ("it was said: by the darknesses He meant the disbeliever's deeds, and by the deep sea his heart"). He also reports Ubayy's five darknesses. On the last line he cites Ibn ʿAbbas: "من لم يجعل الله له دينا فما له من دين" ("whoever God does not grant religion has no religion"), and a second reading about the next world: he "لم يهتد إلى الجنة" ("will not be guided to Paradise").

**Al-Jalālayn** (15th-century Egypt; a short, widely taught commentary): "those who disbelieve their evil deeds are as the manifold darkness on a deep sea"; "he whom God does not guide cannot be guided."

**Tanwīr al-Miqbās** (a commentary attributed to Ibn ʿAbbas): "the similitude of denial in the heart of the disbeliever is like darkness in the depth of a very deep sea", "because Allah sealed the disbelievers hearts and hearings and sights." It reads "light" as knowledge: "he has no knowledge in the Hereafter".

**On the Light Verse (24:35).** Ibn Kathir reports Ibn ʿAbbas that "Light" here "means, the Guide of the inhabitants of the heavens and the earth", and Ubayy ibn Kaʿb that the niche and lamp are "the likeness of the heart of the believer": "The lamp is the light, and this refers to the Qur'an and the faith that is in his heart." On "Allah guides to His light whom He wills", Ibn Kathir: it "means, Allah shows the way to the ones whom He chooses". Al-Jalālayn: "God guides to His Light that is to the religion of Islam whom He will."

**Where they differ.** They agree that the verse is a parable about those without faith. They differ on what the darkness stands for: their *deeds* (al-Ṭabarī; al-Jalālayn), the *heart* of one who follows blindly (Ibn Kathir), or *denial* sealed in the heart (*Tanwīr*); al-Qurṭubī reports the physical layers first and the deeds-and-heart reading as a "said" view. On the last line, "light" is faith and guidance (al-Ṭabarī; Ibn Kathir), religion (Ibn ʿAbbas via al-Qurṭubī), Paradise in the next world (al-Qurṭubī's second reading), or knowledge (*Tanwīr*). None of them reads the waves as hidden layers inside the sea.

### How it works
Each point is tied to the verse's picture: "darknesses within an unfathomable sea … he can hardly see it".
- **Where sunlight ends.** [Established] NOAA: "there is rarely any significant light beyond 200 meters (656 feet)". From 200 to 1,000 m "is usually referred to as the 'twilight' zone" (dim light); the aphotic zone, with no sunlight, "exists in depths below 1,000 meters". LibreTexts adds that light "can be detected about 1,000 meters in the ocean".
- **Colours go one by one.** [Established] Seawater absorbs red light first and blue last: "Blue light penetrates best", then green, yellow, and orange and red last; "At 100 meters, red light does not penetrate", which is why a red deep-sea fish "appears blackish because there is no red light to reflect at that depth" (NOAA Ocean Exploration).
- **It depends on how clear the water is.** [Established] The lit layer (the euphotic zone) is defined as the depth "where radiation is degraded down to 1% of its surface strength". "Typical euphotic depths vary from only a few centimetres in highly turbid eutrophic lakes, to around 200 meters in the open ocean"; in murky water it "can be only fifty feet (fifteen meters) deep" (*Wikipedia*, "Photic zone"). So the 200 m and 1,000 m figures are for clear open ocean; in muddy or plankton-rich water, darkness comes much sooner.
- **Light made in the dark.** Many sea animals make their own light (bioluminescence). In 17 years of video from remotely operated vehicles off the California coast, with "More than 350,000 observations" from the surface "down to 3,900 m depth", Martini and Haddock found that "76% of the observed individuals have bioluminescence capability" (*Scientific Reports*, 2017). [Moderate] — one region and the animals the cameras saw, not a global census.
- **Waves inside the sea.** [Established] Where layers of different density meet (warmer or fresher water over colder or saltier water), waves can travel along the boundary below the surface. In the South China Sea such internal waves "can top 200 meters in height" (Alford et al., *Nature*, 2015, via *Physics Today*). They were first noticed through "dead water": in 1893 Fridtjof Nansen's ship "was slowed by a mysterious force", and in 1904 Vagn Walfrid Ekman linked the drag to "waves formed under the surface at the interface between the salt water and freshwater layers" (CNRS, 2020).

### Points of awe
- Below about 1,000 m no sunlight arrives at all (NOAA), yet in one long survey about three in four animals seen could make their own light (Martini & Haddock).
- Red is the first colour the sea takes away; by 100 m it is gone, and a red fish looks black (NOAA).
- In clear ocean the lit layer can reach about 200 m; in murky water, as little as 15 m (*Wikipedia*).

### The reflection
The verse asks us to picture a place with no light left: night above, clouds over the night, waves over waves, and under them the deep. You stretch out your own hand, the nearest thing you have, and you can hardly see it.

The commentators did not read this as a lesson about the sea. They read it as a picture of a life. Al-Ṭabarī saw deeds done without faith; Ibn Kathir saw a heart that follows without knowing what it follows; Ubayy ibn Kaʿb named five darknesses, from a person's words to his final end.

The verse does not stop in the dark. Its last line is about light: "he to whom Allah has not granted light - for him there is no light." Sura al-Nūr has already said where light comes from: "Allah is the Light of the heavens and the earth" (24:35). Light is given, and that is not a reason for despair. It tells us whom to ask. Ibn Kathir ends his comment with exactly that: a prayer for light in the heart, on the right and on the left. The Quran also promises: "And those who strive for Us - We will surely guide them to Our ways" (29:69).

Science adds one quiet image. Even where no sunlight reaches, most of the creatures that drift through the deep carry a light of their own. The reflection — ours, not the verse's — is that no darkness is so deep that God cannot place a light in it.

### Honest notes
- **The popular claim.** A widely shared article says the verse describes deep-sea darkness and internal waves: "Scientists have recently discovered this darkness", and, quoting an oceanography textbook (Gross), "Internal waves cannot be seen by the human eye" (IslamReligion, article 217). The argument is that no one in the 7th century could have known about darkness below about 200 m or about waves inside the sea.
- **Weighing it.** (1) The verse is a parable, introduced by "Or [they are] like", in a passage about disbelievers' deeds; every commentator read here takes it that way. (2) None of them reads "waves, upon which are waves" as waves *inside* the sea. Al-Qurṭubī's layers are cloud, wave, night and sea — a storm at night, seen from above. (3) That deep water grows dark is something divers and sailors can notice; a critic makes this point (WikiIslam). (4) "Scientists have recently discovered this darkness" is not accurate: what modern science added is measurement — the depths, the colours, the role of clarity — not the bare fact that the deep is dark. (5) Internal waves fit the words "waves, upon which are waves" only on a modern reading the classical commentators did not give. It can be offered as a reflection, labelled as one, never as a prediction.
- **Fatalism.** The last line can sound as if God withholds light and then blames people for being in the dark. The commentators read here do not draw that conclusion. They read "light" as faith and guidance, and they answer by asking for it (Ibn Kathir's prayer). The wider question of God's will and human choice is on the Ask page "If everything is written, how am I free?"; this page does not settle it.
- **Numbers.** The 200 m and 1,000 m figures are for clear open ocean. The 76% figure comes from one region off California.

### Questions readers ask next
**Does the verse describe internal waves?** The classical commentators read it as a parable of the heart and of deeds, with surface waves, night and cloud. Internal waves are real (some exceed 200 m), but linking them to the verse is a modern reading; see Honest notes.

**Which commentator said what?** Al-Ṭabarī: the darkness is the disbelievers' deeds. Ibn Kathir: the heart of one who follows blindly, with Ubayy's five darknesses. Al-Qurṭubī: cloud, wave, night and sea, and a "said" view that the sea is the heart. *Tanwīr*: denial in a sealed heart.

**Is it God's fault if someone is in darkness?** The verse says light is God's gift. The commentators' response is to ask for it: Ibn Kathir closes with a prayer for light. The Quran also promises guidance to those who strive (29:69). The deeper question of predestination is on the Ask page "If everything is written, how am I free?".

**How deep does light reach?** In clear open ocean, useful light reaches about 200 m and the last faint light about 1,000 m (NOAA); in murky water the lit layer can be as shallow as 15 m (*Wikipedia*).

**Is it completely dark down there?** Without sunlight, yes, below about 1,000 m. But many animals make their own light: 76% of those seen in a long survey off California (Martini & Haddock, 2017).

## S27 — Gardens and fruits watered by one water

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via `src/data/quran.json` — 13:3, 13:4.
- Ibn Kathir (abridged English), *al-Jalālayn* and *Tanwīr al-Miqbās*, on 13:4, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-4-sora-13.html.
- Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī 3118, via hadithunlocked, https://hadithunlocked.com/tirmidhi:3118.
- CORDIS (European Commission), "Productivity and quality of grape vary according to plot of vineyard under cultivation", on Olatz Unamunzaga's study at Neiker-Tecnalia, https://cordis.europa.eu/article/id/119888-productivity-and-quality-of-grape-vary-according-to-plot-of-vineyard-under-cultivation/pl.
- FAO, *Date palm cultivation* (Plant Production and Protection Paper 156), chapter on propagation, https://www.fao.org/4/Y4360E/y4360e09.htm.

### In brief
13:4 points to plots of land side by side, vines and palms, "watered with one water", yet God makes "some of them exceed others in [quality of] fruit" — and calls this a sign "for a people who reason".

### Verse note
"And within the land are neighboring plots and gardens of grapevines and crops and palm trees, [growing] several from a root or otherwise, watered with one water; but We make some of them exceed others in [quality of] fruit. Indeed in that are signs for a people who reason" (13:4). Sura al-Raʿd is Medinan in the Tanzil classification.

- **Setting.** The verse before speaks of the spread-out earth, mountains, rivers, fruits in pairs, and night covering day, ending "Indeed in that are signs for a people who give thought" (13:3). 13:4 narrows the view from the whole earth to a single landscape of neighbouring fields.
- **Whom it addresses.** "A people who reason" — anyone willing to think about what they see.
- **Key words.** *Qiṭaʿun mutajāwirāt* ("neighboring plots"). *Ṣinwān* ("several from a root"): plants growing as several trunks from one root; *ghayru ṣinwān* ("or otherwise"): single trunks. *Al-ukul* ("[quality of] fruit"; literally "what is eaten"). The bracketed words are the translator's.

### Classical tafsir
**Ibn Kathir.** The plots are "next to each other, some of them are fertile and produce what benefits people, while others are dead, salty and do not produce anything." *Ṣinwān* "means, growing into two or three from a single stem, such as figs, pomegranate and dates", and *ghayru ṣinwān* "means, having one stem for every tree, as is the case with most plants." The point: "all these variances and complex diversities are watered by the same water", and "Therefore, there are differences between plants and fruits with regards to shape, color, taste, scent, blossoms" and the shape of their leaves.

**A hadith Ibn Kathir cites.** Abu Hurayra (ra) reports that the Prophet (pbuh), on "some of them We make more excellent than others to eat", named kinds of dates: "The Daqal, the Persian …, the sweet, the bitter" ({hadith:tirmidhi:3118}). Al-Tirmidhi graded it *ḥasan gharīb*; al-Albani *ḥasan*; Zubayr ʿAlī Zaʾī *ḍaʿīf* (weak), and one narrator in the chain, Sayf ibn Muḥammad, was accused of lying (hadithunlocked). Because its grade is disputed, nothing on this page rests on it.

**Al-Jalālayn.** The plots are "some good some briny some of little yield and some fruitful"; "sinwān is the plural of sinw which are date-palms belonging to the same root but with many offshoots"; they are watered "by the same source of water".

**Tanwīr al-Miqbās** pictures "a bad, swampy land close to a good, fertile land", "watered with the rain or water from rivers."

**Where they differ.** Ibn Kathir and al-Jalālayn read the neighbouring plots as soils of different quality side by side; all three stress that the water is the same. They differ on *ṣinwān*: al-Jalālayn limits it to date palms; Ibn Kathir includes figs and pomegranates. Neither explains *why* the fruits differ beyond God's will; the verse leaves the reader to reason.

### How it works
Each point is tied to "neighboring plots … watered with one water; but … some of them exceed others".
- **Neighbouring soils really do differ.** [Established, for this case] In one eight-hectare vineyard at Oion (Araba-Álava, Basque Country), a team led by Olatz Unamunzaga (Neiker-Tecnalia) sampled "more than 190 points". The vineyard had "plots with soils of different characteristics", producing "significant differences in the production of the grape and in the quality of the must". Soil depths ranged from 25–80 cm (sandstone) to 85–100 cm (argillite); soils that held more water gave the most robust vines, while on sandstone the colour intensity was "up to four times greater than on the other soils" (CORDIS). This is one study of one vineyard, reported in a summary; the paper itself has not yet been consulted.
- **"One water" does not mean equal water.** [Established, for this case] The same study found that "Hydric availability affected the unit production of the vine": rain or irrigation can be the same, while each soil keeps a different share of it. This matches the commentators' picture of good and poor soils side by side.
- **Several trunks from one root.** [Established] Date palms send up offshoots from the base. FAO: "Offshoot plants are true to type to the parent palm", while seedlings differ — "half of the progeny will be males and half will be females" and "no two seedling palms are alike". A palm produces offshoots "in a limited number (20 to 30 at most)". The verse's *ṣinwān* describes this growth habit; it says nothing about cloning.

### Points of awe
- In a single vineyard, soils a few metres apart changed how much grape a vine gave and how deep its colour was — on one soil, up to four times the colour intensity (CORDIS).
- A date-palm offshoot is "true to type": the same tree, carried forward (FAO).

### The reflection
Stand at the edge of a field and look across a fence at the next one. The same rain fell on both. The same sun crossed both. Yet one gives sweet fruit and the other gives sour, or little at all.

The verse makes this ordinary sight into a sign. It lists the things that are the same — the land side by side, the "one water" — and then the one thing that is not: "We make some of them exceed others in [quality of] fruit." The difference is placed with God.

Ibn Kathir slows down to name it: shape, colour, taste, scent, blossom, leaf. We pass these differences every day without noticing. The verse asks us to notice, and then to reason. Its last words are not "for people who know botany" but "for a people who reason".

Today we can describe some of the causes: a soil that holds water and one that lets it go, roots that go deep or shallow, the inherited nature of each plant. This does not empty the sign. A reader can still ask why the world is built so that one water gives a thousand flavours, and why that variety is so often generous to us. That question is the reflection; the verse invites it but does not answer it with a mechanism.

### Honest notes
- **No popular "miracle" claim was found** for this verse in the sources read. The risk here is the opposite: treating the verse as if it denied natural causes. It does not; it attributes the variety to God and asks people to reason about it.
- **Science and the verse.** The vineyard study shows that neighbouring soils differ, which fits the commentators' picture. It does not test the verse and should not be presented as confirming it.
- **Al-Rāzī.** That may well be so, but his commentary on 13:4 has not yet been consulted, so the claim cannot stay unsourced.
- **The hadith of the dates** (Tirmidhi 3118) has a disputed grade; it is reported, not relied on.

### Questions readers ask next
**Doesn't soil science explain the differences?** It explains some of the *how*: in one vineyard, soil depth and water-holding changed yield and grape colour (CORDIS). The verse's question is a different one — why the world is ordered so that one water gives such variety — and it leaves that to the reader's reasoning.

**What does "several from a root" mean?** *Ṣinwān*: several trunks from one root. Ibn Kathir gives figs, pomegranates and dates; al-Jalālayn says date palms with many offshoots.

**Is "one water" literally the same water?** The commentators read it as the same rain or river water (*Tanwīr*; al-Jalālayn). Soils then hold different amounts of it, which is part of why neighbouring plots differ.

**Did the Prophet (pbuh) comment on this verse?** A report in al-Tirmidhi (3118) names kinds of dates for "some … more excellent than others to eat", but its grade is disputed (*ḥasan* to al-Albani, weak to Zubayr ʿAlī Zaʾī).

## S28 — Livestock and their benefits

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via `src/data/quran.json` — 16:5–7, 36:71–73.
- Ibn Kathir (abridged English), *al-Jalālayn* and *Tanwīr al-Miqbās*, on 16:5, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-5-sora-16.html, and on 36:71, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-71-sora-36.html.
- Cardiff University news, "New light shed on the domestication history of sheep and goats", https://cardiff.ac.uk/news/view/1128739-new-light-shed-on-the-domestication-history-of-sheep-and-goats.
- Almathen et al., "Ancient and modern DNA reveal dynamics of domestication and cross-continental dispersal of the dromedary", *PNAS* 113(24):6707–6712 (2016), https://orca.cardiff.ac.uk/id/eprint/90782/1/Almaten_Charruau_Mohandesan_etal_2016.pdf; and the phys.org report, https://phys.org/news/2016-05-dromedary-domestication.html.

### In brief
16:5–7 and 36:71–73 list what grazing animals give — warmth, food, beauty, the carrying of loads — and end with a question: "so will they not be grateful?"

### Verse note
"And the grazing livestock He has created for you; in them is warmth and [numerous] benefits, and from them you eat. And for you in them is [the enjoyment of] beauty when you bring them in [for the evening] and when you send them out [to pasture]. And they carry your loads to a land you could not have reached except with difficulty to yourselves. Indeed, your Lord is Kind and Merciful" (16:5–7), Meccan, at the start of a long list of blessings in Sura al-Naḥl ("The Bee").

"Do they not see that We have created for them from what Our hands have made, grazing livestock, and [then] they are their owners? And We have tamed them for them, so some of them they ride, and some of them they eat. And for them therein are [other] benefits and drinks, so will they not be grateful?" (36:71–73), Meccan, near the end of Sura Yā Sīn, in a passage answering those who doubt the resurrection.

- **Key words.** *Al-anʿām* ("grazing livestock"): camels, cattle, sheep and goats. *Difʾ* ("warmth"). *Jamāl* ("beauty"). *Dhallalnāhā* (36:72, "We have tamed them"): made them manageable, submissive.
- **Whom they address.** People in general ("for you"), and in 36 those who "do not see" — an invitation to look at a familiar sight.

### Classical tafsir
**Ibn Kathir on 16:5–7.** "The blessings include the benefits derived from their wool and hair, from which clothes and furnishings are made"; Ibn ʿAbbas said the warmth "refers to clothing". The food is "from their milk which is drunk, and their young which are eaten." On 16:6: "Their beauty is a kind of adornment", seen "when they are brought back from the pasture in the evening" and "when you send them out to the pasture in the morning." On 16:7, the loads are "the heavy burdens that you cannot move or carry by yourselves".

**Ibn Kathir on 36:71–73.** "Allah mentions the blessing that He has bestowed upon His creation by subjugating these cattle for their use." On "they are their owners", he cites Qatada: "They are their masters." On "We have tamed them": "even if a young child comes to a camel he can make it kneel down", and "Even if the train consists of a hundred or more camels, they could all be led by a young child." On riding: "some of them they ride on their journeys, and use them to carry their burdens to all regions." The other benefits include "wool, fur, and hair, furnishings and articles of convenience"; the "drinks" are "their milk" (he also mentions the use of their urine as medicine).

**Al-Jalālayn** on 16:5: "In them there is warmth that by which you keep warm in the way of clothes and garments taken from their hairs and wools". On 36:71, the livestock are "camels cows and sheep", and the people are "masters controlling them."

**Tanwīr al-Miqbās** on 16:5 reads the livestock as "i.e. the camels", giving "warm clothing" "made of their skin."

**Where they differ.** Mostly they agree. They differ in detail: the warmth comes from wool and hair (Ibn Kathir; al-Jalālayn) or from skins (*Tanwīr*); the animals are camels alone (*Tanwīr* on 16:5) or camels, cattle and sheep (al-Jalālayn on 36:71). Ibn Kathir alone dwells on the beauty of the evening return and on the image of a child leading a hundred camels.

### How it works
Each point is tied to "We have tamed them for them" (36:72) and to the benefits named.
- **When sheep and goats were tamed.** [Established, date approximate] Comparing the genes of domestic sheep and goats with their wild ancestors (the Asiatic mouflon and the Bezoar ibex), Cardiff researchers (Pablo Orozco-terWengel and Mike Bruford) place their domestication "approximately 10,500 years ago" "near the fertile crescent" (Cardiff University news; the journal paper has not yet been consulted).
- **When camels were tamed.** [Established, date approximate] Using DNA from 1,083 modern dromedaries from 21 countries, plus ancient specimens, Almathen et al. (2016) conclude that "The domestication of the dromedary likely happened in the late second millennium BCE" — roughly 3,000 years ago — with a reduction in bone size dated to "ca. 1,100–800 BCE", and identify "the Southeast Arabian Peninsula as the region of first domestication" (phys.org report). Unusually, dromedaries kept high genetic diversity, because wild animals kept breeding into domestic herds.
- **What "tamed" involved.** [Established] Domestication was a long human process of selecting and breeding animals that were calm enough to handle. The verse speaks of God taming them; the historical process is described here separately and is not the verse's meaning.

### Points of awe
- Sheep and goats have lived with people for about 10,500 years (Cardiff); camels, the animal the commentators had most in mind, for about 3,000 (Almathen et al.).
- A camel can weigh many times a child's weight, yet Ibn Kathir's picture is a child leading a hundred of them.

### The reflection
These verses take the most ordinary thing in a herder's life and ask him to look at it again. Animals that are larger and stronger than us let us milk them, shear them, ride them and load them. A child, says Ibn Kathir, can make a camel kneel.

Then the Quran adds something unexpected: beauty. "For you in them is [the enjoyment of] beauty when you bring them in [for the evening] and when you send them out [to pasture]." Ibn Kathir sees the scene: the herd coming home full in the evening, going out in the morning. The verse does not stop at usefulness; it invites the pleasure of looking.

The question in 36:73 is the centre: "so will they not be grateful?" Gratitude here is not only a feeling. The verses link it to seeing — "Do they not see" — and to remembering where the gift comes from: "We have created for them". Owning the animals ("they are their owners") is itself a trust, not an achievement.

A modern reader knows that domestication took thousands of years of human work. The reflection does not deny that. It asks why there were animals at all that could be shaped this way, and why they were placed within reach. For many people today, livestock are something seen only on a plate. The verses ask us to see the animal before the meal, and to be grateful for both.

### Honest notes
- **Domestication and "tamed".** The verse says God tamed them; science describes a long human process of selection. These are answers to different questions (who gives, and how it happened). The page should not present the verse as describing domestication.
- **Dates vary.** 10,500 years (sheep and goats) comes from a university news summary of a genetic study; 3,000 years for camels from the paper's own wording ("likely", "late second millennium BCE"). Other studies give other dates.
- **The urine-as-medicine remark** in Ibn Kathir on 36:73 is reported here for completeness; the hadith behind it has not yet been consulted and is not used.
- **Animal welfare.** These verses speak of benefit and gratitude. The Islamic ethics of how animals must be treated (kindness, not overloading) is a real and related subject, but no source on it was read.

### Questions readers ask next
**Why does the Quran mention the beauty of cattle?** 16:6 names it directly. Ibn Kathir: "Their beauty is a kind of adornment", seen as the herd goes out in the morning and comes home in the evening.

**Does "tamed" mean they were domesticated?** The commentators read it as God making them manageable — "even if a young child comes to a camel he can make it kneel down" (Ibn Kathir). The history of domestication (about 10,500 years for sheep and goats; about 3,000 for camels) is a separate, scientific account.

**What is the "warmth" in 16:5?** Clothing, said Ibn ʿAbbas (via Ibn Kathir); al-Jalālayn: garments from their hair and wool; *Tanwīr*: clothing made from their skins.

**Which animals are meant?** *Al-anʿām*: al-Jalālayn names camels, cattle and sheep; *Tanwīr* reads 16:5 as camels.

## S29 — Human consciousness and the soul

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via `src/data/quran.json` — 17:85, 51:20–21, 15:29.
- Ibn Kathir (abridged English), *al-Jalālayn*, *Tanwīr al-Miqbās* and Mawdudi's *Tafhīm al-Qurʾān* (all English), on 17:85, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-85-sora-17.html; on 51:21, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-21-sora-51.html; on 15:29, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-29-sora-15.html.
- Josh Weisberg, "The Hard Problem of Consciousness", *Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy*, https://iep.utm.edu/hard-con.
- Max Planck Institute for Empirical Aesthetics, "Rethinking Consciousness: When Science Puts Itself to the Test" (30 April 2025), on Cogitate Consortium et al., *Nature* (2025), doi:10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1, https://www.aesthetics.mpg.de/en/newsroom/news/news-article/article/rethinking-consciousness-when-science-puts-itself-to-the-test-1.html.
- *Scientific American*, "A 25-Year-Old Bet about Consciousness Has Finally Been Settled", https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/a-25-year-old-bet-about-consciousness-has-finally-been-settled.

### In brief
17:85 answers a question about the *rūḥ* (soul or spirit) by setting a limit — "The soul is of the affair of my Lord. And mankind have not been given of knowledge except a little" — while 51:21 points to the self as a sign: "And in yourselves. Then will you not see?"

### Verse note
"And they ask you, [O Muhammad], about the soul. Say, 'The soul is of the affair of my Lord. And mankind have not been given of knowledge except a little'" (17:85), Meccan, in Sura al-Isrāʾ.

"And on the earth are signs for the certain [in faith]. And in yourselves. Then will you not see?" (51:20–21), Meccan, in Sura al-Dhāriyāt.

"And when I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My [created] soul, then fall down to him in prostration" (15:29), Meccan, God's words to the angels about Adam (pbuh) in Sura al-Ḥijr.

- **Key words.** *Al-rūḥ*: spirit or soul; the same word is used elsewhere in the Quran for the angel of revelation, which is why commentators differ on what it means in 17:85 (below). *Min amri rabbī* ("of the affair of my Lord"): from His command or His matter. *Min rūḥī* (15:29, "of My [created] soul"): the bracket "[created]" is Saheeh International's, not in the Arabic.
- **Occasion.** A report in al-Bukhari, cited by Ibn Kathir (below), says the question came from some Jews; *Tanwīr al-Miqbās* says it came from the Meccans. The sura is Meccan.

### Classical tafsir
**Who asked (17:85).** Ibn Kathir: "Al-Bukhari recorded in his Tafsir of this Ayah that 'Abdullah bin Mas'ud said" that "some Jews passed by" and asked; the Prophet (pbuh) "kept quiet and did not give them an answer" until the verse came. He also cites ʿIkrima: "The People of the Book asked". Al-Jalālayn says the askers were "the Jews". *Tanwīr al-Miqbās* says instead "the people of Mecca-Abu Jahl and his host-who asked".

**What the *rūḥ* is here.**
- *Al-Jalālayn*: "the Spirit from which the body receives life".
- *Ibn Kathir*, discussing the soul's link to the body: "the Ruh is not called Nafs except when it joins the body and is affected by it"; "the Ruh is the origin and essence, and the Nafs consists of the Ruh and its connection to the body." (*Nafs*: the self.)
- *Tanwīr al-Miqbās*: "the Spirit is of the marvels of my Lord", or "it is the restricted knowledge of my Lord."
- *Mawdudi* (d. 1979, Pakistan; a modern commentator, not classical): the word here "stands for the angel who brings Revelation", citing Ibn ʿAbbas, Qatada and al-Hasan al-Basri; he expresses "great hesitation in accepting" the soul reading.

**On "except a little".** In the ʿIkrima report cited by Ibn Kathir, the Prophet (pbuh) says of human knowledge: "but in comparison to the knowledge of Allah, it is very little."

**On 51:20–21.** Ibn Kathir: "Allah says that there are signs on earth that testify to the might of the Creator and His boundless ability"; in ourselves, "Allah put every organ in their bodies in its rightful place where they most need it to be." He cites Qatada: "He who thinks about his own creation will realize that he was created with flexible joints" (so that worship is easy for him). Al-Jalālayn: "in your souls there are also signs from the beginning of your creation to its end". *Tanwīr*: "signs and lessons such as pain, sickness and misfortune."

**On 15:29.** Al-Jalālayn: "the annexation of 'the Spirit' to him is a conferring of honour upon Adam" — "My spirit" is an attribution of honour, not a part of God. Ibn Kathir's translator renders it "the soul which I created for him"; *Tanwīr*: God "placed the spirit into him."

**Where they differ.** On who asked: some Jews (Ibn Masʿud via Bukhari; al-Jalālayn) or the Meccans (*Tanwīr*). On what *rūḥ* means in 17:85: the life-giving spirit (al-Jalālayn), the soul in its relation to the self (Ibn Kathir), a marvel or hidden knowledge of God (*Tanwīr*), or the angel of revelation (Mawdudi, citing early authorities). On 51:21: the body's design (Ibn Kathir, Qatada) or the whole course of life, including pain (*Tanwīr*). All agree that human knowledge here is "a little".

### How it works
Each point is tied to "mankind have not been given of knowledge except a little" and "in yourselves".
- **The "hard problem".** [Debated] Philosophers call it "the problem of explaining why any physical state is conscious rather than nonconscious" — why "there is 'something it is like' for a subject in conscious experience", as Thomas Nagel asked "what it is like to be a bat" (Weisberg, *IEP*). Responses range widely: "Eliminativism holds that there is no hard problem of consciousness"; others hold "dualism, the claim that consciousness is ontologically distinct from anything physical"; "Panpsychism holds that the phenomenal is basic to all matter." There is no agreed answer.
- **Testing theories head to head.** [Established, for this study] In a large "adversarial collaboration", the Cogitate Consortium "tested a total of 256 participants" in seven labs with "three complementary neuroimaging techniques" (fMRI, MEG and intracranial EEG). Integrated Information Theory's key prediction, sustained synchronisation in the back of the brain, "was not supported by the data"; for Global Neuronal Workspace Theory, "key features were missing" and its predicted "ignition" at the end of an experience "could not be confirmed". "Both theories were challenged" (Max Planck Institute summary of the *Nature* paper, 2025).
- **A bet that was lost.** [Established] In 1998 the neuroscientist Christof Koch bet the philosopher David Chalmers "a case of wine" that within 25 years researchers would find a clear neural signature of consciousness. At the 2023 meeting of the Association for the Scientific Study of Consciousness in New York, Chalmers said, "It's clear that things are not clear"; Koch handed over the wine, and the two renewed the bet for another 25 years (*Scientific American*).

### Points of awe
- Two leading theories of consciousness were tested on 256 people with three kinds of brain scan, and both came out "challenged" (Cogitate, 2025).
- A 25-year bet on finding consciousness in the brain ended with a case of wine and a new 25-year bet (*Scientific American*).

### The reflection
Some questions are answered by giving more information. 17:85 answers by drawing a line. People asked about the *rūḥ*; the reply was that it belongs to God's affair, and that human knowledge is "a little".

That is not the end of looking. 51:21 sends us straight back to ourselves: "And in yourselves. Then will you not see?" Qatada saw the joints that let us bow; Ibn Kathir saw each organ "in its rightful place". *Tanwīr* goes further and finds signs even in pain and misfortune. The self is a book to read, even if its deepest page stays closed.

Modern science has walked a long way into the brain. It can map which activity comes with which experience, and design careful tests between rival theories. Yet when two leading theories were put to the test, both stumbled, and a famous bet on solving the puzzle by 2023 was lost. This does not prove the Quran's answer, and the verse does not need it to. But it gives the believer and the honest sceptic a shared place to stand: we are each the thing we understand least.

15:29 adds what the Quran does say: that the human being was "proportioned" and then given something "of My [created] soul", a gift of honour. A person who feels small before this mystery is in the position the verse describes. The fitting response is not to stop asking, but to ask humbly.

### Honest notes
- **The popular claim.** It is sometimes said that the unsolved hard problem shows the soul is real. That does not follow. Many philosophers hold that consciousness will be explained physically, or that the "hard problem" is confused (eliminativism, in Weisberg's account). An unsolved problem is not evidence for any one answer.
- ***Rūḥ* and "consciousness".** The words do not match exactly. Classical commentators disagree on what *rūḥ* means in 17:85, and one reading (Mawdudi, citing early authorities) is that it refers to the angel of revelation, not the human soul at all. Linking the verse to the modern study of consciousness is a reflection, and should be labelled as one.
- **What the Cogitate study shows.** It challenged two theories; it did not show that consciousness cannot be explained. Its results are reported here through the Max Planck Institute's summary; the *Nature* paper itself has not yet been consulted.
- **Not read.** Ibn al-Qayyim's *Kitāb al-Rūḥ* and al-Ghazālī's *ʿAjāʾib al-Qalb*, both.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is the soul the same as consciousness?** Not exactly. *Rūḥ* in 17:85 is read as the life-giving spirit (al-Jalālayn), the soul in its link to the self (Ibn Kathir), or even the angel of revelation (Mawdudi). Consciousness is a modern philosophical term for inner experience.

**Who asked the Prophet (pbuh) about the *rūḥ*?** Some Jews, according to Ibn Masʿud's report in al-Bukhari (cited by Ibn Kathir); the Meccans, according to *Tanwīr al-Miqbās*.

**Does neuroscience disprove the soul?** No. Nor does it prove it. It has mapped much of what happens in the brain during experience, but why experience exists at all is still debated (Weisberg, *IEP*), and the 2025 Cogitate tests challenged both leading theories.

**What does "My [created] soul" in 15:29 mean?** Al-Jalālayn: the attribution to God is "a conferring of honour upon Adam", not a part of God. The bracket "[created]" is Saheeh International's addition.

## S30 — DNA and the information in life

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via `src/data/quran.json` — 20:50, 87:2–3, 54:49, 25:2.
- Ibn Kathir (abridged English), *al-Jalālayn* and *Tanwīr al-Miqbās*, on 20:50, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-50-sora-20.html; on 87:3, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-3-sora-87.html; on 54:49, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-49-sora-54.html.
- National Human Genome Research Institute, *Human Genome Project FAQ* (Smithsonian exhibition), https://www.genome.gov/Pages/Education/Smithsonian_Exhibition/Human_Genome_Project_FAQ.pdf.
- Nurk, Koren, Rhie et al., "The complete sequence of a human genome", *Science* 376(6588):44–53 (2022), abstract, https://profiles.wustl.edu/en/publications/the-complete-sequence-of-a-human-genome/.
- News-Medical, "Researchers trim count of human genes to 20,000–25,000" (21 October 2004), on the International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium's paper in *Nature*, https://www.news-medical.net/news/2004/10/21/5738.aspx.
- *Wikipedia*, "Kitzmiller v. Dover Area School District", https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kitzmiller_v._Dover_Area_School_District.
- *Wikiwand* (Wikipedia), "Signature in the Cell", https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Signature_in_the_Cell.
- Shoaib Ahmed Malik, *Islam and Evolution: Al-Ghazālī and the Modern Evolutionary Paradigm* (Routledge, 2021), OAPEN record and abstract, https://library.oapen.org/handle/20.500.12657/48443.

### In brief
20:50 gives Moses' (pbuh) answer to Pharaoh — "Our Lord is He who gave each thing its form and then guided [it]" — and 87:2–3 and 54:49 say the same in other words: God created, proportioned, "destined and [then] guided", and made all things "with predestination".

### Verse note
"He said, 'Our Lord is He who gave each thing its form and then guided [it]'" (20:50), Meccan, in Sura Ṭā Hā: Moses (pbuh) answering Pharaoh's question, "who is your Lord?" (the question is in the verse before, not quoted here).

"Who created and proportioned. And who destined and [then] guided" (87:2–3), Meccan, in Sura al-Aʿlā, describing "your Lord, the Most High" of its first verse.

"Indeed, all things We created with predestination" (54:49), Meccan, near the end of Sura al-Qamar.

- **Key words.** *Khalqahu* (20:50, "its form"): its created nature. *Hadā* ("guided"). *Qaddara* (87:3, "destined"): measured out, determined. *Bi-qadar* (54:49, "with predestination"): with measure, by decree; the same root as 25:2, "determined it with [precise] determination".
- **Whom they address.** 20:50 is a reply to a ruler who claimed divinity. 87 and 54 address the listeners in Mecca. None of them speaks of cells or molecules.

### Classical tafsir
**Ibn Kathir on 20:50.** He gives several early readings of "gave each thing its form":
- Ibn ʿAbbas (via ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥa): "He is saying that He created a mate for everything."
- Ibn ʿAbbas (via al-Ḍaḥḥāk): "He made the man a man, and the donkey a donkey and the sheep a sheep."
- Mujahid: "He gave everything its form"; and "He fashioned the creation of every moving creature."
- Saʿīd ibn Jubayr: "He gave each of His creatures what is suitable for its creation", and "He also gave creature a suitable spouse, and He influenced everything towards that mate."

**Al-Jalālayn on 20:50.** God gave everything "its peculiar nature", and guided each animal "to its source of food drink and procreation and in other ways." *Tanwīr al-Miqbās*: "He gave unto everything its nature", and "then He inspired them how to eat, drink and copulate."

**Ibn Kathir on 87:2–3.** "He created that which has been created, and He fashioned every creation in the best of forms." On "destined and guided", he cites Mujahid: "He guided man to distress and happiness, and he guided the cattle to their pastures", links the verse to 20:50, and explains: "meaning, He decreed a set measure and guided the creation to it." He cites the hadith in Sahih Muslim from ʿAbdullah ibn ʿAmr (ra): "Verily, Allah ordained the measure of all creation fifty thousand years before He created the heavens and the earth" (Muslim; the number was not given on the page read). Al-Jalālayn on 87:3: God "determined what He wants and guided to what He had determined of good or evil". *Tanwīr*: "He measured felicity and wretchedness for His created beings".

**Ibn Kathir on 54:49.** He reads it as a verse on divine decree (*qadar*), cited "to refute the Qadariyyah sect, who started their sect during the latter time of the Companions" (the Qadariyya denied God's prior decree). He cites a report that "The idolators of the Quraysh came to the Messenger of Allah" disputing about decree, and the saying "Every thing is predetermined, even laziness and intelligence" (from ʿAbdullah ibn ʿUmar, collected by Muslim; the page gives no number). *Tanwīr*: "This verse was revealed about those who argued about destiny."

**Where they differ.** On "form" in 20:50: a mate for each thing (Ibn ʿAbbas via ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥa; Saʿīd ibn Jubayr) or each kind made what it is (Ibn ʿAbbas via al-Ḍaḥḥāk; Mujahid). On "guided": guidance to food, drink and mating (al-Jalālayn; *Tanwīr*), to pasture for animals and to happiness or misery for humans (Mujahid), or to a measure decreed in advance (Ibn Kathir). 54:49 is read mainly as a verse about God's decree, not about nature's proportions.

### How it works
Each point is tied to "gave each thing its form" and "destined" (a measure set in advance).
- **A written set of instructions.** [Established] DNA carries an organism's inherited instructions in four chemical "letters". "One complete set of genomic instructions is about 3 billion base pairs" (NHGRI, *Human Genome Project FAQ*).
- **The full text, finally read.** [Established] In 2022 the Telomere-to-Telomere Consortium published a "complete 3.055 billion-base pair sequence" of one human genome (T2T-CHM13). It filled "the remaining 8% of the genome", adding "nearly 200 million base pairs" (Nurk et al., *Science*).
- **Fewer genes than expected.** [Established] In 2004 the International Human Genome Sequencing Consortium counted "19,599 protein-coding genes", with a headline estimate of "20,000-25,000" — far fewer than had been predicted (News-Medical, reporting the *Nature* paper). Much of the rest of the genome helps control when and where genes are used.

### Points of awe
- About 3 billion letters make one set of human instructions; a complete, gap-free reading came only in 2022 (NHGRI; Nurk et al.).
- A human being is built from around 20,000 protein-coding genes (2004 count) — fewer than many expected.

### The reflection
Pharaoh asked a question meant to humiliate: "Who is your Lord?" Moses (pbuh) did not answer with a name or a title. He pointed at the world: the One "who gave each thing its form and then guided [it]."

The commentators heard two steps in that sentence. First, form: a man made a man, a sheep a sheep, each creature given "what is suitable for its creation". Then guidance: each creature finding its food, its drink, its mate, and the herd its pasture. Al-Aʿlā repeats the pattern: created, proportioned, measured, guided.

A modern reader cannot hear "form, then guidance" without thinking of the instructions every living cell carries. It is striking that each creature carries, inside itself, something like a text that shapes what it becomes and how it behaves. For a believer this fits the verse's picture of a world measured and guided. But the verse does not mention DNA, and the commentators did not read it so. The link is ours, and it should be offered as a reflection, not as the verse's meaning.

What the verse offers everyone is the question Moses (pbuh) put to Pharaoh: when you see form and guidance running through every living thing, what do you think lies behind it?

### Honest notes
- **The popular claim.** It is often said that DNA proves design. The best-known version is Stephen Meyer's *Signature in the Cell* (2009), which argues that "intelligent design is therefore the best explanation for the emergence of life on this planet", because chance and chemistry have not been shown able to produce the information in DNA and proteins (*Wikipedia* summary). [Debated]
- **The reply.** Critics such as the chemist Stephen Fletcher (Loughborough) argue that natural selection is itself a chemical process that operated before cells existed, and that research on RNA undercuts Meyer's case ("reality has overtaken Meyer's book"); the biologist Darrel Falk (BioLogos) also rejected the argument (*Wikipedia*). In the US, a federal district court ruled in *Kitzmiller v. Dover* (20 December 2005, Judge John E. Jones III) that "intelligent design is not science" (*Wikipedia*). *Limit:* a court ruling on what may be taught in schools does not settle the philosophical question of whether information points to a mind.
- **A Muslim alternative.** Shoaib Ahmed Malik argues, using al-Ghazālī's framework, that "his ideas can be used to reach an alignment between Islam and Neo-Darwinian evolution" (*Islam and Evolution*, Routledge, 2021, abstract). On this view, belief in God does not depend on gaps in biology.
- **This page's position.** DNA is presented as a sign that invites wonder, not as proof, and not as something the verses predicted.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is "guided" a reference to DNA?** No classical commentator read it that way. They read it as guidance to food, drink and mates (al-Jalālayn), to pasture and to happiness or misery (Mujahid), or to a measure set in advance (Ibn Kathir). The DNA link is a modern reflection.

**What does "each thing its form" mean?** In Ibn ʿAbbas's words (via al-Ḍaḥḥāk), each kind made what it is: "He made the man a man, and the donkey a donkey and the sheep a sheep." In another report from him, it means God created "a mate for everything."

**What does "with predestination" in 54:49 mean?** Ibn Kathir reads it as God's decree, against those who denied it, citing "Every thing is predetermined, even laziness and intelligence" (Muslim).

**Does DNA prove God?** That is debated. Meyer argues it points to design; critics argue natural processes can produce biological information; some Muslim scholars, such as Shoaib Ahmed Malik, hold that faith does not depend on the answer.

## S31 — Ecological balance and measure

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via `src/data/quran.json` — 55:7–9, 15:19, 57:25.
- Ibn Kathir (abridged English), *al-Jalālayn* and *Tanwīr al-Miqbās*, on 55:7, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-7-sora-55.html, and on 15:19, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-19-sora-15.html.
- Estes & Palmisano, "Sea otters: their role in structuring nearshore communities", *Science* 185(4156):1058–1060 (1974), abstract, USGS record, https://pubs.usgs.gov/publication/1007670.
- Utah State University release via *ScienceDaily*, on MacNulty, Cooper, Procko & Clark-Wolf, comment on Ripple et al. (2025), *Global Ecology and Conservation* 63, e03899 (2025), https://sciencedaily.com/releases/2026/02/260212025612.htm.

### In brief
55:7–9 joins the sky and the scale: God "raised" the heaven and "imposed the balance", then tells people "establish weight in justice and do not make deficient the balance".

### Verse note
"And the heaven He raised and imposed the balance. That you not transgress within the balance. And establish weight in justice and do not make deficient the balance" (55:7–9), in Sura al-Raḥmān, Medinan in the Tanzil classification.

"And the earth - We have spread it and cast therein firmly set mountains and caused to grow therein [something] of every well-balanced thing" (15:19), Meccan.

- **Key words.** *Al-mīzān* ("the balance"): a scale, and by extension justice. It appears three times in 55:7–9. *Mawzūn* (15:19, "well-balanced"): weighed, measured.
- **Setting.** Al-Raḥmān lists God's gifts one after another, with a repeated refrain asking which of them its hearers would deny. 55:7–9 moves in three verses from the sky to the marketplace.
- **A related verse.** "We have already sent Our messengers with clear evidences and sent down with them the Scripture and the balance that the people may maintain [their affairs] in justice" (57:25) — Ibn Kathir cites it to explain *mīzān* in 55:7.

### Classical tafsir
**Ibn Kathir on 55:7–9.** The balance is "the justice", citing 57:25. On 55:8: "He created the heavens and earth in justice and truth so that everything is founded on, and observing, justice and truth." On 55:9: "do not cheat in the weights and measures, but rather observe justice and fairness".

**Al-Jalālayn on 55:7.** "He has raised the heaven and set up the balance He has established justice".

**Tanwīr al-Miqbās on 55:7.** "He explained fair measure through the scale".

**Ibn Kathir on 15:19.** "All kinds of things in due proportion": Ibn ʿAbbas said "this means with their predetermined proportions", and this "was also the opinion of Sa'id bin Jubayr, 'Ikrimah, Abu Malik, Mujahid, Al-Hakim bin 'Utaybah, Al-Hasan bin Muhammad, Abu Salih and Qatadah."

**Al-Jalālayn on 15:19.** "every kind of thing known and determined." *Tanwīr*: "of vegetation and fruits in measured and known quantities", or, as another reading, things measured by weight "such as gold, silver, ore, copper, etc."

**Where they differ.** On 55:7 they agree that the balance means justice (Ibn Kathir; al-Jalālayn), or fair measure (*Tanwīr*). On 15:19 they read *mawzūn* as a proportion set by God (Ibn ʿAbbas and the early commentators via Ibn Kathir), as what is "known and determined" (al-Jalālayn), or, in a second reading, as metals that are weighed (*Tanwīr*). None of them reads either verse as being about ecology.

### How it works
These are modern facts about balance in nature. They connect to the verses only by reflection; the verses' own subject is justice.
- **One predator, a whole coast.** [Established] Comparing "western Aleutian Islands with and without sea otter populations", Estes and Palmisano found that "Sea otters control herbivorous invertebrate populations", and that "Removal of sea otters causes increased herbivory and ultimately results in the destruction of macrophyte associations" (large seaweeds such as kelp) (*Science*, 1974). Ecologists call such animals "keystone" species.
- **Yellowstone, and why simple stories fail.** [Debated] After wolves returned to Yellowstone in 1995, it was widely said they set off a "trophic cascade" (a chain of effects down the food web) that restored willows. A 2025 study by Ripple and colleagues reported a 1,500% rise in willow crown volume. In a formal reply, Daniel MacNulty (Utah State) and colleagues argued that this estimate "relies on circular reasoning", because "height was used both to compute and to predict volume", and that the data show a "more modest and spatially variable response". David Cooper (Colorado State): "there is no evidence that predator recovery caused a large or system-wide increase in willow growth". MacNulty: "Predator effects in Yellowstone are real but context-dependent". The release also notes Hobbs et al. (2024), whose roughly 20 years of field experiments found only weak cascade effects (*ScienceDaily*, 2026). The debate continues.

### Points of awe
- On islands without sea otters, the kelp forests were destroyed by grazers; with otters, they stood (Estes & Palmisano, 1974).
- Even the most famous story of balance restored — Yellowstone's wolves — is still being argued about by careful scientists (MacNulty et al., 2025).

### The reflection
Al-Raḥmān does something unusual in three short verses. It looks up at the raised sky and says God "imposed the balance". Then, without a pause, it looks down at a merchant's scale: "establish weight in justice and do not make deficient the balance."

Ibn Kathir explains the link: the heavens and earth were made "in justice and truth so that everything is founded on, and observing, justice and truth". The same order that holds the sky is asked of the hand that weighs flour. Cheating on a scale is not a small thing; it goes against the grain of creation.

The verses do not speak of ecosystems. But a modern reader can extend the thought, and label it as an extension: we also hold a scale over the living world. Remove the sea otter, and the kelp forest falls. Overfish, clear forests, warm the climate, and we "make deficient the balance" in another sense.

Yellowstone adds a needed warning. A beautiful story — wolves return, rivers heal — turned out to be more complicated than it was told. The Quranic command "establish weight in justice" applies here too: to weigh the evidence fairly, and not to tip the scale toward the story we like.

### Honest notes
- **The verses are about justice.** Ibn Kathir, al-Jalālayn and *Tanwīr* read *mīzān* as justice and fair measure. Reading 55:7–9 as a teaching about ecology is a modern extension. It is a reasonable one, but it must be labelled.
- **Nature's "balance" is not harmony.** Ecosystems change, species go extinct, and competition is constant. The sea otter case shows strong effects, not a fixed balance.
- **Yellowstone.** The willow debate is live. This page reports both sides through a university release; neither paper itself was read.
- **15:19.** The early commentators read *mawzūn* as a measure set by God. Modern claims that it describes chemical or ecological proportions go beyond them.

### Questions readers ask next
**Is the verse about ecology?** Classically it is about justice and fair measure (Ibn Kathir; al-Jalālayn). Applying it to the environment is a modern reflection, and this page labels it as one.

**What does "do not make deficient the balance" mean in practice?** Ibn Kathir: "do not cheat in the weights and measures, but rather observe justice and fairness".

**What does "well-balanced" in 15:19 mean?** "With their predetermined proportions", said Ibn ʿAbbas, followed by Mujahid, Qatada and others (via Ibn Kathir).

**Did wolves really save Yellowstone?** Scientists disagree. One 2025 study reported a large willow recovery; a reply argued that its method was circular and that the effect is "modest and spatially variable" (MacNulty et al.).

## S32 — The human being taught to speak

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via `src/data/quran.json` — 55:3–4, 90:8–10, 2:31.
- Ibn Kathir (abridged English), *al-Jalālayn*, *Tanwīr al-Miqbās* and Mawdudi's *Tafhīm al-Qurʾān* (English), on 55:4, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-4-sora-55.html; on 90:9, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-9-sora-90.html; on 2:31, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-31-sora-2.html.
- University of Washington News, "How babies acquire building blocks of speech affects later reading and language ability, UW researcher tells White House summit on cognitive development" (26 July 2001), on Patricia Kuhl, https://www.washington.edu/news/2001/07/26/how-babies-acquire-building-blocks-of-speech-affects-later-reading-and-language-ability-uw-researcher-tells-white-house-summit-on-cognitive-development/.
- Patricia K. Kuhl, "Early language acquisition: cracking the speech code", *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 5:831–843 (2004), key points, https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn1533.pdf.
- Kristina Simonyan & Barry Horwitz, "Laryngeal motor cortex and control of speech in humans", *The Neuroscientist* 17(2):197–208 (2011), https://simonyanlab.meei.harvard.edu/wp-content/uploads/2023/04/1073858410386727.pdf.
- Ethnologue, "How many languages are there in the world?", https://ethnologue.com/insights/how-many-languages.

### In brief
Sura al-Raḥmān names speech among the first gifts of the Most Merciful — "Created man, [And] taught him eloquence" (55:3–4) — and 90:8–10 ties the tools of seeing and speaking to the moral choice between "the two ways".

### Verse note
"Created man, [And] taught him eloquence" (55:3–4), in Sura al-Raḥmān, Medinan in the Tanzil classification. The sura opens by naming the Most Merciful and His teaching of the Quran (55:1–2, not quoted here because they were not in the verified data used); creating the human and teaching *al-bayān* come next.

"Have We not made for him two eyes? And a tongue and two lips? And have shown him the two ways?" (90:8–10), Meccan, in Sura al-Balad.

"And He taught Adam the names - all of them. Then He showed them to the angels and said, 'Inform Me of the names of these, if you are truthful'" (2:31), Medinan.

- **Key words.** *Al-bayān* (55:4, "eloquence"): clear expression, making plain what is inside. *Al-najdayn* (90:10, "the two ways"): literally two raised paths. *Al-asmāʾ* (2:31, "the names").
- **Whom they address.** 55 addresses its hearers with a repeated question about God's favours; 90 speaks of the human being in general.

### Classical tafsir
**Ibn Kathir on 55:1–4.** He frames the opening: "Allah informs of His favors and His mercy for His creatures." He cites al-Hasan al-Basri on *al-bayān*: "Eloquent speech." He explains God "making it easy for them to speak and pronounce letters with the various parts of the mouth", naming the alveolar ridge, the tongue and the lips, and links this to teaching the Quran: "This refers to Allah teaching the Qur'an."

**Al-Jalālayn on 55:4.** "teaching him the coherent speech utterance."

**Tanwīr al-Miqbās on 55:4.** "Allah inspired him with the exposition of everything", and it mentions the names of every creature.

**Mawdudi** (modern, 20th century) reads *al-bayān* more widely, as including "the distinction between good and evil, virtue and vice".

**Ibn Kathir on 90:8–10.** The eyes are "for him to see with them"; the tongue "for him to speak with, and so that he can express that which is inside of him"; the lips help "with speaking, eating food, and beautifying his face and his mouth." "The two ways": "This refers to the two paths"; Ibn Masʿud (ra): "The good and the evil." Ibn ʿAbbas is listed among those with the same view. *Tanwīr* on 90:9: the tongue "with which he speaks", the lips "which he can join together or separate".

**Ibn Kathir on 2:31.** Ibn ʿAbbas (via al-Ḍaḥḥāk) said the names were "the names that people use, such as human, animal, sky, earth, land, sea"; asked whether this included "the names of the plate and the pot", he said, "Yes". Mujahid: "The name of the pigeon, the crow and everything." Zayd ibn Aslam's report has the names of angels; *Tanwīr* gives "the names of all his progeny". Ibn Kathir's own summary: God taught Adam (pbuh) "the names of everything, their proper names, the names of their characteristics, and what they do", which showed "Adam's superiority over them in knowledge". Al-Jalālayn: the names were taught by "placing knowledge of them into his heart."

**Where they differ.** On *al-bayān*: speech itself (al-Hasan via Ibn Kathir; al-Jalālayn), the exposition of everything (*Tanwīr*), or, in a modern reading, moral discernment (Mawdudi). On 2:31: the names of all things (Ibn ʿAbbas; Mujahid; Ibn Kathir), of the angels (Zayd ibn Aslam), or of Adam's descendants (*Tanwīr*). On 90:10, the early authorities cited agree: good and evil.

### How it works
Each point is tied to "taught him eloquence" and "a tongue and two lips".
- **Many muscles, one sentence.** [Established] Speaking "involves a precise coordination of more than 100 laryngeal, orofacial, and respiratory muscles", controlled in part by a region of the motor cortex for the larynx (Simonyan & Horwitz, *The Neuroscientist*, 2011).
- **Born able to hear every language.** [Established] Patricia Kuhl (University of Washington): infants are "citizens of the world" who "can hear the differences between all the consonants and vowels used in any language"; they then "have to learn which sound distinctions are meaningful in their language" — English separates "r" and "l", Japanese does not. "Already by 12 months, infants have the rules down" (UW News, 2001, reporting Kuhl). Her 2004 review: "Young infants are sensitive to subtle differences between all phonetic units", followed by "The loss of discrimination for foreign-language distinctions".
- **Learning is social.** [Established] "Infants learn more easily from interactions with human beings speaking another language" (Kuhl, 2004). Language is learned from people, not just heard.
- **Many tongues.** [Established] "7,164 languages are in use today"; "Roughly 40% of languages are now endangered", and "just 23 languages account for more than half the world's population" (Ethnologue).

### Points of awe
- A single spoken sentence needs more than 100 muscles working in time (Simonyan & Horwitz).
- A baby can hear sound differences its parents no longer can, before tuning in to its own language around the first birthday (Kuhl).
- More than 7,000 languages are spoken today; about 40% are endangered (Ethnologue).

### The reflection
Al-Raḥmān opens with a short list of mercies: the teaching of the Quran, the creation of the human, and then *al-bayān*, the gift of making plain what is inside. Before the sun and moon, the stars and the trees, the sura names speech.

Ibn Kathir sees it in the body: letters shaped by tongue, ridge and lips. Al-Balad turns the same body into a question: "Have We not made for him two eyes? And a tongue and two lips? And have shown him the two ways?" The tools of seeing and speaking come first; the choice between good and evil comes right after. Speech is not neutral. What we say can be the first step on either path.

Science fills in how great the gift is. More than a hundred muscles move in time for one sentence. A baby is born able to hear the sounds of any human language, then learns, from the faces and voices around it, which ones matter. Seven thousand languages carry prayers, poems, jokes and laws.

2:31 adds the deepest layer. Adam (pbuh) was taught "the names - all of them", and Ibn Kathir saw in this his "superiority … in knowledge". To name a thing is to begin to understand it. For a believer, every word we speak uses a gift we did not make, and every word will be weighed. The reflection is simple: use it for the better of "the two ways".

### Honest notes
- **No popular "miracle" claim** about these verses was found in the sources read. The risk is a different one: reading modern linguistics into 55:4 or 2:31. Neither verse describes the larynx, the brain, or how children learn language.
- **"The names" (2:31).** Commentators differ on what they were. Reading them as "the capacity to form concepts" is a modern reflection, not a classical reading.
- **Bayān and sign language.** Whether *al-bayān* includes writing or sign language is not addressed by the commentators read. It is a reasonable modern extension (deaf people express themselves fully in sign), but should be labelled as one.

### Questions readers ask next
**Why is speech counted as a blessing?** Al-Raḥmān lists it among the first mercies (55:3–4). Ibn Kathir explains it as God making it easy to speak and form letters with the mouth.

**What are "the two ways" (90:10)?** "The good and the evil", said Ibn Masʿud (ra), via Ibn Kathir.

**Did Adam (pbuh) learn a language?** The verse says he was taught "the names - all of them". Ibn ʿAbbas: the names people use, down to the plate and the pot; other reports say the names of the angels or of his descendants.

**How do babies learn to speak?** They start able to hear the sound differences of all languages and, by about 12 months, have tuned in to their own (Kuhl). They learn best from live people (Kuhl, 2004).

## S33 — The diversity of colors in nature

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via `src/data/quran.json` — 35:27–28.
- Ibn Kathir (abridged English), *al-Jalālayn* and *Tanwīr al-Miqbās*, on 35:27, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-27-sora-35.html.
- KQED *Deep Look*, "What Gives the Morpho Butterfly Its Magnificent Blue?", https://www.kqed.org/science/24552/what-gives-the-morpho-butterfly-its-magnificent-blue.
- Tom Kuntzleman, "The Blue Butterfly Effect", ChemEd X (28 January 2019), https://www.chemedx.org/blog/blue-butterfly-effect.
- Jablonski & Chaplin, "The evolution of human skin coloration", *Journal of Human Evolution* 39(1):57–106 (2000), abstract, https://carta.anthropogeny.org/node/309979.

### In brief
35:27–28 lists colour after colour — fruits, white and red mountain tracts, "extremely black" rock, people and animals — and then ends with knowledge: "Only those fear Allah, from among His servants, who have knowledge."

### Verse note
"Do you not see that Allah sends down rain from the sky, and We produce thereby fruits of varying colors? And in the mountains are tracts, white and red of varying shades and [some] extremely black. And among people and moving creatures and grazing livestock are various colors similarly. Only those fear Allah, from among His servants, who have knowledge. Indeed, Allah is Exalted in Might and Forgiving" (35:27–28), Meccan, in Sura Fāṭir ("The Originator").

- **Key words.** *Mukhtalifan alwānuhā* ("of varying colors"), repeated for fruits, mountains and living things. *Judad* ("tracts"): streaks or paths. *Gharābību sūd* ("extremely black"). *Yakhshā* ("fear"): reverent awe (*khashya*). *Al-ʿulamāʾ* ("those … who have knowledge").
- **Whom it addresses.** "Do you not see" — the listener, invited to look. The verse ties the list of colours directly to the sentence on knowledge.

### Classical tafsir
**Ibn Kathir on 35:27.** "From water He brings forth fruits of various colors, yellow, red, green, white and other colors"; "He created the mountains like this, with different colors, as we also see that there are indeed white and red mountains", and "in some of them there are streaks which are also of varying colors." "Ibn 'Abbas said Al-Judad means pathways." ʿIkrima: "Al-Gharabib means mountains which are high and black." On people and animals: "the same is true of living creatures too, humans and animals", and "animals and cattle vary in their colors, even within one species". He gives examples of human colour from his own world — "Berbers, Ethiopians and some non-Arabs who are very black", "and Slavs and Romans who are very white, and the Arabs who are in between, and the Indians."

**Ibn Kathir on 35:28.** "Only those who have knowledge truly fear Him as He should be feared, because the more they know about the Almighty, All-Powerful, All-Knowing Who has the most perfect attributes and is described with the most beautiful Names, … the more they will fear Him." Ibn ʿAbbas (via ʿAlī ibn Abī Ṭalḥa): they are "those who know that Allah is able to do all things", and "The one among His servants who knows about Ar-Rahman, is the one who does not associate anything in worship with Him". Saʿīd ibn Jubayr: "Fear is what stands between you and disobeying Allah, may He be glorified." Al-Hasan al-Basri: "The knowledgeable person is the one who fears Ar-Rahman with regard to the Unseen".

**Al-Jalālayn on 35:27.** "gharābību sūd … meaning dark-black rocks". *Tanwīr al-Miqbās*: "black mountains, very intense in their blackness".

**Where they differ.** On *judad*: pathways (Ibn ʿAbbas) or streaks (Ibn Kathir's own wording; *Tanwīr*). On "knowledge" in 35:28: knowledge of God's power and attributes (Ibn Kathir; Ibn ʿAbbas), shown in obedience (Saʿīd ibn Jubayr) and in fear of God unseen (al-Hasan). None of them reads "knowledge" here as natural science; that is a modern extension.

### How it works
Each point is tied to "of varying colors".
- **Colour without pigment.** [Established] Morpho butterfly wings "contain no blue pigment — they harness the physics of light at the nanoscale". This is "structural color": "the production of color by nano-sized elaborate shapes that reflect and bend light"; ridges on the scales are "a key component" (KQED, quoting the Patel lab, UC Berkeley). Each scale is a single cell, about 50 microns across and 200–250 microns long.
- **Blue is rare as a pigment.** [Established] "Blue is one the rarest colors made as a pigment"; "Most animals can't produce blue pigments" (Ryan Null, UC Berkeley, in KQED).
- **Change the gaps, change the colour.** [Established] When methanol fills the gaps between a Morpho's scale ridges instead of air, the calculated reflected peak shifts from 452 nm to 545 nm — from blue towards green — because the refractive index of the gaps changes (Kuntzleman, ChemEd X, 2019). The colour is in the shape, not in a dye.
- **Human skin colour.** [Established] Across populations, "skin reflectance is strongly correlated with absolute latitude and UV radiation levels". Jablonski and Chaplin argue that dark skin "protected against UV-induced photolysis of folate", while lighter skin evolved away from the tropics "to permit UVB-induced synthesis of previtamin D(3)" — "a compromise solution" between the two needs (*Journal of Human Evolution*, 2000, abstract).

### Points of awe
- The Morpho's blue is made by shape alone; fill the shape with alcohol instead of air and the blue turns greenish (KQED; Kuntzleman).
- Most animals cannot make blue pigment at all (KQED).
- Human skin colour tracks sunlight closely: a balance between protecting folate and making vitamin D (Jablonski & Chaplin).

### The reflection
35:27 asks a simple question: "Do you not see?" Then it shows us the world as a painter's palette. One rain falls; fruits come out yellow, red, green and white. Mountains run in white and red tracts, and some are "extremely black". People and animals, too, come in "various colors similarly".

Ibn Kathir read this list through the eyes of his own world, naming peoples from Ethiopia to the Slavs. The verse's point is that human colour sits in the same list as the fruits and the hills: a sign of the Creator, not a ranking of people.

Then the verse turns from colour to the one who looks: "Only those fear Allah, from among His servants, who have knowledge." For Ibn Kathir and Ibn ʿAbbas, this is knowledge of God: the more one knows of Him, "the more they will fear Him." A modern reader may extend it, and should say it is an extension: learning how colour works — a butterfly blue made only by shape, a skin tone tuned to sunlight — can deepen the same awe. The danger is the reverse: knowing a lot and seeing nothing. The verse asks for knowledge that leads to reverence.

### Honest notes
- **No popular "miracle" claim** about these verses was found in the sources read. The science here is a way to deepen appreciation, not a fulfilled prediction; the verse celebrates variety and does not describe pigments or structural colour.
- **"Those who have knowledge."** Classically, this means those who know God (Ibn Kathir; Ibn ʿAbbas). Reading it as scientists is a modern extension, and should be labelled as one.
- **Human colour.** Ibn Kathir's examples reflect the peoples known in 14th-century Damascus. The verse itself names no group and ranks no colour. The site should present this verse as a sign of variety, never as a basis for judging people by colour.
- **Not read:** al-Rāzī on 35:27–28; a source on mineral colours in mountains; a source on peacock feathers.

### Questions readers ask next
**Who are "those who have knowledge"?** Classically, those who know God: "those who know that Allah is able to do all things" (Ibn ʿAbbas, via Ibn Kathir). Many today extend it to anyone whose study of nature leads to reverence; that is a modern reading.

**Why mention the colours of people and animals?** The verse puts them in the same list as fruits and mountains, as a sign. Ibn Kathir: "the same is true of living creatures too, humans and animals".

**What are the "tracts" in the mountains?** *Judad*: "pathways" (Ibn ʿAbbas) or streaks (Ibn Kathir; *Tanwīr*); the "extremely black" are high black mountains (ʿIkrima) or dark-black rocks (al-Jalālayn).

**Why is a Morpho butterfly blue?** Not because of pigment. Tiny ridged structures on its scales reflect and bend light (KQED); fill them with methanol and the colour shifts towards green (Kuntzleman).
