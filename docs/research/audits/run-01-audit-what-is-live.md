## Part B
1. M8: "the International Islamic Fiqh Academy of the OIC ruled in 1985 (Resolution 10) that any fixed increase on a loan is forbidden riba" — **Verdict:** Correct in number and year; Needs context in wording — **Evidence:** The Academy's own English text (iifa-aifi.org/en/32234.html) is headed "Resolution No. 10 (10/2)", "Rulings on Usury-based Bank Transactions and Dealing with Islamic Banks". It was adopted at the Council's "2nd session in Jeddah, Saudi Arabia on 10–16 Rabīʿ al-Awwal 1406H (22–28 December 1985)". The operative clause reads: "First: Any increase or interest on a matured debt in exchange for an extension of the maturity date, and in case the borrower is unable to pay and the increase (or interest) on loan at the inception of its agreement are both forms of usury, which are therefore prohibited in Shariah." The word "fixed" is not in the text. The ruling covers two cases: (a) any increase charged for extending a debt that has fallen due, and (b) any increase stipulated on a loan when the contract is made. The resolution also has two more clauses: Shariah-compliant trade as the alternative, and a call to governments to encourage Shariah-compliant financial institutions. One conflict between sources: the Islamic Development Bank's printed collection, *Resolutions and Recommendations of the Council of the Islamic Fiqh Academy 1985–2000*, gives the session dates as "10 to 16 Rabiul Thani 1406 H (22-28 …"; this was seen only in a search snippet. The Academy's own site has Rabīʿ al-Awwal, and both give 22–28 December 1985. — **Fix:** "In December 1985 the International Islamic Fiqh Academy of the Organization of the Islamic Conference (now Cooperation), meeting in Jeddah, adopted Resolution No. 10 (10/2). It declared that any increase charged on a debt in return for extending its due date, and any increase stipulated on a loan when the contract is made, are 'forms of usury, which are therefore prohibited in Shariah'."

2. M8: "Al-Azhar's Islamic Research Academy, in 2002, held fixed returns from bank investment to be permissible" — **Verdict:** Correct, but Needs context — **Evidence:** Mahmoud A. El-Gamal (Rice University) gives a full English translation of the fatwa in "'Interest' and the Paradox of Contemporary Islamic Law and Finance", pp. 8–11 (ruf.rice.edu/~elgamal/files/interest.pdf). The body was the Council of the Islamic Research Institute/Academy (Majmaʿ al-Buḥūth al-Islāmiyya) at Al-Azhar. It was answering a letter dated 22/10/2002 from Dr. Hasan Abbas Zaki, chairman of the International Arab Banking Corporation, about depositors who receive "profit distributions that are pre-determined". The timeline has three steps: (a) on "Thursday, 25 Sha'ban, 1423 A.H., corresponding to 31 October, 2002" the Council "determined that investing funds in banks that pre-specify profits is permissible under Islamic Law"; (b) it approved the written fatwa text on "23 Ramadan 1423, corresponding to 28 November 2002"; (c) the fatwa was signed by the Rector of Al-Azhar, "Dr. Muhammad Sayyid Tantawi, 27 Ramadan 1423 A.H., 2 December 2002 A.D." Its reasoning treats the depositor as an investor and the bank "as an agent who invests the funds on their behalf". It quotes 4:29 and argues that "no Canonical Text in the Book of Allah or the Prophetic Sunnah forbids this type of transaction within which profits or returns are pre-specified". El-Gamal makes three further points (p. 2–3). First, the fatwa deals "exclusively with the relationship between bank depositors and the bank, without addressing the nature of banks' assets". Second, its authors "exerted effort in its wording … to avoid using the term 'interest'", yet the semi-official Egyptian press hailed it as legalising bank interest; El-Gamal (n. 5) names Rose al-Yusuf, 13 December 2002, pp. 18–21: "A slap to the face of extremists and peddlers of religion: Finally, bank interest is permissible". Third, "a month later, in January 2003, the Council of the Islamic Jurisprudence Academy (IJA; Majma' al-Fiqh al-Islamī) issued a rebuttal, reiterating many of the points its members and numerous other jurists had made to reject the Islamic legitimacy of all forms of bank interest". His §5 heading, seen only in a search extract of the PDF, calls this the "Rebuttal by the Islamic Fiqh Institute in Qatar (January 2003)", made at "the Fourteenth Session of Majlis Majma' Al-Fiqh Al-Islami in Doha, Qatar, January 11-16, 2003". El-Gamal also notes (p. 2) that Tantawi "re-iterated a fatwā … that he had issued in 1989, and published in the semi-official newspaper Al-Ahram, when he was the Mufti of Egypt", and (n. 3) that the Pakistani Sharī'a Appellate Court dismissed it as "the solitary opinion of Dr. Tantawi of Egypt". — **Fix:** "In late 2002 the Council of Al-Azhar's Islamic Research Academy (Majmaʿ al-Buḥūth al-Islāmiyya) ruled that placing money with a bank as one's investment agent, in return for a profit fixed in advance, is permissible. The Council agreed this on 31 October 2002 and approved the fatwa text on 28 November. It was signed by the Shaykh of Al-Azhar, Muhammad Sayyid Tantawi, on 2 December 2002. The fatwa deliberately avoided the word 'interest' and dealt only with depositors. The economist Mahmoud El-Gamal reports that in January 2003 the Council of the Islamic Fiqh Academy (Majmaʿ al-Fiqh al-Islāmī), at its fourteenth session in Doha, Qatar (11–16 January 2003), issued a rebuttal rejecting 'the Islamic legitimacy of all forms of bank interest'."

M12: "If the Hour is about to come and one of you has a palm shoot in his hand, let him plant it" (Musnad Ahmad 12902; al-Adab al-Mufrad 479) "graded sahih by al-Albani" — **Verdict:** Numbers correct; wording Needs context; grading Correct but should name both graders — **Evidence:** (a) Musnad Ahmad 12902, as shown on hadithprophet.com (hadith-17025.html), narrated by Anas ibn Malik (ra): «إن قامت على أحدكم القيامة، وفي يده فسيلة فليغرسها» ("If the Resurrection comes upon one of you while there is a palm shoot in his hand, let him plant it"). The editors' note there, from the Shu'ayb al-Arna'ut edition, reads: «إسناده صحيح على شرط مسلم» ("its chain is sound by Muslim's standard"). The note lists al-Bukhari's al-Adab al-Mufrad (479) among other sources and gives a fuller version at Ahmad 12981. (b) al-Adab al-Mufrad 479 (sunnah.com, Book 27, Hadith 4; Arabic/English book reference 479; seen as a search-result extract because the page itself blocked the fetch) reads: "If the Final Hour comes while you have a shoot of a plant in your hands and it is possible to plant it before the Hour comes, you should plant it." Arabic: «إِنْ قَامَتِ السَّاعَةُ وَفِي يَدِ أَحَدِكُمْ فَسِيلَةٌ، فَإِنِ اسْتَطَاعَ أَنْ لاَ تَقُومَ حَتَّى يَغْرِسَهَا». (c) "About to come" is not in either wording. It comes from the commentators: al-Sindi, in his gloss on the Musnad on the same page, explains «إن قامت» as «أي: قربت» ("that is, drew near"), and al-Haythami suggests it means the Hour's signs. (d) Al-Albani: secondary listings that reproduce his grades give "صحيح" in Sahih al-Adab al-Mufrad no. 371 and "إسناده صحيح على شرط مسلم" in al-Silsila al-Sahiha no. 9. These were seen in search extracts only; his books were not opened. — **Fix:** "Anas ibn Malik (ra) reported that the Prophet (pbuh) said: 'If the Final Hour comes while you have a shoot of a plant in your hands and it is possible to plant it before the Hour comes, you should plant it' (al-Bukhari, al-Adab al-Mufrad 479; a shorter wording in Musnad Ahmad 12902, whose chain Shu'ayb al-Arna'ut grades sound by Muslim's standard; al-Albani also grades it sahih). Commentators such as al-Sindi read 'comes' as 'draws near'."

4. M1: al-Ghazali (in the Ihya'), as quoted by al-Nawawi (Riyad al-Salihin, chapter on permitted lying), that lying to protect an innocent person from an oppressor is required — **Verdict:** Wrongly attributed (the passage is real, but Riyad al-Salihin does not quote al-Ghazali); the substance also Needs context — **Evidence:** (a) Al-Ghazali, Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn, Kitāb Āfāt al-Lisān, section «بيان ما رخص فيه من الكذب», vol. 3, p. 137 (al-Maktaba al-Shamila, book 9472, p. 883; same text on Arabic Wikisource): «فنقول الكلام وسيلة إلى المقاصد فكل مقصود محمود يمكن التوصل إليه بالصدق والكذب جميعاً فالكذب فيه حرام وإن أمكن التوصل إليه بالكذب دون الصدق فالكذب فيه مباح إن كان تحصيل ذلك القصد مباحاً وواجب إن كان المقصود واجباً كما أن عصمة دم المسلم واجبة فمهما كان في الصدق سفك دم امرئ مسلم قد اختفى من ظالم فالكذب فيه واجب». In translation: "Speech is a means to ends. Any praiseworthy end that can be reached by both truth and lying, lying is forbidden in it. If it can be reached by lying but not by truth, lying is permitted if the end is permitted, and obligatory if the end is obligatory. Just as protecting a Muslim's life is obligatory, whenever telling the truth would shed the blood of a Muslim who has hidden from an oppressor, lying is obligatory." Just before this, al-Ghazali quotes the early scholar Maymun ibn Mihran, ending «وهذا الكذب واجب» ("and this lie is obligatory"). (b) Al-Nawawi, Riyāḍ al-Ṣāliḥīn, chapter 261 «باب بيان ما يجوز من الكذب» (Islamweb library edition, vol. 1, pp. 359–360; English at riyadh.islamenc.com/en/page/265). Al-Nawawi states the rule in his own voice and does not name al-Ghazali: «ومختصر ذلك: أن الكلام وسيلة إلى المقاصد … وإن كان واجبا كان الكذب واجبا. فإذا اختفى مسلم من ظالم يريد قتله … وجب الكذب بإخفائه». He refers the reader to his own al-Adhkār («قد أوضحتها في كتاب الأذكار»). He adds: «والأحوط في هذا كله أن يوري» ("the more cautious course in all this is tawriya", i.e. ambiguous speech), and that an outright lie «فليس بحرام في هذا الحال» ("is not forbidden in this situation"). (c) Al-Nawawi does quote al-Ghazali by name in al-Adhkār: «وأحسن ما رأيته في ضبطه، ما ذكره الإمام أبو حامد الغزالي». This was seen only in search extracts of islamway.net and islamcontent.com; the full page was not read. (d) Al-Ghazali's example is "a Muslim" (امرئ مسلم) hidden from an oppressor, and al-Nawawi's is "a Muslim" too. "An innocent person" is a modern extension. — **Fix:** "Al-Ghazali (d. 1111), in the Iḥyāʾ (Book of the Banes of the Tongue, vol. 3, p. 137), set out the rule: speech is a means to ends. If a good end can be reached by truth, lying is forbidden. If it can be reached only by lying, lying is permitted when the end is permitted and obligatory when the end is obligatory. His example: 'whenever telling the truth would shed the blood of a Muslim who has hidden from an oppressor, lying is obligatory.' Al-Nawawi (d. 1277) states the same rule and the same case in Riyāḍ al-Ṣāliḥīn (ch. 261), and adds that ambiguous speech (tawriya) is the more cautious course. Applying this to any innocent person is a modern extension of their example."
