## S5 — Sun and moon, each in an orbit
- **Sentence:** The Moon goes once around the sky relative to the stars "in about 27.3 days", but "takes 29.5 days to return to the same point… as referenced to the Sun" (NASA StarChild); new moon to new moon is 29.5 days.
  **Section:** Key points; How it works
  **Verdict:** Confirmed (official dataset)
  **Source:** NASA Goddard, "Moon Fact Sheet" (nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov): revolution period "27.3217" days; synodic period "29.53" days.
  **New text:** The Moon circles the Earth relative to the stars in 27.32 days, but new moon to new moon takes 29.53 days, because the Earth has moved along its own orbit in the meantime (NASA Moon Fact Sheet).

- **Sentence:** The Sun moves "about 230 km/s (828,000 km/h)"; "it still takes us about 230 million years to make one complete orbit" (NASA StarChild; *Wikipedia* gives about 225 million years, citing Leong 2002).
  **Section:** How it works
  **Verdict:** Corrected
  **Source:** Reid et al., "Trigonometric parallaxes of high mass star forming regions: the structure and kinematics of the Milky Way", *Astrophysical Journal* 783:130 (2014), arXiv 1401.5377: distance to the Galactic centre "8.34 +/- 0.16 kpc"; "a circular rotation speed at the Sun… 240 +/- 8 km/s". At these values one circuit (2π × 8.34 kpc ÷ 240 km/s) takes about 214 million years — this is my arithmetic, not the paper's. ("Leong 2002" is a student entry in *The Physics Factbook*, not a research paper.)
  **New text:** Radio measurements put the Sun about 8.3 kiloparsecs (some 27,000 light-years) from the centre of the galaxy, moving at about 240 km/s (Reid et al., *Astrophysical Journal*, 2014). At that speed one circuit takes a little over 200 million years.
