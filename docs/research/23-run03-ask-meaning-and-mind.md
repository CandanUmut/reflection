## Q29 — Why would a self-sufficient God create?

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via the site's verified Tanzil data (`src/data/quran.json`) — 51:56, 51:57, 35:15, 67:2, 2:30, 7:156, 21:107, 17:15, 18:29.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English), on 51:56–58, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-56-sora-51.html.
- IslamWeb fatwa 426097, "True Meaning of Verse {And I Did Not Create the Jinn and Mankind Except to Worship Me}", https://islamweb.net/en/printfatwa.php?id=426097 — reports the views of Mujahid, Ibn Jurayj, ʿAli, al-Zajjaj and Ibn Taymiyya with their sources.

### The question, at its strongest
"A God who needs nothing has no reason to do anything. If He created out of a lack, He is not self-sufficient; if He created for no reason, creation is arbitrary and my life has no point. And the Quran's own answer — 'I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me' — makes it worse: it sounds like a ruler who wants praise. Worse still, many of the beings He made will suffer, and some will end in Hell. How can that be called generosity?"

### Why people ask it
It comes up in crises of meaning — after a loss, in depression, or simply when someone asks "what is the point of me?" It also comes up in philosophy, as a puzzle about how a perfect being can act at all.

### Short answer
Muslim theologians agree that God does not create from need: "Allah is the Free of need" (35:15), and "I do not want from them any provision" (51:57). They disagree about how to describe His reasons. The Ashʿari school says God acts by His will and wisdom but not "for a purpose" that would complete Him; the Maturidi school says His acts are never futile and always wise. Either way, the benefit of creation goes to the creatures, not to God. Worship, in this view, is not a payment God collects but the shape of a life turned toward its source — and classical scholars read 51:56 in several ways, the most common being "to command them to worship Me". The hardest part is suffering and Hell: Islam answers with a creation made for testing (67:2), a mercy that "encompasses all things" (7:156) and outweighs anger, and a rule that no one is punished without a message (17:15). That answer is serious, but it does not remove every difficulty.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If you are asking because your own life feels pointless, that feeling deserves attention before any argument.** Meaning can feel absent even when it is there, especially in grief or depression. If the question comes with hopelessness, thoughts of not wanting to live, or weeks of numbness, speak to a doctor or a counsellor, and tell someone you trust today. In many countries you can call a local crisis line; findahelpline.com lists them by country. Faith and professional help are not rivals.

**The Islamic answer is not "you exist to fill God's need".** It is closer to "you exist because you were given existence, and you were made able to know the One who gave it". You were wanted, not used.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**God is free of need; people are the ones in need.** "O mankind, you are those in need of Allah, while Allah is the Free of need, the Praiseworthy" (35:15). This is from Sura Fatir, a Meccan sura, addressed to all people.

**The key verse, and the verse after it.** "And I did not create the jinn and mankind except to worship Me" (51:56). The next verse removes the idea of need: "I do not want from them any provision, nor do I want them to feed Me" (51:57). Both are from Sura al-Dhariyat, Meccan. The verses before them (51:50–55) call the listener to "flee to Allah" and tell the Prophet (pbuh) to keep reminding people. So the passage is spoken to people who doubt or ignore God, and it ends by denying that God gains anything from them. Ibn Kathir reads 51:56 as: "I, Allah, only created them so that I order them to worship Me, not that I need them", and adds that God "does not need creatures, but rather, they are in need of Him in all conditions".

**Creation as a test.** "[He] who created death and life to test you [as to] which of you is best in deed" (67:2), from Sura al-Mulk, Meccan. This names a purpose that concerns the creature: the chance to do good.

**Human beings as successors.** When God tells the angels, "Indeed, I will make upon the earth a successive authority", they ask, "Will You place upon it one who causes corruption therein and sheds blood…?" God answers: "Indeed, I know that which you do not know" (2:30). The verse (Sura al-Baqara, Medinan) admits the cost of making human beings — corruption and bloodshed — and answers it with God's knowledge rather than with a full explanation.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**Mercy comes first.** The Prophet (pbuh) said: "When Allah completed the creation, He wrote in His Book which is with Him on His Throne, 'My Mercy overpowers My Anger'" ({hadith:bukhari:3194}, narrated by Abu Hurayra). This is the hadith most often quoted when Muslims explain what kind of Creator God is.

**The "hidden treasure" saying is not a hadith.** Many people quote: "I was a hidden treasure; I loved to be known. Hence I created the world so that I would be known" (one of several English versions given by *Wikipedia*). It has no chain of narrators. According to al-ʿAjluni's *Kashf al-Khafaʾ*, Ibn Taymiyya said that "it was not said by the Prophet (peace be upon him)" and that it has "neither Sahih (authentic) nor Daʿif (weak) Sanad (chain of narrators)", and that al-Zarkashi, Ibn Hajar, al-Suyuti and others agreed (as reported by *al-Feqh*). The Hadith Answers team calls it "a fabrication" and says one "should therefore abstain from quoting it", citing *al-Maqasid al-Hasana* (no. 838) and other works. It is popular because it puts into one sentence an idea many Sufis hold: that God created so that He would be known and loved (*Wikipedia*). Its meaning may be defended on other grounds, but it must not be presented as the Prophet's (pbuh) words.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**What 51:56 means: several readings, not one.** According to the IslamWeb fatwa:
- **Mujahid** (d. c. 722, a leading Successor in tafsir), by a chain it calls authentic, said the verse means "to command and forbid them". This is reported as his known opinion.
- **Ibn Jurayj** (d. 767), as reported by Ibn Abi Hatim and cited by Ibn Taymiyya in *Darʾ Taʿaruḍ al-ʿAql wa-l-Naql*, said: "It means, except to know Me."
- **ʿAli ibn Abi Talib** (ra), as reported by Ibn Taymiyya in *Majmuʿ al-Fatawa*: "except to command them to worship Me and call them to My worship". The grammarian **al-Zajjaj** chose the "command" reading too.
- **Ibn Taymiyya** (d. 1328) listed seven readings and preferred: the worship the messengers taught, so the verse means God created them to command them to worship Him alone. He rejected reading "worship" as mere inborn knowledge of God.
- **Ibn ʿAbbas** (ra), via ʿAli ibn Abi Talha, as quoted by Ibn Kathir: "So that they worship Me, willingly or unwillingly."

So "to know Me" is a real classical reading, but it belongs to Ibn Jurayj, not (as often said) to Mujahid, and it is not the majority reading. It is not a modern softening either. The readings can fit together: worship that the messengers taught includes knowing God, since one cannot turn to Someone one does not know.

**Does God act "for a purpose"? The Ashʿari–Maturidi debate.** Kalam (Islamic rational theology) asked whether God's acts have purposes (*taʿlīl al-afʿāl*, "giving reasons for God's acts"). Three positions:
- **The Muʿtazila** (an early rationalist school) interpreted God's acts by "the principles of *ṣalāḥ* and *aṣlaḥ*" (the good and the best), "prioritising human benefit" (Kuyucu, 2026).
- **The Ashʿaris** (the largest Sunni theological school, after al-Ashʿari, d. 936) accept that God's acts are wise, but define wisdom not as benefit but as conformity with God's knowledge and will. They hold that attributing a purpose (*gharaḍ*) to God would imply need, so they prefer to speak of God's will (*irāda*). They also argued that if benefit were the rule, God would have had to create humans directly in Paradise without any trial (Gültekin, 2025, as summarised in the abstract).
- **The Maturidis** (the other main Sunni school, after al-Maturidi, d. 944) "affirm the non-futility of divine acts without reducing them to rational necessity" (Kuyucu, 2026).

**Why it matters.** The Ashʿari position protects God's self-sufficiency: nothing outside God moves Him. The Maturidi position protects the sense that creation has meaning: nothing God does is pointless. A reader who fears that creation is "arbitrary" is closer to the Maturidi worry; a reader who fears that God "needed" us is closer to the Ashʿari one. Both schools deny that God gains anything, and both deny that creation is futile. Ibn al-Qayyim's *Shifāʾ al-ʿAlīl* defends God's wise purposes at length, but it has not yet been consulted.

#### reasoning — How philosophers argue about it
**An argument in five steps.**
1. A being who lacks nothing cannot create to gain something. So if God creates, the point of creating is not in what God receives.
2. Then the point must lie in what is given: existence, life, the ability to know and to choose. Giving without gaining is what we call generosity.
3. Generosity toward beings who can understand includes letting them know the giver. That is why a self-sufficient God can still call people to know and worship Him: the call is for their sake (51:57; Ibn Kathir).
4. Worship, in this sense, is not God's income. It is the creature's right relation to reality — gratitude to the source of everything it has. A person who never thanks anyone is not more free; he is less in touch with what is true.
5. So a self-sufficient God creating is not a contradiction. It is what one would expect if God is good: goodness can give without needing.

**Does "goodness shares itself" make creation necessary?** If goodness *must* share itself, then God *had to* create, and creation would be necessary — which would make God dependent on the world in a new way. Muslim theologians of all schools rejected this: God creates freely, by His will (Ashʿari) and wisdom (Maturidi). The phrase "goodness naturally shares itself" is best read as describing why creation *fits* God's goodness, not why He was forced to create. *Limit:* philosophers dispute whether "free but fitting" is a stable middle position; this page does not settle that.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Commanding worship shows a need for praise."** *Reply:* the Quran denies that God gains anything (51:57; 35:15), and Ibn Kathir reads 51:56 in that light. A doctor who tells a patient to take medicine does not need the patient to recover; the command is for the patient. *Limit:* the analogy breaks where the Quran also threatens punishment for refusing, which a doctor does not do.

**"If God gains nothing from my worship, why punish its absence?"** *Reply:* in the Quran, punishment follows wrongdoing and rejecting a message one has truly received, not failing to "pay" God: "never would We punish until We sent a messenger" (17:15), and "whoever wills — let him believe; and whoever wills — let him disbelieve" (18:29). Refusing worship, in this view, is refusing the truth about oneself, and the harm falls on the one who refuses ("whoever errs only errs against it", 17:15). *Limit:* many readers still find eternal punishment for unbelief hard to reconcile with this, and the site's page on eternal Hell takes up the question at length.

**"If God creates from generosity, why create beings who will suffer, or go to Hell? Is that generous?"** *Reply:* the Islamic answer has three parts. First, a world with real choice and testing (67:2) is a world where harm is possible; the angels themselves raised this objection, and God answered that He knows what they do not (2:30). The Ashʿari argument that God could have created humans directly in Paradise — and chose not to — shows that the tradition saw this question clearly (Gültekin, 2025). Second, mercy is the governing attribute: "My mercy encompasses all things" (7:156), and "My Mercy overpowers My Anger" ({hadith:bukhari:3194}). Third, no one is punished without a message (17:15), and judgment of any person belongs to God. *Limit:* this is not a full explanation. It asks for trust that God's knowledge includes reasons we cannot see — the same move as "I know that which you do not know" (2:30). For someone who does not already trust God, that may not satisfy.

#### views — The range of views
- **Ashʿaris** — God acts by will and wisdom, not for a purpose that would complete Him (Gültekin; Kuyucu).
- **Maturidis** — God's acts are never futile; wisdom without rational necessity (Kuyucu).
- **Muʿtazila** — God acts for the good of creatures (*ṣalāḥ/aṣlaḥ*) (Kuyucu).
- **On 51:56** — "to command them to worship Me" (ʿAli, Mujahid, al-Zajjaj, Ibn Taymiyya); "to know Me" (Ibn Jurayj); "willingly or unwillingly" (Ibn ʿAbbas, via Ibn Kathir).
- **Sufi thought** — creation so that God might be known and loved, often expressed through the "hidden treasure" saying, which has no chain.

### Questions readers ask next
**What does Islam mean by worship, beyond ritual prayer?** In the classical readings above, worship (*ʿibāda*) is what the messengers taught: belief, prayer, and obedience to God's commands in daily life. Many scholars describe any good act done for God's sake as worship; that wider sense has not yet been checked in a named source.

**What is the "hidden treasure" saying?** "I was a hidden treasure; I loved to be known. Hence I created the world so that I would be known." Hadith experts (Ibn Taymiyya, al-Zarkashi, Ibn Hajar, al-Suyuti and others) say it has no chain and is not from the Prophet (pbuh).

**Who are the Ashʿaris and Maturidis?** The two main Sunni schools of theology, named after al-Ashʿari (d. 936) and al-Maturidi (d. 944). On this question, the Ashʿaris stress that God needs nothing; the Maturidis stress that nothing God does is pointless.

**Is "worship means knowing God" a real reading?** Yes, from Ibn Jurayj (via Ibn Abi Hatim and Ibn Taymiyya). The more common reading is "to command them to worship Me".

**Does God need me?** No. The Quran says the opposite (35:15; 51:57). You need Him, and He gives without needing.

**If God knew some would go to Hell, why create them?** This is the hardest form of the question. The tradition answers with a real test (67:2), mercy that outweighs anger ({hadith:bukhari:3194}), no punishment without a message (17:15), and God's knowledge beyond ours (2:30). It does not claim to remove the difficulty entirely.

### Honest limits
The classical schools agree that God creates without need, but they explain His reasons differently, and none claims to know them fully. The answer to suffering and Hell relies partly on trusting God's knowledge, which will not persuade someone who does not already trust Him. Ibn al-Qayyim's *Shifāʾ al-ʿAlīl* and al-Tabari's tafsir of 51:56 have not yet been consulted.

## Q33 — Miracles vs natural laws

### Sources read

- Alin C. Cucu and J. Brian Pitts, "How Dualists Should (Not) Respond to the Objection from Energy Conservation", *Mind & Matter* 17:1 (2019), pp. 95–121, https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.13643 — used for what conservation laws do and do not forbid.

### The question, at its strongest
"Science works because nature is regular. Every time we have checked, dead people stay dead, water does not turn into wine, and the moon does not split. Hume showed that a report of a miracle should never convince a reasonable person: the chance that the witnesses were mistaken or lying is always greater than the chance that the laws of nature were broken. Every religion reports miracles, and they cancel each other out. And if God made the laws, why would He break them?"

### Why people ask it
Students in science and engineering learn that nature follows laws, then hear religious stories that seem to deny this. Hume's essay is widely taught and widely quoted online, often in one sentence.

### Short answer
Hume's argument is stronger than one sentence, but it does not show that miracles are impossible; at most, it sets a high bar for believing reports of them, and the height of the bar depends on whether one already thinks God exists. The philosopher Peter Millican, a leading Hume scholar, concludes that Hume's maxim as stated is mistaken, though his stress on how often reports go wrong is valuable. The Islamic tradition has its own account: for the Ashʿari theologians and al-Ghazali, the regularity of nature is God's custom (*ʿāda*), so a miracle is not a broken machine but God acting otherwise than usual, as a sign. The Quran itself refuses many demanded miracles, says that signs come only from God, and presents the Quran as the sign that is "sufficient" (29:51). A miracle, if it happens, need not break a conservation law in the sense physics uses, since those laws describe closed systems.

### The full answer

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The Quran calls miracles "signs" (*āyāt*).** The same word, *āya*, is used for a verse of the Quran, for a miracle and for a feature of nature that points to God. A miracle in the Quran is a sign that addresses people, not a show of power for its own sake.

**It records demands for miracles, and refuses them.** In Sura al-Isra (Meccan), the Prophet's (pbuh) opponents say: "We will not believe you until you break open for us from the ground a spring. / Or [until] you have a garden of palm tress and grapes and make rivers gush forth within them in force [and abundance] / Or you make the heaven fall upon us in fragments as you have claimed or you bring Allah and the angels before [us] / Or you have a house of gold or you ascend into the sky. And [even then], we will not believe in your ascension…" (17:90–93). The end of 17:93, with the Prophet's (pbuh) reply, have not yet been consulted in full.

**It gives a reason for the refusal.** "And nothing has prevented Us from sending signs except that the former peoples denied them. And We gave Thamud the she-camel as a visible sign, but they wronged her. And We send not the signs except as a warning" (17:59). Ibn Kathir reports from Imam Ahmad that Ibn ʿAbbas (ra) said the Quraysh asked: "Ask your Lord to turn As-Safa' into gold and we will believe in you", and that God offered the Prophet (pbuh) a choice, warning that if they then disbelieved, "they will be destroyed as the nations before them were destroyed"; the Prophet (pbuh) chose to give them more time. So in the Quran's own account, a demanded miracle is dangerous for those who demand it.

**It says miracles do not force belief.** "And they swear by Allah their strongest oaths that if a sign came to them, they would surely believe in it. Say, 'The signs are only with Allah.' And what will make you perceive that even if a sign came, they would not believe" (6:109); "And even if We had sent down to them the angels [with the message] and the dead spoke to them [of it] and We gathered together every [created] thing in front of them, they would not believe unless Allah should will" (6:111). These are Meccan verses about the Prophet's (pbuh) opponents.

**It presents the Quran as the central sign.** "But they say, 'Why are not signs sent down to him from his Lord?' Say, 'The signs are only with Allah, and I am only a clear warner.' And is it not sufficient for them that We revealed to you the Book which is recited to them?" (29:50–51), from Sura al-ʿAnkabut, Meccan. And: "Say, 'If mankind and the jinn gathered in order to produce the like of this Qur'an, they could not produce the like of it…'" (17:88); compare 2:23. The Quran also tells of miracles given to earlier prophets — Moses' (pbuh) staff, Jesus' (pbuh) healing of the sick — but in the Prophet's (pbuh) own case it points first to the Book.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Occasionalism in plain words.** Occasionalism is the view that God is "the one and only true cause" (Lee, SEP). When fire touches cotton and the cotton burns, the fire is the occasion; God creates the burning. Al-Ghazali (d. 1111) argued this in the seventeenth discussion of *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* (*The Incoherence of the Philosophers*), a book attacking the Aristotelian philosophers of his day, especially Ibn Sina. His point, as Lee summarises it: observation shows that two events happen together, not that one *necessarily* produces the other; a fire could, if God willed, fail to burn dry cotton. The connections we see are "due to the prior decree of God" (Lee).

**Habit, not necessity.** The Ashʿari theologians called nature's regularity God's custom (*ʿāda*). Because God's custom is constant, we can rely on it and do science; because it is a custom and not a necessity, God can act otherwise, as in the miracles of prophets. Al-Ghazali's own example in the *Tahāfut* is Abraham (pbuh) thrown into the fire and not burned (the Quran, 21:68–69; not quoted here).

**Did al-Ghazali deny all natural causes?** Scholars disagree. Michael Marmura read him as a full occasionalist; others hold that he "leaves open the possibility of some kind of genuine causality in creatures" (Lee, SEP). Adamson translates his view of natural causes this way: "their effects only proceed if the true Agent who gave them their natures wishes it." On this reading, things have real natures, but those natures work only by God's will.

**Ibn Rushd's reply.** Ibn Rushd (Averroes, d. 1198), the Andalusian philosopher and judge, answered in *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut* (*The Incoherence of the Incoherence*). As Adamson presents it, his key principle is that "certain (*yaqīnī*) knowledge is the knowledge of the thing according to what it is in itself"; if things have no fixed natures, then, he charges, "there is no fixed (*thābit*) knowledge of anything". In other words: deny causes, and you lose science.

**Why this matters to Muslims today.** The debate frames two ways a Muslim can relate faith and science. One stresses God's freedom, and treats laws as reliable habits. The other stresses fixed natures, and treats God as the giver of those natures. Both accept miracles; both accept science. They differ on what a "law of nature" is.

#### reasoning — How philosophers argue about it
**Hume's argument, in full.** Hume (1711–1776) defines a miracle as "a violation of the laws of nature", and argues that because those laws are established by "a uniform experience", there "must, therefore, be a uniform experience against every miraculous event" (E 10.12). His example: "it is a miracle, that a dead man should come to life; because that has never been observed, in any age or country" (E 10.12). His maxim: "That no testimony is sufficient to establish a miracle, unless the testimony be of such a kind, that its falsehood would be more miraculous, than the fact, which it endeavours to establish" (E 10.13). In Part 2 he adds four reasons why, in practice, the testimony never meets that bar:
1. No miracle in history is "attested by a sufficient number of men" of good sense, education and integrity (E 10.15).
2. "The passion of surprize and wonder, arising from miracles, being an agreeable emotion" makes people eager to believe and repeat such stories (E 10.16).
3. Miracle stories "are observed chiefly to abound among ignorant and barbarous nations" (E 10.20).
4. The miracles of different religions cancel each other: "all the prodigies of different religions are to be regarded as contrary facts" (E 10.24).

**Where Hume is open to challenge.** Millican, who edited the *Enquiry* for Oxford, gives a careful assessment:
- **The maxim fails as stated.** Hume compares the reliability of the witnesses with the improbability of the event. Millican shows that this rule gives wrong answers for ordinary improbable events, like lottery results, because it ignores how many different ways a false report could arise. He offers a "Revised Humean Maxim" instead (§§2–3).
- **The question-begging charge.** If "uniform experience" means that *no one has ever* seen a dead man rise, then citing it against a resurrection report assumes the report is false. Millican says the better point is that the Christian (or Muslim) claims a special divine intervention, which is compatible with accepting that the dead normally stay dead (§4). The evidence for a miracle is the witness reports and physical traces, not the evidence for the natural law: "There is no serious dilemma here, just distinct bodies of evidence" (§4).
- **The real issue is prior belief.** Millican concludes that whether to accept a miracle report depends on whether there is good evidence for supernatural intervention in general, and that the dispute between naturalism and theism "cannot be settled by inductive evidence alone". A successful argument for God would raise the prior probability of a miracle (§6).

**So where does Hume "assume what he needs to prove"?** In treating "uniform experience against every miraculous event" as already known. Whether experience is uniform is exactly what a miracle report disputes. Hume's Part 2 points are stronger: they are real reasons to be cautious about miracle stories, and Muslim hadith scholars, who judged reports by the reliability and number of their narrators, would agree with much of that caution.

**Why would God break His own habits?** In the Islamic view, a miracle is not a repair of a faulty machine. It is a sign that addresses people at a particular moment, usually to confirm a messenger, and the Quran says, "We send not the signs except as a warning" (17:59). A habit that is never broken teaches that God is reliable; a rare, meaningful exception teaches that He is free and present. Both are part of the message.

#### science — What science says
**Laws describe; they do not command.** [Established, as a point about scientific practice.] A law of nature, in the sense scientists use, is a description of how systems behave, tested by observation. It says what happens when nothing else intervenes. Whether there is anything that *could* intervene is a philosophical question, not one a law can answer.

**Conservation laws and miracles.** [Debated, as philosophy of physics.] The best-known worry is that a miracle would break the conservation of energy. Cucu and Pitts (2019), in a paper on the parallel question for the mind, note that energy conservation follows from a symmetry in time (Noether's theorem) and holds for closed systems. Their conclusion for the mind is that "energy is conserved when and where minds do not act on bodies, but is not conserved when and where minds act on bodies", and that no *a priori* objection from conservation survives. *Extension (not their claim):* the same reasoning would apply to God acting in the world: a conservation law says what happens in a closed system; a miracle is, by definition, the system not being closed. Physics cannot show that such an event happened, but its laws do not show that it cannot.


#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Uniform experience shows the dead stay dead."** *Reply:* uniform experience shows what happens without God's special action. A miracle claim is a claim about God's special action, and has to be judged by the evidence for it and by the prior case for God (Millican, §§4, 6). *Limit:* if you have no prior reason to believe in God, Hume's caution remains reasonable.

**"Every religion reports miracles, so they cancel out."** *Reply:* the Quran itself does not rest the case for Islam on reported wonders; it rests it first on the Book (29:51) and on the character of the message. Competing miracle reports show that people are credulous, which Islam agrees is a danger. *Limit:* Muslims do report miracles of the Prophet (pbuh) in hadith (for example the splitting of the moon, linked to 54:1), and those reports face Hume's challenge like any others. They were not examined.

**"Occasionalism destroys science."** *Reply:* the Ashʿari view keeps the regularity that science needs — God's custom is constant — and only denies that it is necessary. Many readers of al-Ghazali hold that he allowed real natures acting by God's will (Lee; Adamson). *Limit:* Ibn Rushd's objection, that knowledge needs fixed natures, still has defenders, and the debate continues.

#### views — The range of views
- **Hume** — testimony can never establish a miracle as a basis for religion (*Enquiry* §10).
- **Millican** — Hume's maxim fails as stated; the real question is prior evidence for supernatural action.
- **Al-Ghazali and the Ashʿaris** — natural regularity is God's custom; miracles are signs.
- **Ibn Rushd** — things have real natures; denying causes undermines knowledge.
- **The Quran** — miracles are signs from God, do not compel belief, and the Book is the central sign.

### Questions readers ask next
**What is the *Tahāfut*?** *Tahāfut al-Falāsifa* (*The Incoherence of the Philosophers*), al-Ghazali's attack on the Aristotelian philosophers. Ibn Rushd answered with *Tahāfut al-Tahāfut*. Their debate on causality is still read today.

**What are occasionalism and "secondary causality"?** Occasionalism: God alone truly causes; created things are occasions. Secondary causality: created things truly cause, but only because God gave them their natures and sustains them.

**Which miracles does Islam actually claim?** The Quran describes miracles of earlier prophets (Moses' staff, Abraham in the fire, Jesus' healing). For the Prophet (pbuh), it points to the Quran itself (29:51; 17:88). Hadith reports describe others; their evidence was not assessed here.

**Do Muslims see the Quran as the main miracle?** The Quran presents itself that way (29:50–51; 2:23), and the classical study of the Quran's inimitability (*iʿjāz*) was mainly about its language (see "Are 'scientific miracles' in the Quran real?").

**Does a miracle break the law of conservation of energy?** Not in the sense physics uses: those laws describe closed systems, and a miracle claims that the system was not closed. Physics cannot confirm that; it does not rule it out either.

**If God works through habits, why break them?** As a sign, at a moment that matters, usually to confirm a messenger — and, in the Quran, as a warning (17:59).

### Honest limits
Accepting a miracle depends heavily on whether one already has reasons to believe in God; this page does not claim otherwise. The Quran's refusal to perform demanded miracles is itself something skeptics point to. Al-Ghazali's exact words in the seventeenth discussion have not yet been consulted in translation; his view is given through Lee and Adamson. The miracle reports in hadith were not assessed.

## Q34 — Consciousness: can matter explain the mind?

### Sources read
- Saheeh International via the site's Tanzil data — 17:85, 15:29, 39:42.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English), on 17:85, https://quran.com/17:85/tafsirs/en-tafisr-ibn-kathir.
- Quran.com, hadith listed for 17:85, https://quran.com/en/al-isra/85/hadith — Sahih al-Bukhari 7297 (Ibn Masʿud).
- David J. Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness", *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 2:3 (1995), pp. 200–219, https://consc.net/papers/facing.html.
- PhilPapers Survey 2020, results, https://survey2020.philpeople.org/survey/results/all.
- Christof Koch, Marcello Massimini, Mélanie Boly and Giulio Tononi, "Neural correlates of consciousness: progress and problems", *Nature Reviews Neuroscience* 17 (2016), pp. 307–321, https://www.nature.com/articles/nrn.2016.22.pdf.
- Cogitate Consortium, "Adversarial testing of global neuronal workspace and integrated information theories of consciousness", *Nature* (2025), via the Max Planck Institute press release, https://idw-online.de/en/news851431 (the paper itself was not read).
- Yair Pinto et al., "Split brain: divided perception but undivided consciousness", *Brain* 140:5 (2017), pp. 1231–1237, https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28122878/ — abstract.
- Cucu and Pitts, "How Dualists Should (Not) Respond to the Objection from Energy Conservation", *Mind & Matter* 17:1 (2019), https://arxiv.org/pdf/1909.13643.
- Rahimah Hussin, "Ibn Qayyim al-Jawziyyah: Conception of the Soul and Its Relevance to Contemporary Psychology", *IIUM Journal of Human Sciences* 8:1 (2026), pp. 162–180, https://journals.iium.edu.my/irkh/index.php/ijohs/article/download/413/296/2897.
- IslamWeb fatwa 325312, "Contents of Kitaab Ar-Rooh by Ibn Al-Qayyim", https://www.islamweb.net/en/printfatwa.php?id=325312.

### The question, at its strongest
"Every year neuroscience explains more. Damage one part of the brain and a person loses the ability to recognise faces; damage another and their personality changes. Anaesthesia switches consciousness off in minutes. Drugs change mood, memory and even moral judgement. If the self can be dimmed, split and altered by chemicals and injuries, then the self *is* the brain. 'Soul' is a word for what we have not yet explained — a soul of the gaps that will shrink as science grows. And if a machine one day acts conscious, the soul will have nothing left to do."

### Why people ask it
Neuroscience and artificial intelligence are in the news every day, and many people meet the question in a psychology or biology course. Others meet it at a hospital bedside, watching dementia take away a parent's memory.

### Short answer
The evidence that the mind depends on the brain is strong, and no honest answer denies it. What science has not explained is why any of this brain activity is *experienced* at all — what the philosopher David Chalmers called "the problem of experience", or the hard problem. The leading scientific theories are still being tested, and in a 2025 test neither of the two main theories passed all its predictions. Among philosophers, about half accept physicalism and about a third reject it (PhilPapers 2020). The Quran says that the *rūḥ* (spirit or soul) "is of the affair of my Lord" and that human knowledge is "a little" (17:85); classical scholars such as Ibn al-Qayyim held that the soul is created and depends on God, and is joined to the body. Dependence of the mind on the brain fits that view too: a soul joined to a body acts through it. The hard problem does not prove a soul; it shows that "matter explains the mind" has not been shown either.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If you are watching someone you love lose their memory to dementia, or lose consciousness at the end of life, this question is not abstract.** The Islamic view is that a person is more than what the illness takes. The Quran describes God taking souls in death and in sleep, keeping some and releasing others (39:42); in that picture, a person whose brain is failing has not stopped being someone before God. You can keep speaking to them, reciting, holding their hand. Ask the care team about palliative and memory-care support, and about support for carers; caring for someone with dementia is exhausting, and asking for help is not failure.

**If the question itself makes you anxious — "am I just chemistry?" — notice that you are asking it.** The fact that there is someone asking is what the hard problem is about.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The key verse.** "And they ask you, [O Muhammad], about the soul. Say, 'The soul is of the affair of my Lord. And mankind have not been given of knowledge except a little'" (17:85). Saheeh International translates *rūḥ* here as "the soul"; the Arabic *min amri rabbī* is rendered "of the affair of my Lord". The word *amr* means both "affair, matter" and "command".

**Its setting.** Sura al-Isra is Meccan, but the best-known report places this question in Medina. Ibn Masʿud (ra) reports that the Prophet (pbuh) "passed by a group of Jews and some of them said to the other, Ask him (the Prophet) about the spirit", and others said: "Do not ask him, lest he should tell you what you dislike" ({hadith:bukhari:7297}; also Bukhari 125). Ibn Kathir notes the problem — a Meccan sura, a Medinan question — and suggests either that the verse was revealed a second time in Medina, or that the Prophet (pbuh) was inspired to answer with a verse already revealed. He also cites a report from ʿIkrima (via Ibn Jarir) in which the questioners say they have been given the Torah, and the reply is that their knowledge is little compared with God's.

**What *rūḥ* means here.** The abridged Ibn Kathir read for this page discusses the *rūḥ* as the human soul; it reports al-Suhayli describing it as "light and soft, like air", and treats the *nafs* (self) as the *rūḥ* joined to the body. Other classical readings of *rūḥ* in this verse (as the angel Gabriel, or as the Quran) are reported by other commentators but has not yet been checked.

**Other verses.** God says of Adam: "when I have proportioned him and breathed into him of My [created] soul" (15:29) — Saheeh adds "[created]" to make clear that the spirit is not a part of God. And: "Allah takes the souls at the time of their death, and those that do not die [He takes] during their sleep" (39:42).

**What the verse does and does not say.** It says the spirit belongs to God's *amr* and that human knowledge is limited. It does not describe how the spirit relates to the brain, and it does not forbid studying the mind. Reading it as a statement about the hard problem is a modern application.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Ibn al-Qayyim's *Kitāb al-Rūḥ*.** Ibn al-Qayyim (d. 1350), a Hanbali scholar and student of Ibn Taymiyya, wrote *Kitāb al-Rūḥ* (*The Book of the Spirit*), the best-known classical work on the soul. According to Hussin (2026): he used *nafs*, *rūḥ*, *qalb* (heart), *ʿaql* (intellect) for the soul and "argued that the difference between the terminologies is its attributes, not entity" (p. 174); the soul "is created by Allah" (p. 174) and is "self-standing and independent of the physical body" (p. 175); in sleep "the soul does not entirely leave the body, unlike when one is dead" (p. 175); after death it returns to the body in the grave for questioning (p. 175). An IslamWeb summary adds that he held "the souls are not like the bodies".

**Are *rūḥ* and *nafs* the same?** For Ibn al-Qayyim, they name one thing under different descriptions (Hussin, p. 174). Ibn Kathir's abridgement similarly treats *nafs* as the *rūḥ* joined to the body. Other scholars distinguished them; that range has not yet been checked.

**Kalam debates.** Muslim theologians disagreed on whether the soul is a subtle body, an accident of the body, or (with the philosophers such as Ibn Sina) an immaterial substance. These positions have not yet been consulted in primary sources.

**Does Islam require a soul separate from the body?** Islam teaches that the person continues after death and will be raised (39:42; the afterlife page). That requires that the person can exist and be restored by God. It does not, by itself, settle *how* the soul relates to the brain in this life. Some classical theologians held views closer to "the soul is a subtle body"; Ibn al-Qayyim held it is created and self-standing. A Muslim who accepts that the mind depends on the brain *in this life* is within that range, as long as he affirms that God sustains the person and raises them. *Limit:* a view that says the person simply *is* the brain and ends with it contradicts Islamic teaching on the afterlife.

#### science — What science says
**The mind depends on the brain.** [Established.] Koch, Massimini, Boly and Tononi (2016) review the evidence: damage to the brainstem and thalamus is tied to loss of consciousness; a combined TMS–EEG measure can tell whether a person is conscious — awake, in deep sleep or under anaesthesia — "at the single-person level". They locate the best candidates for the brain areas that underlie experience in "a posterior cortical hot zone that includes sensory areas".

**But not in the simple way people assume.** [Moderate.] The same review cites a patient with near-complete removal of both frontal lobes who was "clearly conscious", and a patient born without a cerebellum who lives a normal life. In a study of two split-brain patients (whose brain halves were surgically separated), Pinto and colleagues (2017) found that perception was split but the patients did not behave like "two separate conscious perceivers" (*Brain*). These findings make the picture more complicated, not less physical.

**The hard problem, in plain words.** [Established as a problem; Debated as to its solution.] Chalmers (1995) separated the "easy problems" — explaining "the ability to discriminate, categorize, and react to environmental stimuli", "the reportability of mental states", "the focus of attention" — from the hard one: "The really hard problem of consciousness is the problem of experience." The easy problems "are easy precisely because they concern the explanation of cognitive abilities and functions": explain the mechanism and you have explained the function. But you could explain every mechanism and still ask: why is it like something, from the inside, to be this brain?

**The theories, and their status.** [Debated.] The Cogitate Consortium tested two leading theories in a preregistered study of 256 people in seven laboratories, using fMRI, MEG and intracranial EEG (*Nature*, 2025, as reported by the Max Planck Institute). Integrated Information Theory predicted sustained synchronisation in posterior brain regions; "the data did not support this". Global Neuronal Workspace Theory predicted a burst of activity ("ignition") when an experience ends; this "could not be confirmed". One researcher noted: "There are over twenty theories of consciousness out there. We've tested two." These theories try to say which brain activity goes with experience; they do not claim to have solved why there is experience at all.

**What philosophers think.** [Survey data.] In the 2020 PhilPapers survey of 1,733 philosophers, 51.93% accepted or leaned to physicalism about the mind, 32.08% to non-physicalism, and 16.56% chose "other". The same survey found 18.93% theists and 66.95% atheists — so many philosophers who reject physicalism are not religious.

#### reasoning — How philosophers argue about it
**Why dependence does not settle the question.** A radio's sound depends on its circuits: damage them and the sound distorts or stops. That does not show that the music is made by the radio. The analogy is imperfect, and physicalists reject it, but it shows that "A depends on B" and "A is B" are different claims. The evidence from lesions and anaesthesia supports the first. Whether it supports the second depends on whether the hard problem can be solved in physical terms.

**The interaction problem.** If the soul is not physical, how does it move the body? A common version says a non-physical mind acting on the brain would break the conservation of energy. Cucu and Pitts (2019) argue that this objection fails *a priori*: conservation follows from a symmetry that holds only where nothing else acts, so "energy is conserved when and where minds do not act on bodies, but is not conserved when and where minds act on bodies". They add that "a decent objection to interactionism should be a posteriori, based on empirically studying the brain." *Limit:* this answers the energy objection only; it does not explain *how* interaction works, which remains unexplained.

**The Islamic angle on interaction.** For an occasionalist (see "Miracles vs natural laws"), the problem looks different: no created thing causes anything by its own power, so the soul does not need a mechanism to move the body; God creates the movement in line with the soul's will. *Limit:* this moves the question to God's action rather than answering it in terms a scientist could test.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"Brain damage, anaesthesia and drugs change who we are, so the mind is the brain."** *Reply:* they show dependence, which Islam accepts: the soul in this life is joined to the body and acts through it (Ibn Kathir on *nafs*; Ibn al-Qayyim). They do not show identity, and the hard problem — why the activity is experienced — remains open (Chalmers). *Limit:* a physicalist will reply that the hard problem is a confusion or will be solved, and that is a live view held by about half of philosophers.

**"The soul is a soul of the gaps."** *Reply:* a "gap" argument says "science can't explain X, so God did it". The hard problem is different: it is a question about what kind of explanation could work at all, raised by philosophers many of whom are atheists. *Limit:* if a physical explanation of experience were found, this reply would lose its force. The Islamic belief in the soul does not rest on the hard problem, but this page's argument does.

**"If AI seems conscious, the soul has nothing left to do."** *Reply:* seeming conscious is one of the "easy problems" — behaviour and report. Whether a machine *experiences* anything is the hard problem again, and no current theory can settle it (Cogitate, 2025). *Limit:* no Islamic source read for this page addresses machine consciousness directly, and Muslims hold different views on it.

#### views — The range of views
- **Physicalism** — the mind is a physical process (51.93% of philosophers, PhilPapers 2020).
- **Non-physicalism** (dualism and others) — experience is not fully physical (32.08%).
- **Ibn al-Qayyim** — the soul is created, self-standing, one thing under the names *rūḥ* and *nafs* (Hussin, 2026).
- **Interactionists such as Cucu and Pitts** — energy conservation does not rule out a mind acting on the brain.

### Questions readers ask next
**What exactly is the "hard problem"?** Why brain activity is *experienced* — why it feels like something from the inside — as opposed to how the brain performs its functions (Chalmers, 1995).

**Could a Muslim accept that the mind comes from the brain?** A Muslim can accept that the mind *depends on* the brain in this life. Islamic teaching on death and resurrection requires that God preserves and restores the person; a view that the person simply ends with the brain contradicts it.

**What do *rūḥ* and *nafs* mean?** *Rūḥ*: spirit or soul. *Nafs*: self or soul. Ibn al-Qayyim held they are the same thing described in different ways; Ibn Kathir's abridgement treats the *nafs* as the *rūḥ* joined to the body.

**What does *amr* mean in 17:85?** "Affair" or "command". Saheeh International renders it "of the affair of my Lord"; the verse places the soul in God's domain and says human knowledge of it is little.

**If AI one day seems conscious, what would that mean for the soul?** That it behaves as if conscious. Whether it experiences anything is an open question that neither science nor this page can answer today.

**Who was asking about the soul in 17:85?** In Ibn Masʿud's report, a group of Jews in Medina ({hadith:bukhari:7297}).

### Honest limits
The hard problem does not prove that a soul exists; it shows that the physicalist explanation is not complete. The scientific theories are changing quickly. The classical Islamic discussion of the soul is much richer than what could be read here: the kalam positions, the full *Kitāb al-Rūḥ*, and the full tafsir of 17:85 were read only through summaries. The Cogitate results come from a press release, not the paper.

## Q26 — Isn't belief just upbringing?

### The question, at its strongest
"If I had been born in Tamil Nadu, I would almost certainly be Hindu; in Utah, a Mormon; in Riyadh, a Muslim. My reasons feel convincing to me, but a Hindu's reasons feel convincing to him. So my feeling that my reasons are good is exactly what I would have if my belief came only from my upbringing. Saying 'that's the genetic fallacy' misses the point: I'm not saying my belief is false because of where it came from — I'm saying I have no way of telling, from the inside, whether my reasons are real or just my upbringing talking."

### Why people ask it
University students meet people raised in other faiths, often for the first time, and notice how much of their own belief came with their family. The question is sharper for Muslims because the Quran itself criticises people who follow their fathers' religion.

### Short answer
Upbringing shapes belief strongly; the data and the Prophet (pbuh) both say so. The weak form of the objection ("where a belief came from shows it is false") is a fallacy. The strong form ("I can't tell whether my reasons are real") is serious, and it is the question al-Ghazali faced in the eleventh century: he saw that "Christian youths always grew up to be Christians, Jewish youths to be Jews and Muslim youths to be Muslims", doubted everything he had inherited, and came out with a faith he had tested. The answer is not to stop believing — the same doubt would undermine moral, political and even atheist beliefs, which are also shaped by upbringing — but to examine one's reasons. The Quran rebukes not inherited belief as such, but refusing to look at evidence because "we found our fathers" doing otherwise (2:170; 43:22–24). And most classical theologians held that the faith of someone who has not reasoned it through is still valid, though they urged people to think.

### The full answer

#### heart — For the heart and mind
**If this question has unsettled you, you are in good company.** One of the greatest theologians in Islamic history began his search exactly here. Doubt that leads to examination is not a betrayal of faith.

**Owning your faith does not mean starting from zero.** It means asking, of what you were given: what are my reasons now, and do they hold? Some of what you inherited may turn out to be custom rather than religion; some may turn out to be truer than you knew.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**The Quran criticises following fathers against evidence.** "And when it is said to them, 'Follow what Allah has revealed,' they say, 'Rather, we will follow that which we found our fathers doing.' Even though their fathers understood nothing, nor were they guided?" (2:170). This is Medinan, in Sura al-Baqara, about people who held to inherited practices.

**It says this happened to every prophet.** "Rather, they say, 'Indeed, we found our fathers upon a religion, and we are in their footsteps [rightly] guided.' And similarly, We did not send before you any warner into a city except that its affluent said, 'Indeed, we found our fathers upon a religion, and we are, in their footsteps, following.' [Each warner] said, 'Even if I brought you better guidance than that [religion] upon which you found your fathers?'" (43:22–24), from Sura al-Zukhruf, Meccan. Ibn Kathir reads it as addressed to idolaters who "have no grounds for their belief in idolatry apart from the fact that they are imitating their fathers", making "their claim with no evidence".

**What the verses criticise.** Not belief that one learned from one's parents, but refusing to consider "better guidance" when it is brought (43:24). This applies to Muslims too: a Muslim who would refuse to look at evidence because "this is what my family believes" is doing what the verse describes.

**An innate disposition.** "[Adhere to] the fitrah of Allah upon which He has created [all] people" (30:30). *Fiṭra* means the original nature God gave people, which the tradition reads as an inclination toward God.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**The Prophet (pbuh) himself said parents shape a child's religion.** "Every child is born with a true faith of Islam (i.e. to worship none but Allah Alone) and his parents convert him to Judaism or Christianity or Magianism" ({hadith:bukhari:1385}; the words in brackets are the translator's). This hadith *agrees* with the objection that upbringing shapes belief. What it adds is a claim about what lies underneath: a natural disposition (*fiṭra*) that upbringing builds on or redirects. On this view, upbringing explains the differences between religions, while *fiṭra* explains why belief in God is so widespread.

#### scholars — How scholars have understood it
**Al-Ghazali's story.** Al-Ghazali (d. 1111) tells in *al-Munqidh min al-Dalal* (*Deliverance from Error*) that from youth he wanted to know "the true meaning of things". He writes: "I saw that Christian youths always grew up to be Christians, Jewish youths to be Jews and Muslim youths to be Muslims", and recalled the hadith that everyone is born with a sound nature (Watt, section I). He resolved to find knowledge "in which the thing known is made so manifest that no doubt clings to it". He then tested every source of knowledge — first the senses (the eye sees a star as small, though it is larger than the earth), then reason itself — and fell into doubt for "almost two months". He says the doubt ended not by an argument but by "a light which God most high cast into my breast", which restored his trust in the basic truths of reason (section II). He then examined the theologians, the philosophers, the Ismaʿilis and the Sufis in turn. His faith after this was not inherited; it was tested, and partly given back to him.

**The faith of the imitator (*īmān al-muqallid*).** Classical theologians asked whether belief held only by *taqlīd* (following others, without one's own reasoning) is valid. Kamil Güneş (2007) surveys the positions:
- **Valid, but sinful for neglecting reasoning** — the view of most Maturidi and Ashʿari theologians, and of Ibn Taymiyya (pp. 10–11).
- **Valid** — Abu Hanifa, Sufyan al-Thawri, Malik, al-Awzaʿi, al-Shafiʿi, Ahmad ibn Hanbal, al-Muhasibi, al-Nasafi, al-Baghdadi, al-Ghazali and al-Taftazani; al-Taftazani said nearly all jurists held it (p. 11).
- **Invalid** — the Muʿtazila (Qadi ʿAbd al-Jabbar), and among Ashʿaris al-Juwayni and al-Isfarayini (pp. 12–14).
- **Al-Ashʿari's own view is disputed.** His surviving works contain no clear statement that the imitator's faith is invalid; later authors attributed it to him, and others (al-Baghdadi, al-Taftazani) read it as referring to *complete* faith (pp. 14, 24–25).

So the mainstream answer is: the believer who has not reasoned is still a believer, but is urged to think. That is the opposite of "blind faith" being the ideal.

#### reasoning — How philosophers argue about it
**The weak form: the genetic fallacy.** "You believe X because of your upbringing, so X is false" confuses where a belief came from with whether it is true. Philosophers describe this as conflating "the context of discovery with the context of justification" (Jong and Visala, as reported by Van Eyghen).

**The strong form: religious luck.** The philosopher Guy Axtell argues that because believers would probably hold opposite beliefs if raised elsewhere, exclusivist religious belief suffers from "a malignant strand of epistemic luck" (Jackson's review, *NDPR*). This is the objection at full strength.

**Why debunking arguments "prove too much".** A debunking argument tries to show that a class of beliefs was formed by a process that does not track truth (Van Eyghen). The problem with using upbringing this way: almost all our deep beliefs are shaped by upbringing. Jackson's review lists the replies: some beliefs — such as that discrimination is wrong — we rightly keep despite their cultural origin; atheism and theism are equally affected by upbringing; and the same problem hits moral, political and philosophical beliefs. *Example:* someone raised in a family that believes in human equality would, if raised in a different time and place, perhaps not believe it. That does not make belief in human equality unjustified. If the argument worked against religion, it would also work against the belief in equality — and against the atheist's own atheism.

**What actually answers the strong form.** Not the observation that everyone is affected, but checking one's reasons against ones that do *not* depend on upbringing: arguments that a person from any culture can examine (the existence and order of the world; the character and message of a prophet; the transmission of a scripture), and listening to the best case for other views. This is what al-Ghazali did. *Limit:* no one can fully step outside their upbringing; examination can reduce the role of luck, not remove it.

#### science — What research says
**Belief is passed on in families.** [Established, for the US sample.] In a Pew survey of 1,811 pairs of US teens and their parents (2019), "Most teens share the religion of their parents", and 48% of teens said they have "all the same" religious beliefs as their parent.

**Children are naturally inclined to some religious ideas.** [Moderate.] Justin Barrett, a psychologist then at Oxford, argues in *Born Believers* (2012) that "belief in gods appears to be a naturally occurring human phenomenon": children readily detect agents, see purpose in nature (a "promiscuous teleology"), and tend to posit a creator. He distinguishes a cross-cultural "natural religion" from detailed theology, which "takes more time and effort to acquire" (as reported in BHodges's review). This does not favour any particular religion; the reviewer notes that Barrett "doesn't provide a compass" for judging between them. The book has not yet been consulted directly.

**Thinking analytically does not reliably reduce belief.** [Moderate.] A 2012 study in *Science* (Gervais and Norenzayan) reported that prompting analytic thinking reduced religious belief. A large, pre-registered, multi-site replication with 941 participants found "little to no effect on religious belief": d = 0.07, in the opposite direction, 95% CI [−0.12, 0.25] (Sanchez et al., *PLOS ONE*, 2017).

**What this means.** Upbringing strongly shapes *which* religion people hold; a general openness to belief in God appears early in children; and the claim that thinking carefully makes people stop believing did not hold up in this replication. None of this shows that any religion is true or false.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"I can't tell from the inside whether my reasons are real."** *Reply:* you can test them from the outside — against the best arguments of other views, and against reasons that do not depend on where you were born. *Limit:* most people never do this, and for them the objection stands.

**"*Fiṭra* is just a religious name for a cognitive bias."** *Reply:* the research shows a natural tendency; whether it tracks something real or is a misfire is what is disputed. Van Eyghen notes the reply that belief-forming faculties can be "sensitive to something": if God exists, a faculty that leads people toward Him would be working properly. *Limit:* this reply works only if there are independent reasons to think God exists.

**"Doesn't the Quran blame people of other faiths for following their parents?"** *Reply:* the verses blame refusing "better guidance" when it is brought (43:24), and the Quran says no one is punished until a messenger has come (17:15). Mainstream scholars apply this to individuals the message did not truly reach (see "Are good people of other faiths saved?"). *Limit:* who counts as having truly received the message is something only God knows.

#### views — The range of views
- **Mainstream Sunni theology** — faith by *taqlīd* is valid, though reasoning is urged (most Ashʿaris and Maturidis; the four imams; Güneş).
- **Muʿtazila, al-Juwayni, al-Isfarayini** — faith without reasoning is not valid (Güneş).
- **Al-Ghazali** — inherited belief must be tested to become certainty (*al-Munqidh*).
- **Axtell** — exclusivist religious belief is undermined by epistemic luck.
- **Critics of Axtell** — the same argument undermines too much (Jackson's review).

### Questions readers ask next
**What does Bukhari 1385 actually say?** "Every child is born with a true faith of Islam… and his parents convert him to Judaism or Christianity or Magianism." It agrees that parents shape religion, and adds that a natural disposition lies underneath.

**How did al-Ghazali get out of his doubt?** After about two months, by "a light which God most high cast into my breast" that restored his trust in reason; he then tested each school of thought in turn (*al-Munqidh*).

**What are debunking arguments, and why do they "prove too much"?** Arguments that a belief came from an unreliable process. Used against religion because of upbringing, they would also undermine moral and political beliefs — and atheism.

**Is a faith I never examined worth anything?** Most classical scholars said it is valid faith; most also said you should examine it.

**How do I examine my faith?** Read the strongest case for and against it; ask which of your reasons would convince someone raised elsewhere; separate religious teaching from family custom; and talk with someone who knows the arguments well.

**What is Barrett's 2012 work about?** *Born Believers*, a book arguing that young children are naturally inclined to believe in agents, purpose and a creator, though not in any particular religion.

### Honest limits
Upbringing does shape belief, and examining one's faith cannot remove that influence entirely. *Fiṭra* fits the research on children, but the research does not prove it. Barrett's book and the Pew paper's full methods were read only through summaries. Ibn Taymiyya's *Darʾ* has not yet been consulted.
