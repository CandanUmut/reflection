## Q18 — "If religious people act like this, why believe?"

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via the site's verified Tanzil data (`src/data/quran.json`) — 2:44, 61:2–3, 4:59; via legacy.quran.com — 63:1–4.
- Sahih al-Bukhari 33 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:33 — the signs of a hypocrite. (Muslim 55, "religion is sincere counsel", could not be opened; see Gaps.)
- Al-Ghazali, *Iḥyāʾ ʿUlūm al-Dīn*, Book of Knowledge (*Kitāb al-ʿIlm*), Section VI, tr. Nabih Amin Faris, https://www.ghazali.org/works/bk1-sec-6.htm.
- Zeki Saritoprak, "Islam and Politics in the Light of Said Nursi", *Islam and Christian–Muslim Relations* 19(1):113–126 (January 2008), abstract, https://risaleinur.com/studies/79-research-papers/4303-islam-and-politics-in-the-light-of-said-nursi.html.

- Lake Institute on Faith & Giving (Indiana University Lilly Family School of Philanthropy), *Giving USA Special Report on Giving to Religion* (24 October 2017), news release, https://philanthropy.indianapolis.iu.edu/news-events/news/_news/2017/religiously-affiliated-people-more-likely-to-donate-whether-to-place-of-worship-or-other-charitable-organizations.html.

### The question, at its strongest
"If a faith truly changes people, its public face should look better than the world around it. Instead I see preachers who lie, communities that cover up abuse, scholars who serve whoever is in power, and religion used to win votes and justify coups. And the usual answer — 'those people aren't real Muslims' — can explain away anything: if good believers prove the faith and bad believers also prove it (because the Quran 'predicted' hypocrites), then nothing could ever count against it. Isn't religion, in practice, just a tool of power?"

### Why people ask it
Because many people's doubt starts not with an argument but with a betrayal: by a teacher, an imam, a family member, or a movement. In Chouhoud's survey of Muslim Americans, the top four triggers of doubt were all moral and social concerns, led by "The way that [some] insist there is only one 'right' way to practice faith" (Yaqeen, 2018). In Türkiye, young people have watched religion used by rival political actors, abuse cases in religious dormitories, and a coup attempt that the state attributes to a religious network (the current page).

### Short answer
Your anger is legitimate, and the Quran shares it: "Do you order righteousness of the people and forget yourselves while you recite the Scripture? Then will you not reason?" (2:44); "Great is hatred in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do" (61:3). The Prophet (pbuh) gave the signs of a hypocrite: lying, breaking promises, betraying trust ({hadith:bukhari:33}). Al-Ghazali wrote pages against scholars who use religion for "worldly luxury" and "power and status". So the tradition does not ask you to excuse religious people; it gives you the standard to judge them by. But a message is not disproved by those who betray it, any more than medicine is disproved by a doctor who smokes. The serious version of the objection is about institutions and power, and that needs a real answer: religion fused with state power tends to corrupt both, and many Muslim thinkers (Nursi among them) have said so. In practice: judge the message by its source and its best people, choose your teachers carefully, and do not let abusers own your faith. If you were abused, it was a crime as well as a sin, and you should report it.

### The full answer

#### heart — If you were hurt by religious people
**What they did was wrong, and it was not God who did it.** Many people who leave a community feel they are leaving God. They are not the same. Pargament and Exline describe "interpersonal" religious struggles as "Conflicts with other people and institutions about sacred issues, anger at organized religion" — a recognised kind of suffering, not a sign of weak faith (APA, 2020).

**If it was abuse.** Over a hundred female Islamic scholars, in a 2024 statement, define spiritual abuse as "the misuse of religious knowledge, authority, or position to control, manipulate" or harm, and say: "The fault always lies with the perpetrators and never with the victim." They write that "It is an Islamic obligation for anyone who witnesses a criminal act or knows of a criminal act" to report it to the legal authorities, and warn that "concealing harm is going to encourage impunity and further perpetuate that harm" (Female Scholars Network). They add that "no scholar or pious person should be held equal to Allah."

> **What to do.** If you are in danger, call your local emergency number (112 in Türkiye and across Europe). Report crimes to the police, not only to community leaders. For confidential support in your country, Find A Helpline (findahelpline.com) lists free support lines. For trauma, a qualified therapist comes first; spiritual rebuilding can come after you are safe.

**Spiritual recovery.** You can rebuild a relationship with God that does not run through the person who hurt you. Start privately; choose new teachers slowly; trust your own judgement when something feels wrong.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Against saying without doing.** "Do you order righteousness of the people and forget yourselves while you recite the Scripture? Then will you not reason?" (2:44), Medinan, addressed in context to the Children of Israel — the scholars who taught others what they did not practise. "O you who have believed, why do you say what you do not do? Great is hatred in the sight of Allah that you say what you do not do" (61:2–3), Medinan, addressed to believers.

**The hypocrites (Sura 63).** A whole sura, *al-Munāfiqūn*, Medinan, describes people inside the community: "When the hypocrites come to you, [O Muhammad], they say, 'We testify that you are the Messenger of Allah'… and Allah testifies that the hypocrites are liars" (63:1). "They have taken their oaths as a cover, so they averted [people] from the way of Allah" (63:2). "And when you see them, their forms please you, and if they speak, you listen to their speech. [They are] as if they were pieces of wood propped up" (63:4). The Quran's own picture: impressive appearance and fine speech can hide emptiness.

#### sunnah — What the Prophet (pbuh) taught
**The signs of a hypocrite.** Abu Hurayra (ra) reports three: "Whenever he speaks, he tells a lie", whenever he promises he breaks it, and whenever he is trusted he betrays ({hadith:bukhari:33}). These are tests anyone can apply to a teacher.

**Counsel, including to rulers.** The current page cites the hadith "Religion is sincere counsel", including to "the leaders of the Muslims" (Muslim 55); it could not be opened for this run (see Gaps).

#### scholars — What al-Ghazali and Nursi said
**Al-Ghazali against "the learned men of this world".** In the Book of Knowledge of his *Iḥyāʾ*, Section VI, al-Ghazali (d. 1111) distinguishes "teachers of falsehood" from "the learned men of the hereafter" (Faris's translation). The former seek knowledge for worldly luxury, "power and status"; they frequent rulers — he quotes Saʿid ibn al-Musayyab: "If you see a learned man frequent the houses of the rulers, beware of him because he is a thief." He cites a hadith that at "the end of time there will be ignorant worshippers and corrupt learned men", and another: "Whoever would increase in knowledge but would not increase in righteousness would increase Allah's wrath upon him." The scholars of the hereafter, by contrast, "should not seek this world through their knowledge", practise what they teach — "No man will be learned unless he puts knowledge into practice" — live simply, keep away from rulers' courts, and say "I do not know" when unsure. (The hadith he cites are given here as he gives them; their grades were not checked for this run.)

**Nursi: "I take refuge in God from Satan and politics."** Said Nursi coined this phrase, and Zeki Saritoprak's study examines why, comparing his reading of some Quranic verses with Sayyid Qutb's (Saritoprak, 2008). The abstract read for this run does not give the work in which the phrase appears or Nursi's full reasons (see Gaps). The phrase itself is a Muslim scholar's warning against using faith as a political tool.

#### reasoning — The objection, in its three forms
**1. The personal form: "religious people are bad".** *Reply:* this judges a message by its messengers (an *ad hominem*). Hypocrisy shows a gap between what people say and what they do; it does not show that what they say is false. The Quran itself condemns that gap more harshly than most critics (61:3). *Limit:* if a faith claims to transform people, some evidence of transformation is a fair thing to ask for (see "What research shows").

**2. The institutional form: "religion is a tool of power".** This is the strongest version. When religion is joined to state power, those in power have every reason to bend it to their needs, and religious officials have every reason to please them. Al-Ghazali saw exactly this danger in the scholars who "frequent the houses of the rulers". *Reply:* the tradition contains its own critique of this — al-Ghazali's Section VI, Nursi's refusal of politics — and the Quran's standard (justice, honesty, 4:135; 5:8) is above any ruler. *Limit:* a critique inside the tradition does not undo the harm done by institutions; communities have to change practice, not only quote their critics.

**3. The "unfalsifiable" worry.** If good believers confirm the faith and bad believers also confirm it, nothing could count against it. *Reply:* the claim is narrower. The Quran predicts that hypocrites will exist; it also claims that sincere faith and practice change people. The second claim *can* fail: if, over a whole community, sincere practice made no difference to honesty, generosity or care, that would count against it. *Limit:* measuring sincerity is hard, so in practice the test is never clean.

**"No true Scotsman."** It is too easy to say that every bad actor "was never a real Muslim". The honest position: some bad actors are sincere believers who sin; some are hypocrites; communities must own their failures in both cases.

#### science — What research shows
**Giving.** [Moderate; US data] In the Philanthropy Panel Study (more than 9,000 individuals and families), 62% of religiously affiliated households gave to charity of any kind, against 46% of households with no religious affiliation; affiliated households "give as much or more to other types of charities"; monthly attenders were "11 times more likely to give to religious congregations" (Lake Institute, 2017). The practice, not the label alone, carries most of the effect for religious giving; this page does not have the breakdown for secular giving.

**Religious struggles.** Pargament and Exline report that, in a national sample, "higher levels of religious/spiritual struggle were tied to many indicators of psychopathology", and note that "most of this research is correlational" (APA, 2020).

**Religion and the state.** Norris and Inglehart's *Sacred and Secular* (2004) explains religious decline mainly by "existential security": "the experience of people living in weak and vulnerable societies heightens the importance of religious values" (Ikenberry's review). The current page says they link faster disaffiliation among the young to the fusion of religion and state; that claim was not found in the review read (see Corrections).

**Is hypocrisy worse among Muslims?** No study comparing hypocrisy across religious communities was read for this run, so this page makes no claim either way.

#### history — The Turkish context
**What is Diyanet?** The Presidency of Religious Affairs is "a state institution established in 1924" in Türkiye, created "to administer the affairs related to faith and worship of the religion of Islam". It writes a weekly sermon read in about 85,000 mosques, trains and employs Türkiye's imams as civil servants, and follows the Hanafi school. Its staff "doubled to nearly 150,000" by 2015 and its budget "quadrupled to over $2 billion by 2015"; critics have accused it of "serving for the ruling AKP party" and of a "denialist" approach to Alevis (*Wikipedia*).

**The 2016 coup attempt.** On the night of 15 July 2016, "over 300 people were killed" and "more than 2,100 were injured". President Erdoğan said the plotters were "linked to the Gülen movement", which the government later designated "FETÖ". Fethullah Gülen said: "I categorically deny such accusations." Germany's intelligence chief said Germany was unconvinced, and a British parliamentary committee found "no hard evidence that Fethullah Gülen masterminded the failed coup", though some Gülenists were involved. In the aftermath, "150,000 government officials and workers" were suspended (*Wikipedia*). This page takes no side in Turkish party politics; it records that many young people saw a religious movement, the state's religious bureaucracy, and politics tangled together, and that this damaged trust.

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"If religion doesn't make people better, why believe?"** *Reply:* the evidence that practice (not the label) predicts some prosocial behaviour, such as giving, is real but modest; and Islam itself expects many who carry its name to fall short. *Limit:* modest effects will not satisfy someone who expected religion to make people saints.

**"Your own scholars served tyrants."** *Reply:* some did, and al-Ghazali condemned them in his *Iḥyāʾ*. *Limit:* the existence of honest critics does not cancel the record.

**"'Misuse confirms the Quran' is unfalsifiable."** *Reply:* only the narrow claim (that hypocrites exist) is confirmed; the broader claim (that sincere practice changes people) can fail. *Limit:* testing it cleanly is hard.

#### views — The range of views
- **The Quran and Sunna** — hypocrisy is condemned and expected; judge people by honesty, keeping promises and trust (Bukhari 33).
- **Al-Ghazali** — distinguish the scholars of this world from the scholars of the hereafter.
- **Nursi** — keep faith away from politics.
- **Secular critics** — religion functions as a tool of power.
- **Female Scholars Network** — abuse must be reported; cover-ups are a further wrong.

### Questions readers ask next
**How do I tell the message apart from the people, when the people are the ones teaching me?** Use the tests the tradition gives: does the teacher lie, break promises, betray trust (Bukhari 33)? Do they practise what they teach, live simply, avoid courting power, and say "I do not know" (al-Ghazali)? Check what they say against the Quran and against more than one teacher.

**Who are the "best examples" I should look at?** The Prophet (pbuh) first; then people you can see up close who are honest, humble, generous and fair — often not the most famous. Al-Ghazali's description of the "learned men of the hereafter" is a portrait to look for.

**If religious practice really changes people, where is the evidence?** Some, modest: in the US, religious households are more likely to give to charity (62% against 46%), and frequent attenders far more likely to give to congregations (Lake Institute, 2017). No study on honesty or hypocrisy across communities was read for this run.

**What is Diyanet, and which "religious network" is behind the 2016 coup attempt?** Diyanet is Türkiye's state religious authority, founded in 1924. The government blames the Gülen movement ("FETÖ") for the coup attempt; Gülen denied it, and some foreign bodies said they had not seen conclusive evidence (*Wikipedia*).

**Is hypocrisy a bigger problem in Muslim communities than in others?** No comparative study was read for this run; the Quran treats hypocrisy as a danger in every community of faith (2:44 was addressed to the People of the Book; 61:2–3 to believers).

**Where in the *Iḥyāʾ* does al-Ghazali attack the "scholars of this world"?** In the Book of Knowledge (*Kitāb al-ʿIlm*), Section VI, on the evils of knowledge and the signs of the learned men of the hereafter (tr. Faris).

### Honest limits
The research on whether religious practice changes behaviour is mostly American and mostly about giving; nothing was read on honesty or hypocrisy across communities. Muslim 55, the Norris and Inglehart book itself (beyond a review), the KONDA data and the Diyanet magazine cited on the current page, and the work in which Nursi's phrase appears, were not read for this run.

## Q19 — Why one "right" Islam? Why so many sects?

### Sources read
- Saheeh International, via `src/data/quran.json` — 5:48, 3:103, 49:13; via Quranpedia — 3:105, https://quranpedia.net/tafsir/aal-imran/105/en.
- Ibn Kathir, *Tafsir* (abridged English), and *Tafsīr al-Jalālayn* (English), on 3:104–105, https://surahquran.com/tafsir-english-aya-105-sora-3.html.

- Sahih al-Bukhari 7352 (sunnah.com), https://sunnah.com/bukhari:7352.
- The Amman Message, official site: statement of the International Islamic Conference, Amman, 4–6 July 2005, https://ammanmessage.com/?p=15; overview, https://ammanmessage.com/; King Abdullah II's speech to the OIC summit, Mecca, 8 December 2005, https://ammanmessage.com/?p=33.

- "Shia–Sunni relations", "Alevism", "Ibadi Islam", "Ahmadiyya", *Wikipedia*.
- Porter & Schumann, "Intellectual humility and openness to the opposing view", *Self and Identity* 17:139–162 (2018), as summarised by Heterodox Academy, https://heterodoxacademy.org/blog/research-summary-intellectual-humility-and-openness-to-the-opposing-view/.
- Youssef Chouhoud, Yaqeen Institute (2018), as read for run 9.

### The question, at its strongest
"If God's message is clear, why have Muslims split into Sunnis, Shiʿis, Ibadis, Alevis and more — and why does each group claim to be the one 'saved sect' of the famous hadith? Either the message is unclear, or the claim to certainty is arrogance. And the disagreements are not small: Sunnis and Shiʿis fought for centuries over who should lead after the Prophet (pbuh), and some groups say there was a prophet after him. How can you say there is a 'shared core'?"

### Why people ask it
Because "only one right way" rigidity is the leading trigger of doubt in Chouhoud's survey: "The way that [some] insist there is only one 'right' way to practice faith" (Yaqeen, 2018). Young Turks meet rival religious communities (*cemaat*s), online preachers who condemn other Muslims, Alevi neighbours and secular relatives. The "73 sects" hadith is often used as a weapon.

### Short answer
Disagreement among Muslims is real, and some of it is deep. But most of it is about interpretation within a shared core, and the tradition built tools for living with it. The Quran treats diversity of laws as part of God's plan — "To each of you We prescribed a law and a method" (5:48) — while condemning division into hostile camps: "do not become divided" (3:103). The Prophet (pbuh) said that a judge who makes a careful judgement and is right gets a double reward, and if wrong, a single reward ({hadith:bukhari:7352}). In 2005, the Amman Message — from a conference of 200 scholars from 50 countries, later endorsed by over 500 — recognised eight schools of law (four Sunni, two Shiʿi, the Ibadi and the Zahiri), the Ashʿari creed, "real Tasawwuf (Sufism)" and "true Salafi thought", and forbade declaring any of them apostates. The core-level disputes are real, and this page states them: the Sunni–Shiʿi question of who should lead, which became a question of doctrine; and the Ahmadi belief in a prophet after Muhammad (pbuh), which mainstream Muslims reject. The 73-sects hadith is graded sound, but it says the saved group is "the *jamāʿa*" — the main body — not a small sect.

### The full answer

#### heart — If you have been told you are in the wrong group
**Being called a disbeliever by another Muslim is a serious wrong, and the scholars of the Amman Message forbade it.** They wrote that it is "not possible nor permissible to declare as apostates any group of Muslims" who believe in God and His Messenger, accept the pillars of faith and of Islam, and do not deny anything necessarily known of the religion. If you were shunned, mocked or threatened because of your school, your family's tradition, or your questions, the wrong was done to you.

**What helps.** Find teachers who can say "this is my view, and others hold another" — the "I do not know" that al-Ghazali counted as a mark of a true scholar (see Q18). Keep friendships across lines. And remember that on the Day of Judgement, the Quran says, God will settle what people differed about (5:48) — not you, and not the person who excluded you.

#### quran — What the Quran says
**Diversity as part of the plan (5:48).** "To each of you We prescribed a law and a method. Had Allah willed, He would have made you one nation [united in religion], but [He intended] to test you in what He has given you; so race to [all that is] good. To Allah is your return all together, and He will [then] inform you concerning that over which you used to differ" (5:48), Medinan, addressed to the Prophet (pbuh) about the earlier communities (Jews and Christians). It is about different communities with different laws; applying it to schools within Islam is an extension.

**Unity commanded (3:103).** "And hold firmly to the rope of Allah all together and do not become divided. And remember the favor of Allah upon you - when you were enemies and He brought your hearts together and you became, by His favor, brothers" (3:103), Medinan, addressed to the believers of Medina, whose tribes had been at war.

**A warning (3:105).** "And do not be like the ones who became divided and differed after the clear proofs had come to them. And those will have a great punishment" (3:105). Al-Jalālayn: "these are the Jews and the Christians". The *Mukhtasar* commentary: "Do not be, O believers, like the People of the Scripture who became divided into different groups and divisions".

**What these verses say together.** Different paths of law can be part of God's test (5:48); hostile division after clear guidance is condemned (3:103, 105). The line runs between difference and division.

#### sunnah — The 73-sects hadith, and the reward for honest error
**The hadith.** Abu Hurayra (ra) reports: "my community will be split up into seventy-three sects" ({hadith:abudawud:4596}; al-Albani: *ḥasan ṣaḥīḥ*). In Muʿawiya's (ra) version, "seventy two of them will go to Hell and one of them will go to Paradise, and it is the majority group" — the Arabic word is *al-jamāʿa* ({hadith:abudawud:4597}; al-Albani: *ḥasan*). Ibn Kathir cites Muʿawiya's version from Imam Ahmad: "This Ummah will divide into seventy-three sects, all in the Fire except one, that is, the Jama'ah."

**Which group is the *jamāʿa*, and does it mean you?** The hadith's own word is *al-jamāʿa*, "the community", which the sunnah.com translator renders "the majority group". It is not the name of a small sect, and nothing in the hadith says any particular modern group is the saved one. The current page reports that Ibn Taymiyya held the 72 are not thereby unbelievers (Abdallah, *AFKAR*, 2006), and that al-Baghdadi's *al-Farq bayna al-firaq* forced known sects into the number (Gömbeyaz, 2018); neither was read for this run (see Gaps).

**Honest error is rewarded.** ʿAmr ibn al-ʿAs (ra) reports the Prophet (pbuh): a judge who rules carefully and is correct "will receive a double reward", and if wrong, a single reward ({hadith:bukhari:7352}). Careful reasoning that reaches different answers is not sin.

#### scholars — The Amman Message
**What it is.** In July 2005, King Abdullah II of Jordan "convened an international Islamic conference of 200 of the world's leading Islamic scholars" from 50 countries in Amman; "over 500 leading Muslim scholars worldwide" later endorsed its Three Points (ammanmessage.com).

**The eight schools.** The statement lists: "the four Sunni schools (Mathahib) of Islamic jurisprudence (Hanafi, Maliki, Shafi'i and Hanbali)", "the two Shi'i schools of Islamic jurisprudence (Jafari and Zaydi)", and "the Ibadi school of Islamic jurisprudence and the Thahiri school of Islamic jurisprudence".

**Theology, Sufism and Salafism.** It is "not possible nor permissible to declare whosoever subscribes to the Ash'ari creed or whoever practices real Tasawwuf (Sufism) an apostate", and "Likewise… whosoever subscribes to true Salafi thought".

**The other two points.** It forbids *takfīr* (declaring Muslims apostates), and it holds that "no one may issue a fatwa without the requisite personal qualifications". The King's speech to the OIC summit in Mecca (December 2005) adds: "there exists more in common between the various schools of Islamic jurisprudence than there is difference between them."

**Its limits.** Rabbani stresses that the Message "should not be promoted as making Islam a 'free-for-all'" and is "not an affirmation that 'everyone is on the complete truth'"; it recognises groups as Muslim, not every view as correct. It did not list the Ahmadis. Whether it has changed behaviour on the ground — in sectarian conflicts in Iraq, Syria or Pakistan — is not something the sources read could measure.

**Earlier recognition.** In 1959, Mahmud Shaltut, rector of al-Azhar, issued a fatwa "recognizing Shia Islamic law as the fifth school of Islamic law" (*Wikipedia*, "Shia–Sunni relations").

#### history — What the main groups believe and differ on
**Sunnis and Shiʿis.** "The issue of succession following the death of Muhammad divided Muslims into two branches." Shiʿis hold that the Prophet (pbuh) designated ʿAli (citing Ghadir Khumm) and that leadership belongs to his family; Sunnis hold that he named no successor and that Abu Bakr was chosen at the Saqifa. The killing of Husayn at Karbala "finalized the schism" (*Wikipedia*). Other differences: Shiʿis count sayings of the Prophet's family (*Ahl al-Bayt*) as hadith and reject many Sunni-only hadith; Twelver Shiʿis permit temporary marriage (*mutʿa*), which Sunnis, Ismailis and Zaydis do not; and there are differences in prayer (Shiʿis prostrate on a clay tablet; Shiʿis and Malikis pray with hands at their sides) (*Wikipedia*).

**Is imamate a core belief?** For Twelver Shiʿis, yes: the leadership of the Imams is a doctrine, not only a historical question. That is a real difference at the level of belief, not only of law, and this page does not hide it. What the Amman Message affirms is that both groups share the pillars of faith and of Islam and so may not declare each other apostates; it does not claim they agree on imamate.

**Alevis.** Alevis are mainly in Türkiye, where estimates range from about 4% to 15% of the population. They revere ʿAli and the Twelve Imams; their central ritual is the *cem*, a gathering with music and both men and women, led by a *dede*; they fast in the first twelve days of Muharram. Most belong to the Bektashi tradition. They are usually classed with Shiʿism, but their practice differs considerably from Twelver practice; some groups follow Jaʿfari law (*Wikipedia*). The Turkish state recognised Alevism as a "cultural group" in 2022, and President Erdoğan has said a cemevi "is not a place of worship" (*Wikipedia*). Alevis are not named separately among the eight Amman schools; those Alevis who follow Jaʿfari law fall under it, and the Message's general rule against *takfīr* covers all who accept the pillars of faith and Islam.

**Ibadis.** Ibadis call themselves "The People of Truth and Integrity" and "may object to being classified as Kharijites"; their community grew from the teachings of Jabir ibn Zayd in Basra. They are a majority in Oman and live also in the M'zab (Algeria), Djerba (Tunisia), Libya and Zanzibar. They hold that the imam should be elected, "the most pious man of the community", and need not be from Quraysh; that believers "will not see God on the Day of Resurrection"; and some hold the Quran created (*Wikipedia*). They are among the Amman schools.

**Ahmadis.** The Ahmadiyya were "founded by Mirza Ghulam Ahmad (1835–1908)" in British India; Ahmadis regard him as "divinely appointed as both the promised Messiah and Mahdi" and as a "non-law-bearing prophet". Mainstream Muslims regard this as heresy because they believe Muhammad (pbuh) was the "final prophet" (*khātam al-nabiyyīn*); Pakistan "officially declared" them "non-Muslims" by constitutional amendment (*Wikipedia*). This is why the Amman Message did not include them. Whatever one's view, it is a core-level dispute, not a difference of law.

#### reasoning — Is there a shared core?
**What is shared.** Belief in one God; Muhammad (pbuh) as His Messenger; the Quran; prayer, fasting, almsgiving and pilgrimage; the Last Day. The Amman Message's rule is built on exactly this: whoever "believes in God and His Messenger, acknowledges the pillars of faith and the five pillars of Islam, and does not deny any necessarily self-evident tenet of religion" is a Muslim.

**Where the core is disputed.** Two places: whether there can be a prophet after Muhammad (pbuh) (the Ahmadi question), and how leadership after him is to be understood (imamate). The first, on the mainstream view, is outside the core; the second is a real difference that the Message chose not to treat as grounds for *takfīr*.

**The two slogans.** The current page says "Both slogans overstate their case". The two are: "Disagreement in my community is a mercy" — a saying the current page reports has no known chain (al-Subki, al-Suyuti, al-Albani; not checked for this run) — and "Only one sect is saved, so all others are lost". The first overstates by making every difference good; the second by turning the 73-sects hadith into a licence to condemn other Muslims, when the hadith's own word for the saved is *al-jamāʿa*, the community.

*Limit:* a shared core does not make the remaining differences trivial. Centuries of violence show that they can be lethal when mixed with politics.

#### science — What research shows
**Intellectual humility.** [Moderate; US samples] Across four studies (181 students; 188 and 169 adults online; 104 students in an experiment), Porter and Schumann (2018) found that people higher in intellectual humility "were more open to learning about the opposition's views", gave more respectful reasons for disagreement, and in one study read more of the opposing arguments; an article teaching a growth mindset raised intellectual humility (Heterodox Academy summary; effect sizes were not given in the summary read).

**Black-and-white religious thinking.** The current page says it correlates with anxiety and intolerance; no study on this was read for this run (see Corrections).

#### objection — The strongest objections, and the replies
**"If the message were clear, there would be no sects."** *Reply:* the core is clear and shared by almost all Muslims; most disagreement is about interpretation, which the tradition expected and rewarded when done honestly (Bukhari 7352). *Limit:* the imamate and finality-of-prophethood disputes are not just interpretation.

**"Each group claims to be the saved sect, so all are arrogant."** *Reply:* the hadith names "the *jamāʿa*", the community, not a sect; the Amman Message forbids declaring other Muslims apostates. *Limit:* many groups still read the hadith exclusively.

**"Sunnis and Shiʿis killed each other for centuries."** *Reply:* yes, often when sectarian identity was fused with dynastic or state power (from Karbala to modern Iraq and Syria, per *Wikipedia*). Scholars on both sides have also worked for recognition (Shaltut, 1959; the Amman Message). *Limit:* recognition on paper has not ended conflict.

#### views — The range of views
- **Amman Message signatories** — eight schools, Ashʿari creed, Sufism and "true Salafi thought" are all within Islam; *takfīr* forbidden.
- **Some Salafi and some Shiʿi voices** — exclusivist readings of the 73-sects hadith (no named source read for this run).
- **Twelver Shiʿis** — imamate is a doctrine of faith.
- **Ahmadis** — accept Mirza Ghulam Ahmad as a non-law-bearing prophet; regarded by mainstream Muslims as outside Islam.
- **Alevis** — a Türkiye-centred tradition revering ʿAli and the Imams, with its own rituals.

### Questions readers ask next
**If the core is so clear, why did Sunnis and Shiʿis fight over it for centuries? Is imamate not a core belief?** For Twelver Shiʿis it is a core belief; that is a real difference. The fighting was often bound up with political power. The Amman Message's answer is that, sharing the pillars, neither may declare the other apostate — not that they agree.

**Which group is the "*jamāʿa*" in the 73-sects hadith, and does it mean me?** The hadith's word is *al-jamāʿa*, the community or "the majority group" ({hadith:abudawud:4597}). It does not name any modern sect.

**What are the "two slogans" that "both overstate their case"?** "Disagreement is a mercy" (no known chain, as the current page reports) and "only one sect is saved, so the rest are lost". See "Is there a shared core?".

**What do Sunnis and Shiʿis actually disagree about, beyond "leadership after 632"?** Imamate as doctrine; which hadith are accepted; temporary marriage; details of prayer (*Wikipedia*).

**Who are the Alevis, and are they counted among the eight schools?** A Türkiye-centred tradition revering ʿAli and the Twelve Imams, with the *cem* ritual. Not named separately; those following Jaʿfari law fall under it, and the rule against *takfīr* covers all who accept the pillars.

**What are the Ibadis, and why were Ahmadis excluded?** Ibadis: a school centred in Oman, with its own views on the imamate; included in Amman. Ahmadis: excluded because mainstream Muslims hold that no prophet comes after Muhammad (pbuh).

### Honest limits
The real disputes — imamate, and prophethood after Muhammad (pbuh) — are not resolved by pointing to a shared core. The groups here were described from *Wikipedia*, not from their own texts. Ibn Taymiyya, al-Baghdadi, the hadith "disagreement is a mercy" and its critics, Muslim 55, and studies on black-and-white religious thinking were not read for this run. Whether the Amman Message changed behaviour was not measured.
